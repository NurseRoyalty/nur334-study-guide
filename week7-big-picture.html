The Big Picture: Two Stories This Week

Week 7 looks like two unrelated units, but both come down to the same nursing skill: know what the treatment or the disease takes away from the patient, then watch for the consequences.

Story 1: Cancer

  • Chemo and radiation kill rapidly dividing cells — cancer, but also bone marrow, GI lining, mouth, hair, skin
  • So the side effects are predictable: low counts, nausea, stomatitis, hair loss, skin breakdown
  • Nursing job: protect the patient (infection, bleeding, extravasation) and protect yourself (chemo handling, radiation)

Story 2: Hepatobiliary

  • Liver, gallbladder, and pancreas share one drainage system (the biliary tree) emptying into the duodenum
  • A problem in one spills into the others: gallstones → pancreatitis; obstruction → jaundice
  • Nursing job: rest the organ (NPO), manage pain, and watch for bleeding, fluid shifts, and mental status changes

Connecting the Dots

  • Gallstones → blocked common bile duct → obstructive jaundice, and gallstones are a top cause of acute pancreatitis
  • ERCP can remove stones — but its most common complication is pancreatitis
  • Hepatitis B and C → chronic liver damage; hepatitis B is linked to liver cancer and hepatitis C to liver transplant
  • Cirrhosis → portal hypertension → varices + ascites; cirrhosis → low prothrombin → the varices bleed harder
  • GI bleeding → more ammonia → hepatic encephalopathy
  • Chemo → nadir → neutropenia → infection: the most dangerous window is about a week after treatment
  • Acetaminophen keeps showing up: avoid it in hepatitis and cirrhosis because it is hepatotoxic

NCLEX-NGN Clinical Judgment Scenarios

Scenario 1: After chemo

  • Cues: Client 8 days after chemo; temperature 38.3°C; ANC 380; family arrives with fresh flowers
  • Analysis: At the nadir with severe neutropenia — high risk for life-threatening infection
  • Actions: Protective precautions, screen visitors, remove flowers, neutropenic diet, report the fever promptly

Scenario 2: Confusion in cirrhosis

  • Cues: Client with cirrhosis is newly confused, hands flap when wrists are extended, has had no bowel movement in 3 days
  • Analysis: Rising ammonia → hepatic encephalopathy; constipation is making it worse
  • Actions: Lactulose titrated to 2–4 stools/day, monitor level of consciousness, safety precautions, check for GI bleeding

Scenario 3: Acute pancreatitis

  • Cues: Severe epigastric pain, vomiting without relief, HR 126, BP 90/56, amylase and lipase elevated, calcium low
  • Analysis: Acute pancreatitis with hypovolemia and hypocalcemia
  • Actions: IV opioids, knees-bent position, NPO with NG to low wall suction, aggressive IV fluids, frequent vital signs, watch glucose

Scenario 4: After liver biopsy

  • Cues: Two hours after a liver biopsy, HR rises from 82 to 110, breath sounds are diminished on the right
  • Analysis: Possible internal bleeding and/or pneumothorax
  • Actions: Keep on the right side and flat, assess breath sounds, monitor vital signs closely, notify the provider
Priority cues across Week 7: lost blood return during chemo · fever with a low ANC · vomiting blood · new confusion or asterixis · rising heart rate after a liver biopsy · Cullen's or Turner's sign · bile-colored drainage after gallbladder surgery.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

Why do chemo and radiation cause low blood counts, mouth sores, and hair loss?
They kill rapidly dividing cells — cancer cells, but also bone marrow, the GI lining, the mouth, and hair.
What four things does the liver do that explain most cirrhosis complications?
Makes albumin (ascites when low), makes prothrombin (bleeding when low), converts ammonia to urea (encephalopathy), and conjugates bilirubin (jaundice).
How can gallstones cause both jaundice and pancreatitis?
The gallbladder, liver, and pancreas share one drainage system. A stone in the common bile duct blocks bile (obstructive jaundice) and is a leading cause of acute pancreatitis.
Which three Week 7 situations call for checking a lab before acting?
Blood counts before chemo, PT/INR before a liver biopsy, and BUN/creatinine before CT contrast.
Which conditions this week are treated by resting the organ with NPO status?
An acute pancreatitis flare (plus NG to low wall suction) and a cholecystitis flare.
Which Week 7 patients are at risk for bleeding, and why?
Cirrhosis (low prothrombin, varices), chemotherapy (low platelets), and after a liver biopsy (very vascular organ).
What is the earliest sign of internal bleeding after a liver biopsy?
A rising heart rate — blood pressure falls later.
How does a GI bleed make hepatic encephalopathy worse?
Blood in the gut is nitrogen-rich, so it raises serum ammonia.
Which three Week 7 conditions all require avoiding alcohol?
Pancreatitis, hepatitis, and cirrhosis.
Why are patients with pancreatitis hypovolemic while patients with cirrhosis have ascites?
Pancreatitis: inflammation makes vessels leaky. Cirrhosis: low albumin and portal hypertension push fluid into the peritoneum. Both are fluid in the wrong place.
When is a client most at risk for infection after chemotherapy?
At the nadir — when counts are lowest, often around days 7–9.
Which drug should be avoided in both hepatitis and cirrhosis because it harms the liver?
Acetaminophen (also alcohol; isoniazid is hepatotoxic too).
