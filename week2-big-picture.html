Week 2: Big Picture Overview

Hematology - Blood Cells, Labs, Disorders, and Clinical Judgment

🔍 The Big Picture: Why Hematology Matters

Week 2 is about blood — what it's made of, how much of it, whether it's working, and what happens when it isn't.

Everything in hematology connects to oxygen delivery and clotting. Every patient you care for needs adequate oxygen to their tissues and needs their blood to clot (but not too much). That's why we care about red blood cells (carry oxygen), white blood cells (fight infection), and platelets (make clots).

The Core Principle: Blood is a transport system. RBCs transport oxygen. WBCs transport immunity. Platelets transport clotting ability. When any part fails, tissues suffer.

Three Main Problems Week 2 Covers:

  • Too few red blood cells (ANEMIA): Not enough oxygen delivery → fatigue, shortness of breath, organ damage
  • Too many red blood cells (POLYCYTHEMIA): Too thick blood → clots, strokes, tissue damage from lack of flow
  • Bad blood products given (TRANSFUSION REACTIONS): Immune system attacks foreign blood → fever, hemolysis, shock, death

📊 Foundation: Reading the Labs (Complete Blood Count)

The CBC is your map of what's in the blood:

Component What It Means Clinically Why We Care
Hemoglobin (Hgb)
12-17 g/dL		 Protein in RBCs that carries oxygen Low = anemia = poor oxygen delivery. High = polycythemia = thick blood/clots
Hematocrit (Hct)
36-50%		 % of blood that is RBCs Direct mirror of hemoglobin. If Hb is low, Hct is low. Hct roughly 3x Hb value
RBC Count
4.5-6 million		 Total number of red blood cells Low = fewer oxygen carriers. High = more viscosity/clotting risk
MCV (Mean Cell Volume)
80-100 fL		 SIZE of RBCs (Microcytic, Normocytic, Macrocytic) Tells you what KIND of anemia. Size tells the cause
MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Conc.)
32-36 g/dL		 How much hemoglobin per RBC (Hypochromic = pale, Normochromic = normal color) Tells you if RBC is full of hemoglobin or not
WBC (White Blood Cells)
4.5-11 K		 Immune cells fighting infection Low = immunocompromised/infection risk. High = infection or leukemia
Platelets
150-400 K		 Cells that make clots Low = bleeding risk. High = clotting risk
Key Memory Aid: "RBC Bus Analogy"
Think of RBCs as buses carrying oxygen passengers. MCV = bus size. MCHC = how full each bus is. Low Hb/Hct = not enough buses. Polycythemia = too many buses (traffic jam/clots).

🧩 Anemia: The Puzzle to Solve

Anemia is not a diagnosis — it's a symptom of something wrong. Your job is to figure out WHICH type and WHY.

The Anemia Algorithm:
1. Patient has anemia (low Hb/Hct)
2. Check MCV to categorize (size of cells)
3. Run targeted tests based on size
4. Identify the cause
5. Treat the cause (not just the anemia)

The Four Main Types:

1. IRON DEFICIENCY ANEMIA (Microcytic, Hypochromic)

  • What's wrong: Not enough iron to make hemoglobin
  • Cause: Chronic blood loss (GI bleed, menorrhagia) OR poor iron intake
  • Clinical clue: Pica (eating ice, dirt, starch) — body trying to find iron
  • Treatment: Find the bleeding source + iron supplements (black stools expected)
  • NCLEX tie: Teach patient black stools are NORMAL (iron in stool)

2. B12/PERNICIOUS ANEMIA (Macrocytic, Normochromic)

  • What's wrong: Can't absorb B12 (lacking intrinsic factor)
  • Cause: Autoimmune attack on stomach lining (pernicious = "deadly" if untreated)
  • Clinical clue: NEUROLOGIC SYMPTOMS = paresthesia, ataxia, confusion (B12 needed for nerve myelin)
  • CRITICAL: Neurologic damage is IRREVERSIBLE if untreated prolonged. This is why IM B12 is mandatory, not optional
  • Treatment: IM B12 for LIFE (oral won't work, need injection to bypass GI absorption)
  • NCLEX tie: Neurologic symptoms = EMERGENCY. Chronic condition needing lifetime management

3. FOLATE DEFICIENCY (Macrocytic, Normochromic)

  • What's wrong: Not enough folate for cell division/DNA synthesis
  • Cause: Poor diet (alcoholism), malabsorption, pregnancy, methotrexate
  • Clinical clue: NO neurologic symptoms (KEY difference from B12)
  • Treatment: Folic acid supplements + address cause
  • NCLEX tie: Folate vs B12 differentiation = classic exam question. No neuro = folate. Neuro = B12

4. APLASTIC ANEMIA (Normocytic, Normochromic - but PANCYTOPENIA)

  • What's wrong: Bone marrow stops making ALL cell lines (only anemia with all three down)
  • Cause: Chemotherapy, radiation, idiopathic (unknown), medications
  • Clinical clue: Low RBC + Low WBC + Low platelets = TRIPLE TROUBLE (infection AND bleeding risk)
  • Treatment: Supportive care, stem cell transplant, immunosuppressants
  • NCLEX tie: Life-threatening. Highest risk for both infection and bleeding simultaneously

⬆️ Polycythemia: Too Much of a Good Thing

If anemia = not enough RBCs, polycythemia = too many RBCs. Blood gets THICK, blood doesn't FLOW.

The Core Problem: VISCOSITY = CLOTTING RISK
More cells → thicker blood → slower flow → clots in brain (stroke), heart (MI), lungs (PE)
Type Cause Clinical Picture
Relative Dehydration (decreased plasma, RBCs concentrated) False high hematocrit; fix with fluids
Primary (Vera) Genetic mutation (JAK2); bone marrow overproducing Chronic condition; phlebotomy + monitoring for life
Secondary Compensation for chronic hypoxia (COPD, high altitude) Body making MORE RBCs to carry oxygen; treat underlying cause

Classic exam sign: Pruritus (intense itching) worsened by hot baths = POLYCYTHEMIA VERA

⚠️ Transfusion Reactions: When Blood Betrays

You're giving blood to help the patient. Their immune system doesn't recognize it as "self" and attacks it.

Most Critical on Exam:
Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR)
- Occurs within MINUTES to hours
- Fever + chills + BACK/FLANK PAIN (from hemolysis)
- Dark (cola-colored) urine = hemoglobinuria
- STOP transfusion immediately, verify compatibility, aggressive hydration to prevent kidney damage

Other Important Reactions:

  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic (FNHTR): Fever + chills only (NO back pain, NO dark urine). Most common. Can resume after antipyretics
  • Anaphylactic: Airway compromise, severe hypotension. IM epinephrine, washed RBCs for future transfusions
  • TRALI: Pulmonary edema within 6 hours. High-flow oxygen, NOT diuretics
  • Delayed Hemolytic (DHTR): Days later, mild fever + low hematocrit. Self-limited

🔗 How It All Connects: NCLEX-NGN Clinical Judgment

Scenario 1: 62-year-old with fatigue, SOB, pale conjunctiva

  • CBC shows Hb 7.8, MCV 68 (microcytic) → suspect iron deficiency
  • Ask about: GI bleeding, menstrual bleeding, dietary iron intake
  • Order: Iron studies (ferritin = most sensitive), fecal occult blood
  • Treat: Find bleeding source + iron supplements
  • Nursing: Educate on black stools, absorption with food

Scenario 2: 58-year-old on chemo with fever, WBC 0.8, platelets 15K

  • CBC shows pancytopenia → likely aplastic anemia from chemotherapy
  • Recognize: HIGH risk for BOTH infection AND bleeding simultaneously
  • Nursing priorities: Strict hand hygiene, protective precautions, fever monitoring, NO invasive procedures
  • Support: Transfusions as needed, monitor for signs of infection/bleeding

Scenario 3: 52-year-old post-transfusion with fever 101.5°F, headache, myalgia

  • Differential: AHTR vs FNHTR?
  • NO back pain, NO dark urine → likely FNHTR (most common)
  • Action: Stop transfusion, verify compatibility, give antipyretic, resume if FNHTR confirmed
  • If it WERE AHTR: Aggressive fluids, monitor kidney function, prepare for possible shock

📝 Key Takeaways

  • ✓ CBC is your roadmap: Hemoglobin + Hematocrit = how much blood? MCV = what SIZE are cells?
  • ✓ Anemia types: Match MCV to iron deficiency (micro), B12 (macro + neuro), folate (macro, no neuro), aplastic (pancytopenia)
  • ✓ B12 neurologic damage is IRREVERSIBLE: IM only, lifelong treatment, family education
  • ✓ Polycythemia = thick blood = clot risk: Phlebotomy, hydration, anticoagulation, pruritus with hot baths
  • ✓ AHTR = back pain + dark urine: STOP transfusion, verify, aggressive fluids, prevent kidney damage
  • ✓ Clinical judgment: Don't just report labs. Ask "WHY is this happening?" and treat the CAUSE
  • ✓ Patient education matters: Black stools (iron), no hot baths (polycythemia), lifelong B12 (pernicious)
