Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)

← Back to Study Guide

Overview

Nephrolithiasis = kidney stones. These are crystalline formations in the kidneys that can obstruct urine flow and cause excruciating pain—one of the strongest pain sensations known to medicine. Treatment depends on stone type, size, and location.

  • Stones can form anywhere in the urinary tract but originate in kidneys
  • Risk increases with dehydration, certain dietary factors, and metabolic disorders
  • Pain from kidney stones can be severe enough to cause hemodynamic instability

Types of Kidney Stones (C-CUP Mnemonic)

Calcium Oxalate~75% (MOST COMMON)Hypercalciuria, dehydration, high oxalate dietRadiopaque on X-ray

Pathophysiology

How Stones Form

  • Supersaturation of urine with stone-forming substances (calcium, oxalate, phosphate, uric acid)
  • Changes in urine pH promote crystallization
  • Dehydration concentrates urine, increasing risk
  • Urinary stasis allows crystal aggregation

Risk Factors for Formation

  • Inadequate hydration (most common preventable factor)
  • Family history of kidney stones
  • Dietary factors: high sodium, high protein, high oxalate
  • Metabolic disorders: hyperparathyroidism, gout, RTA (renal tubular acidosis)
  • Urinary tract abnormalities causing stasis
  • Recurrent UTIs (especially struvite stones)

Clinical Manifestations

Pain Characteristics

  • Renal Colic: Severe, visceral pain that waxes and wanes with spasms
  • Onset: Sudden, severe flank pain radiating from flank to groin
  • Severity: Often the strongest pain sensation a patient experiences
  • Hemodynamic Impact: Pain can be SO severe it causes changes in vital signs (hypotension, tachycardia)
Clinical Pearl: With kidney stone pain, treating pain may be your PRIORITY intervention on the NCLEX. Unlike post-op pain (which won't kill the patient), severe kidney stone pain can cause hemodynamic compromise. Pain management is critical.

Associated Symptoms

  • Hematuria (visible or microscopic)
  • Dysuria (if stone in lower ureter/urethra)
  • Urinary frequency and urgency
  • Nausea and vomiting (pain-related; doesn't improve even after vomiting)
  • Diaphoresis, restlessness
  • If infected: fever indicates complications

Diagnosis

Diagnostic Tests

  • Non-Contrast Spiral CT (CT without contrast): GOLD STANDARD
    • Detects all stone types (radiopaque and radiolucent)
    • Fastest, most accurate; also shows obstruction level
    • No contrast needed (important in renal patients)
  • Ultrasound: Good alternative; can show hydronephrosis (swollen kidney from obstruction)
  • IVP (Intravenous Pyelogram): Less commonly used now; shows obstruction via dye
  • KUB X-ray: Shows only radiopaque stones (calcium, not uric acid)

Urinalysis

  • Hematuria (RBCs in urine—usually present)
  • Crystals present (specific to stone type)
  • pH assessment (helps determine stone type)
  • Bacteria if secondary infection present

Stone Analysis

  • If stone is passed or retrieved, analyze its composition
  • Guides dietary/medical prevention for future stones

Management

Conservative Management (Expectant)

  • Small Stones (<5mm): Have high spontaneous passage rate (70-80%)
  • Approach:
    • Adequate hydration (dilutes urine, promotes passage)
    • Pain control (NSAIDs, opioids if severe)
    • Strain urine to catch passed stones for analysis
    • Wait for natural passage over days to weeks
    • Close monitoring; watch for signs of obstruction (oliguria, rising creatinine)

Ureteral Stent

  • Placed when stone is obstructing and doesn't pass spontaneously
  • Metal or plastic casing that enlarges the opening between ureter and bladder
  • Allows urine to bypass stone; reduces pain and obstruction
  • Temporary measure; may still pass stone or proceed to more definitive intervention

Surgical/Endourologic Interventions

  • Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy (ESWL): Outpatient procedure
    • Shockwaves pulverize stone into smaller fragments
    • Noninvasive external approach (stone broken and washed out)
    • Patient tolerates well; minimal downtime
    • Advantage: completely noninvasive
  • Ureteroscopy (Internal Approach): Via cystoscope
    • Direct visualization; laser or mechanical removal
    • Good for lower ureteral stones
  • Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy: For large or complex stones
    • Direct approach through flank skin
    • Allows complete stone removal

Post-Intervention Care

  • Expect hematuria after lithotripsy/ureteroscopy (normal from procedure trauma)
  • Hydration and pain control
  • Monitor for signs of infection or obstruction
  • Follow-up imaging to confirm stone passage/removal

Prevention of Recurrence

General Measures

  • Adequate Hydration: Most important—dilutes urine, prevents saturation
  • Strain Urine: Catch stones for analysis (determines prevention strategy)
  • Follow-up Imaging: Confirm passage; watch for recurrence

Dietary Modifications (Based on Stone Type)

  • Calcium Oxalate Stones: Low oxalate diet
    • Avoid: dark leafy greens (spinach), cocoa, nuts, tea
    • Calcium INTAKE doesn't need restriction (contradicts old thinking)
    • Low-sodium diet helps (reduces urinary calcium)
  • Uric Acid Stones: Low purine diet
    • Avoid: organ meats, anchovies, sardines, excessive protein
    • Alkalization of urine helps dissolve uric acid stones
  • Struvite Stones: Treat/prevent UTIs (these are bacteria-related)

Pharmacological Prevention

  • Thiazide Diuretics: For hypercalciuria-related stones (reduce urinary calcium)
  • Allopurinol: For uric acid stones (reduces uric acid production)
  • Citrate Supplementation: May help prevent stone formation

Nursing Priorities

  • Pain Management: Aggressive pain control is priority—severe pain can destabilize patient
  • Hydration: Encourage PO fluid intake (3-4 L/day) to promote passage
  • Monitor I&O: Watch for oliguria (suggests obstruction)
  • Strain All Urine: Collect stone for analysis
  • Watch for Complications: Fever (infection), rising creatinine (obstruction)
  • Patient Education: Lifelong hydration; dietary changes; prevent recurrence

Key Takeaways

  • Kidney stones cause severe visceral pain (one of strongest pain sensations)
  • Calcium oxalate stones are most common (75%)
  • Non-contrast spiral CT is gold standard for diagnosis
  • Small stones (<5mm) often pass spontaneously with hydration
  • ESWL is most common noninvasive treatment
  • Pain from kidney stones can cause hemodynamic compromise—pain management is priority
  • Prevention: adequate hydration is key; dietary modifications based on stone type
  • Always strain urine to obtain stone for analysis

A client with a kidney stone is writhing in pain, diaphoretic, with HR 124 and BP 172/98. What is the priority?
Pain management. Renal colic can be severe enough to change vital signs and destabilize the client.
What does the C-CUP mnemonic stand for?
Calcium oxalate (~75%, most common), Calcium phosphate, Uric acid, Phosphate (struvite).
What is the gold-standard test for kidney stones, and why?
Non-contrast spiral CT. It detects all stone types (including radiolucent uric acid stones) and shows the level of obstruction.
Which stone type won't show up on a KUB X-ray?
Uric acid stones (radiolucent).
What is the classic pain pattern of renal colic?
Sudden, severe flank pain radiating to the groin that waxes and wanes with spasms.
A client with a 4-mm stone is sent home. What should the nurse teach?
Drink 3–4 L/day, strain all urine, take pain medication as prescribed, and report fever or decreased urine output. Stones under 5 mm pass 70–80% of the time.
Why is all urine strained?
To catch the stone for analysis, which guides dietary and medical prevention.
A client with a stone develops a fever. What does it suggest?
A complicating infection behind the obstruction. Notify the provider.
Is pink urine expected after lithotripsy?
Yes, some hematuria is normal from procedural trauma. Still watch for infection or obstruction.
What diet prevents calcium oxalate stones?
Low oxalate (limit spinach, cocoa, nuts, tea) and low sodium. Calcium intake does NOT need to be restricted.
Which prevention measures fit uric acid stones?
Low-purine diet (avoid organ meats, anchovies, sardines), allopurinol, and alkalinizing the urine.
Which medication prevents stones caused by hypercalciuria?
Thiazide diuretics, which reduce urinary calcium.
