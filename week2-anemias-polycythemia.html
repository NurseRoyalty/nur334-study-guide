Anemias & Polycythemia

The anemia types below all fall under decreased RBC production, one of the three causal categories introduced on the Hematology Labs & Diagnostics page. This page also covers the nursing care shared across anemia types, and finishes with polycythemia — the opposite problem, too many RBCs.

Iron Deficiency Anemia

  • Who gets it: inadequate intake (unable to eat, anorexia, limited financial means), GI absorption problems, or blood loss (menstruation, hemolysis).
  • Morphology: microcytic, hypochromic.
  • If chronic, the patient may have no symptoms at all — same acute-vs-chronic pattern as any anemia.
FindingNotes
PallorThe most common symptom. Check the conjunctiva — pull down the lower eyelid and look for a clear line of demarcation between the pale inner lid and the red, capillary-rich tissue. A pale patient loses that line.
GlossitisAn inflamed tongue — red, smooth, and sore. Caused by epithelial atrophy.
Cheilitis / Angular CheilitisInflammation of the lips, especially at the corners of the mouth.
Brittle hair & spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia)Signs of more severe, longer-standing anemia — not something that develops quickly.
Pica / PagophagiaPica is craving non-food items; pagophagia is specifically craving ice. Not every patient gets this, but it's a classic, distinctive finding.

Severe, longstanding iron deficiency anemia has driven patients to swallow genuinely dangerous non-food objects (nails, screws, and similar) — an extreme expression of pica, and a reminder to take the symptom seriously when a patient reports unusual cravings.

B12 (Cobalamin) Deficiency Anemia

  • Who gets it: lack of intrinsic factor (secreted by gastric parietal cells, required to absorb B12) — most commonly from pernicious anemia, an autoimmune disease. Also: bowel surgery, malabsorption, strict vegan diet, current/chronic alcohol abuse, or gastrectomy (loses the parietal cells that make intrinsic factor).
  • Of these causes, only pernicious anemia is autoimmune — the others are mechanical or dietary.
  • Morphology: megaloblastic — large cells (macrocytic), normochromic. The membrane is fragile despite the larger size.

Manifestations are insidious: GI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, anorexia, abdominal pain, glossitis, cheilitis).

What makes B12 deficiency unique: it's the only anemia on this page with neuromuscular manifestations — paresthesia (numbness/tingling in the fingers and toes, often the first thing a patient notices) and ataxia (uncoordinated, off-balance, can look like intoxication). Iron deficiency, folate deficiency, and aplastic anemia don't cause this.

Pernicious anemia must be treated for life — it's autoimmune and doesn't resolve. Contrast that with a vegan patient's B12 deficiency, which can resolve once the diet changes.

Folate Deficiency Anemia

  • Who gets it: usually decreased intake, not an absorption problem — pregnancy (high demand) or alcohol abuse (poor diet) are the classic causes.
  • Morphology: microcytic, normochromic.

How to tell folate deficiency apart from B12 deficiency: the presentation is very similar and also insidious, but folate deficiency does not cause the neurologic problems (paresthesia, ataxia) that B12 deficiency does. Assessing for those neuro findings is how the two get differentiated clinically.

Flagged for verification: this course's lecture states folate deficiency's morphology as microcytic, normochromic — worth double-checking against the slide deck if you see it described elsewhere (e.g. a textbook) as macrocytic, since folate deficiency is more commonly classified that way outside this course.

Aplastic Anemia

  • Cause: usually idiopathic (unknown) — sometimes suspected to be autoimmune, but nothing definitive.
  • What makes it different: aplastic anemia affects all three cell lines at once — RBCs, WBCs, and platelets. That combination is called pancytopenia.
  • Morphology: normocytic, normochromic — the cells themselves look normal, there just aren't enough of them.

Diagnosed with a bone marrow biopsy — same procedure and nursing implications already covered in Hematology Labs & Diagnostics

Why aplastic anemia looks different from the other anemias on this page: because all three cell lines drop, the patient is at risk for fatigue (low RBCs), infection (low WBCs — neutropenia), and bleeding (low platelets — thrombocytopenia) — all in the same patient, all from one disease process.

Nursing Problems & Collaborative Care

TermMeaning
FatigueTired even at rest, without any activity.
Activity IntoleranceFeels okay at rest, but doesn't tolerate getting up and moving — heart rate rises, palpitations, anxiety, shortness of breath. Probably the most common complication seen with anemia.

Nutrition is its own nursing problem when intake or absorption of iron, B12, or folate is inadequate. And a patient with aplastic anemia has three nursing problems layered on top of each other in the same person: risk for bleeding (thrombocytopenia), risk for infection (neutropenia), and fatigue (anemia).

Underlying CauseCollaborative Care
MalnutritionIncrease intake.
Alcohol abuseSupport cessation; supplement.
Absorption issue (e.g. pernicious anemia)Mega-dose PO B12 or parenteral (IM) injections.
Blood lossFind and stop the source of bleeding.

Finding a bleeding source uses the same workup already covered in Hematology Labs & Diagnostics — stool for guaiac, EGD, colonoscopy

Medications for the Anemic Patient

DrugKey Points
Iron (Ferrous Sulfate)Preferred oral form. Once-daily dosing is now considered acceptable (older regimens gave it 3–4x/day). Absorbed in the duodenum — no enteric-coated or extended-release formulations. Best absorbed in an acidic environment, so give before meals when possible; if it causes nausea, give with food for the first couple of weeks, then try again without food. Liquid iron stains — give through a straw. IM injection uses Z-track technique to avoid staining the skin. IV iron carries a risk of fatal anaphylaxis — start slowly and be prepared to treat a reaction.
B12 (Cobalamin / Cyanocobalamin)Can be given PO, IM, sub-Q, or intranasally. IM is preferred if the patient has severe neurologic symptoms. If some intrinsic factor is still present, evidence shows a PO mega-dose (1,000 mcg, of which only ~2.4 mcg is actually absorbed) is as effective as an injection.
Folic AcidGiven PO. No significant adverse effects. Especially important during pregnancy.
Epoetin (synthetic erythropoietin)Used for chronic kidney disease and cancer patients who can't make their own erythropoietin. Given IV or sub-Q, roughly 3x/week — often together with iron, since RBC production needs both. Carries a black box warning; discontinued once hemoglobin exceeds 10 due to increased cardiovascular risk.
Iron turning the stool black is expected, not melena. Melena (digested blood in the stool) is dark, tacky/sticky, and very malodorous. Iron-darkened stool is just dark from the iron itself — it doesn't have that same malodorous, sticky quality. Know the difference and be able to teach it to a patient.
Why give iron and epoetin together: think buses and wheels — erythropoietin drives RBC production (the wheels), and iron builds the hemoglobin inside them (the seats). You need both for the system to actually work.

Nutrition Teaching

NutrientFood Sources
IronA frequent NCLEX/HESI select-all-that-apply topic — know which foods are actually iron-rich.
B12Found only in animal products — this is exactly why strict vegans are at risk without supplementation.
Folic AcidGreen leafy vegetables, legumes, grains, orange juice, nuts.

Whether vitamin C actually enhances iron absorption is mixed in the current literature, but the course's teaching point is to give vitamin C alongside iron.

Polycythemia

Polycythemia is an abnormally high H&H / RBC count — the opposite problem from everything covered so far on this page. There are three types.

TypeCause
Relative PolycythemiaDecreased plasma volume / dehydration — the same false-high hematocrit already covered in Hematology Labs & Diagnostics. Not a true increase in RBCs, and the patient is not actually anemic or polycythemic.
Primary (Polycythemia Vera)A genetic change acquired during the person's lifetime.
SecondaryA compensatory response to chronic tissue hypoxia — most commonly COPD, or long-term life at high altitude. This is the most common cause of polycythemia overall.

Relative polycythemia is the same false-high hematocrit concept already covered in Hematology Labs & Diagnostics

Quick-check ratio: hematocrit runs roughly 3× the hemoglobin — e.g. a hemoglobin around 12 pairs with a hematocrit around 36. Useful for a fast sanity check on whether two values line up.
FindingWhy
Hypertension, headache, difficulty concentratingThicker, higher-volume blood causes congestion.
Ruddy (dusky red) complexion; possible cyanosisRelated to the excess of RBCs.
Angina/MI, TIA/strokeDecreased perfusion from thick, sluggish blood — stroke is the most common serious acute complication.
Night sweats, weight lossA hypermetabolic state from the increased blood viscosity and volume.
Pruritus (itching), worse after a hot bathA classic sign, from the sheer increase in RBC number.
Pain in the fingers and toes—
Increased clotting risk runs through this whole disease. Thick, high-volume blood predisposes to both clot formation and hemorrhage — this is why perfusion to the heart (angina/MI) and brain (TIA/stroke) are real risks, and why anticoagulants are often part of treatment.
  • Phlebotomy is the mainstay treatment for chronic polycythemia — removing 300–500 mL of blood every other day initially, tapering to every few months as the volume comes down. Goal hematocrit: <42% in women, <45% in men.
  • Good hydration is an important teaching point — the blood is thick and viscous.
  • Anticoagulants may be used given the clotting risk.
  • Mobility is not automatically encouraged the way it usually is — the increased clotting risk cuts against the usual "get up and move" default.
  • Antihistamines (e.g. diphenhydramine) for pruritus.
  • Polycythemia vera is not preventable and is chronic — the patient needs ongoing evaluation and support for a disease that doesn't go away.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What is pagophagia, and what condition is it classically associated with?
Craving and chewing ice; a classic finding in iron deficiency anemia.
When checking a patient's conjunctiva for pallor, what finding suggests anemia?
Loss of the normal line of demarcation between the pale inner lid and the red, capillary-rich tissue.
What neurologic symptoms are unique to B12 deficiency, not seen in the other anemias?
Paresthesia (numbness/tingling in the fingers and toes) and ataxia (uncoordinated, off-balance).
Why must pernicious anemia be treated for life?
It's autoimmune — it doesn't resolve, unlike a dietary (e.g. vegan) B12 deficiency, which can improve once the diet changes.
A patient has anemia with insidious symptoms but no paresthesia or ataxia. B12 or folate deficiency?
Folate deficiency — the presentation looks similar to B12 deficiency but lacks its neurologic findings.
What is pancytopenia, and which anemia type causes it?
Low RBCs, WBCs, and platelets all at once — caused by aplastic anemia.
A patient with aplastic anemia is at risk for fatigue, infection, and bleeding. Why all three?
Fatigue from low RBCs, infection risk from low WBCs (neutropenia), and bleeding risk from low platelets (thrombocytopenia) — all from the same pancytopenia.
What's the difference between fatigue and activity intolerance?
Fatigue is tired even at rest. Activity intolerance means feeling okay at rest but not tolerating getting up and moving.
A patient on oral iron has black stool. Concerning?
No — expected finding from the iron itself, different from melena (which is malodorous and sticky from digested blood).
Why is epoetin often given together with iron?
Erythropoietin drives RBC production, but iron is needed to build the hemoglobin inside those RBCs — you need both.
Why avoid heating pads on a severely anemic patient who feels chilly?
Risk of a burn injury if the patient also has decreased sensation (paresthesia).
What are the three types of polycythemia, and which is the most common cause overall?
Relative (dehydration, false-high), primary (polycythemia vera, genetic), and secondary (compensatory, usually from chronic hypoxia like COPD) — secondary/hypoxia-driven is the most common cause.
