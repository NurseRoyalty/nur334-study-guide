Legal & Ethical Issues in Nursing

Fundamentals content, tested on the first exam alongside Week 1 & 2 material. Covers the legal acts every nurse needs to know, plus a brief intro to ethics.

Scope of Practice & the Nurse Practice Act

  • Scope of practice is a legal act that defines nursing and reflects the profession's values — regulated by the American Nurses Association (ANA) and other professional organizations. It shapes what nursing schools teach and what the NCLEX tests, and always reflects current best evidence-based practice.
  • The Joint Commission (which accredits hospitals) requires institutions to have written, accessible scope-of-practice policies — so scope can vary by institution. Example from lecture: at Baptist Health Lexington, nurses can pull arterial sheaths; at UK Healthcare, they cannot. Know what's within your scope at your specific institution.
  • The Nurse Practice Act is the set of state laws governing licensure. Every nurse must be licensed in the state they work in — including travel nurses.
  • Compact licensure: a multi-state agreement that lets a nurse licensed in one participating state practice in another participating state without retesting or extra paperwork. Not every state participates — know your own state's status.

HIPAA

  • The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act establishes a patient's right to privacy over their health information and records.
  • Before HIPAA, patients did not automatically have access to their own medical record. HIPAA established that patients own their medical record and can always request access to it.
  • HIPAA also limits who else can access a patient's information (e.g. a friend who works as a nurse on the floor cannot look up your chart without a care-related reason) and defines what counts as protected health information (PHI).

The lecture mentions there are 11 categories of protected health information worth knowing for the NCLEX-style questions, but didn't list them out loud on the recording — worth checking your textbook or slide deck for the exact list before the exam.

HIPAA ComponentWhat It Covers
Privacy RuleHealth information must be kept private; it's the hospital's responsibility to keep personal health records private.
Security RulePractical safeguards — e.g. firewalls, not leaving charts in an unlocked cabinet — that keep health information inaccessible to the public.
Breach NotificationIf a breach of access occurs, the hospital must notify the affected patients.
Safety / Research UseIdentifiable PHI used to analyze and improve patient safety (e.g. in research or quality improvement) must still be protected.

Patient Self-Determination Act, Advance Directives & Living Wills

  • The Patient Self-Determination Act (PSDA) requires healthcare institutions to give patients written information about their right to make decisions about their own care — including the right to refuse treatment and to formulate an advance directive.
  • It's broader than end-of-life care alone, but in practice it's most often applied to advance directives. Patients must be offered information about advance directives when they're admitted.
  • At minimum, an advance directive must state the patient's wishes about respiratory and cardiac arrest, and name their durable power of attorney for healthcare — the person who makes care decisions on their behalf if they become incapacitated.
  • The central legal/ethical question: is the patient currently decisionally capacitated (able to make their own care decisions)? This connects directly to informed consent, covered next.
  • A living will is a specific type of advance directive that goes into more detail about end-of-life preferences — often including do-not-resuscitate wishes and whether the patient wants enteral (tube) feeding if they can't eat or drink on their own.
  • A patient must have decision-making capacity to create either an advance directive or a living will. If they've lost capacity before completing one (e.g. advanced dementia, or sedated after a traumatic injury), it's too late — care decisions then fall to next-of-kin laws or whatever documentation already exists.

Informed Consent

  • Informed consent is an explanation of a procedure or treatment, required before every procedure performed at a hospital.
  • It must include the names and qualifications of everyone performing or assisting — the attending physician's name must be on the form even if a fellow or resident is actually doing the procedure.
  • It must describe possible complications, including serious harm up to and including death, and anticipated pain or discomfort.
  • It must state that the patient has the right to refuse the procedure or treatment.
  • If the patient is incapacitated (e.g. an emergency), consent authority falls to whoever holds their durable power of attorney for healthcare, per their advance directive.

DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) Orders

A DNR order does not mean "do not care." It means only that if the patient's breathing or heart stops, the team will not begin CPR. Every other aspect of care continues as normal.
  • A DNR can be stated in a living will or an advance directive, and it can be revoked at any time — a patient can change their mind.
  • There is legal complexity around whether a durable power of attorney for healthcare can override a patient's own standing DNR order if the patient becomes incapacitated — this is the kind of situation that involves hospital legal counsel.
  • As the nurse, you need to know if your patient has a DNR order — check the chart, and be aware some facilities also use bracelets or door signage per their own policy.

Other Legal Acts to Know

ActWhat It Does
EMTALAAnyone presenting to an emergency department must be treated — regardless of insurance status, ability to pay, immigration status, or criminal history.
ADA (Americans with Disabilities Act)Protects the rights of people with physical or mental disabilities; can affect where and what kind of care a patient receives.

You're unlikely to be tested on fine detail for most of these — the instructor's guidance was to be able to talk about each in a general sense if asked, with HIPAA's protected health information being the one exception worth knowing more precisely.

Ethics Fundamentals

  • Ethics stem from values — a deeply held personal belief about the worth of an idea, attitude, custom, or object that affects a person's choices and behavior. Values usually form during childhood and early adulthood, starting from family influence and then becoming individual.
  • An ethical dilemma occurs when two opposing courses of action can both be justified by ethical principles — it almost always arises from conflicting values, and happens more often in healthcare than most people expect.
  • Values clarification is the process of resolving an ethical dilemma by distinguishing your own values from the facts of the situation, other people's opinions, and what you're actually being asked to do.
  • Moral distress is when a nurse feels the need to take a specific action while believing that action may be wrong. Lecture example: caring for a Jehovah's Witness patient (a faith that prohibits receiving blood products) after cardiac surgery — even when a transfusion could be life-saving, the patient's right to refuse it must be honored, which can create real moral distress for the nurse.
  • Common sources of ethical conflict in healthcare: social media, quality of life at the end of life, and access to healthcare (see Box 22.5 in your textbook for detailed examples).

Professional Nursing Code of Ethics

As a licensed nurse, you commit to a professional code of ethics built on four pillars: advocacy (for the nursing profession and your patients), responsibility (practicing responsibly), accountability (owning your actions as a nurse), and confidentiality (keeping what you learn as a nurse private).

What is "scope of nursing practice," and who defines it?
A legal act that defines nursing and reflects the profession's values, regulated by the ANA and other professional organizations — but the specific scope can vary by institution.
What is the Nurse Practice Act, and what does compact licensure allow?
State laws governing nursing licensure — a nurse must be licensed in the state they practice in. Compact licensure lets a nurse licensed in one participating state practice in another participating state without retesting.
What are HIPAA's four main components?
Privacy Rule, Security Rule, Breach Notification, and protecting identifiable PHI used in research/safety improvement.
At minimum, what must an advance directive state?
The patient's wishes regarding respiratory and cardiac arrest, and who holds their durable power of attorney for healthcare.
What's the difference between an advance directive and a living will?
A living will is a more detailed type of advance directive focused specifically on end-of-life preferences (e.g. DNR wishes, tube feeding). Both require the patient to have decision-making capacity to create.
What five things must informed consent include?
An explanation of the procedure, the names/qualifications of who's performing it, possible complications (including death), anticipated pain/discomfort, and the patient's right to refuse.
Does a DNR order mean "do not treat"?
No — it only means no CPR if the patient's breathing or heart stops. All other care continues, and the order can be revoked at any time.
What does EMTALA require?
Any patient presenting to an emergency department must be treated, regardless of insurance status, ability to pay, or immigration status.
What's the key limit on Good Samaritan Law protection?
It only protects actions within your actual scope of practice — you can't perform a skill you're not trained or licensed to do, even in an emergency.
What is an ethical dilemma?
A situation where two opposing courses of action can both be justified by ethical principles — it almost always arises from conflicting values.
What is moral distress?
When a nurse feels the need to take a specific action while believing that action may be wrong.
What are the four pillars of the professional nursing code of ethics?
Advocacy, responsibility, accountability, and confidentiality.
