HIV Care & Management

Comprehensive guide to HIV treatment, antiretroviral therapy, prevention strategies (PrEP/PEP), adherence challenges, psychosocial aspects, and nursing care for people living with HIV.

Clinical Management and Treatment

HIV Disease Progression and Clinical Course

Four Stages of HIV Infection

  • Stage 1 — Acute Infection (Early): Virus replicates rapidly but remains undetectable on lab tests; patient is asymptomatic but HIGHLY INFECTIOUS; window period before antibodies develop
  • Stage 2 — Seroconversion: Antibodies become detectable; patient feels "the worst flu ever"; highly symptomatic (fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy); still highly infectious; may not seek testing due to symptom interpretation
  • Stage 3 — Clinical Latency (Asymptomatic Period): Body effectively fighting infection; can last 3-12+ years; patient is asymptomatic or has only mild symptoms; virus continues rapid replication; CD4 steadily drops; patient may not know they're HIV positive if undiagnosed
  • Stage 4 — AIDS: Defined as CD4 < 200 cells/μL OR presence of AIDS-defining opportunistic infections; patient becomes severely immunocompromised; without treatment, becomes symptomatic and vulnerable to multiple OIs

Collaborative and Nursing Management

Key Nursing Responsibilities

  • Monitor Progression and Immune Function: Assess CD4 counts, viral loads; educate patient on meaning of these values; recognize when CD4 drops indicate need for OI prophylaxis
  • Initiate and Monitor Antiretroviral Therapy: Teach medication regimen (timing, how to take, interactions); monitor for side effects; assess adherence; report concerns to provider
  • Prevent Opportunistic Infections: Keep patient as healthy as possible; monitor, detect, and report signs of OIs; ensure prophylaxis medications when CD4 indicates need
  • Manage Symptoms: Address pain, fatigue, GI symptoms, cognitive changes (AIDS dementia complex); provide comfort measures
  • Manage ART Complications: Monitor for and address medication side effects (lipodystrophy, peripheral neuropathy, hyperglycemia, liver toxicity); support adherence despite side effects
  • Prevent Further Transmission: Teach prevention strategies, safe sex practices, avoiding sharing items that may have blood contact
  • Psychosocial Support: Address fear, depression, anxiety related to diagnosis; discuss stigma; support disclosure decisions; connect with mental health resources

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART)

Medication Classes and Mechanisms

  • Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs/NtRTIs): Block reverse transcriptase; prevent RNA → DNA conversion; include AZT, lamivudine, emtricitabine, tenofovir
  • Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs): Block reverse transcriptase at different site; include efavirenz, rilpivirine; often cause dizziness, vivid dreams, liver toxicity
  • Protease Inhibitors (PIs): Block protease; prevent viral protein processing; include ritonavir (used as booster), lopinavir, darunavir; require food for absorption; cause lipodystrophy, hyperglycemia
  • Integrase Inhibitors: Block integrase; prevent viral DNA integration into host genome; include dolutegravir, bictegravir; well-tolerated; allow once-daily dosing
  • Entry/Fusion Inhibitors: Block viral entry; include maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist), enfuvirtide; reserved for treatment-experienced patients

Treatment Initiation and Monitoring

  • When to Start ART: Initiated immediately upon HIV diagnosis regardless of CD4 count (Start treatment as soon as diagnosed)
  • Adherence — Critical Factor: Non-adherence leads to treatment failure, drug resistance, and disease progression; if patient doesn't feel sick, adherence becomes harder
  • Promoting Adherence: Motivate patient BEFORE giving prescription; ensure social support present; negotiate treatment plan giving patient autonomy; devise simple plan; anticipate side effects and address preemptively; establish trust
  • Single-Tablet Regimens (STR): Preferred for simplicity; examples include Biktarvy (bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir), Triumeq (dolutegravir/abacavir/lamivudine); once-daily dosing improves adherence
  • Monitoring During Treatment: CD4 count and viral load every 3-4 months until stable; baseline and periodic labs for liver/kidney function, glucose, lipids; assess for side effects regularly
  • Treatment Goals: Undetectable = Untransmittable (U=U); viral load < 50 copies/mL achieves this; patient with undetectable viral load cannot transmit HIV to sexual partners

Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)

Prevention for High-Risk Individuals

  • Candidates for PrEP: Anyone with anal/vaginal sex in past 6 months WITH sexual partner(s) with HIV (especially unknown/detectable viral load) OR inconsistent condom use; IV drug users with shared injection partners with HIV; sexually transmitted disease in past 6 months
  • Mechanism: Daily medication (or event-based) to lower chance of acquiring HIV before possible exposure
  • Effectiveness: 99% effective at preventing HIV infection through sexual contact WHEN TAKEN AS PRESCRIBED; 74% effective for IV drug users; requires consistent adherence
  • Common Regimen: Tenofovir/Emtricitabine (Truvada) once daily; alternative: Bictegravir/Emtricitabine/Tenofovir (Biktarvy)
  • Critical Education: PrEP does NOT protect against other STIs; patient must continue condom use; requires regular monitoring (baseline and follow-up labs every 3 months)
  • Insurance Coverage: Most insurance and Medicaid cover PrEP medications, reducing cost barrier

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)

Emergency Prevention After Exposure

  • Indications: Needle stick/cut with sharp from HIV+ source, mucosal membrane exposure to blood/certain body fluids, unprotected sexual exposure to HIV+ partner
  • Needle Stick Risk: Occupational exposure (needle stick) carries only 0.3% transmission risk even from HIV+ source — GOOD NEWS but still requires action
  • Timing — CRITICAL: Must be started within 1-2 hours of exposure (IDEAL), but MUST start within 3 days maximum; efficacy decreases with delayed initiation
  • Regimen: 28-day combination antiretroviral therapy (usually 3-drug combination); specific drugs chosen based on source's treatment history and resistance pattern
  • Follow-Up Testing: HIV testing required at 6 weeks, 12 weeks, and 6 months post-exposure
  • Hospital Protocol: Rapid incident reporting, blood draws from both exposed person and source, quick turnaround for testing = all reasons why needle stick procedures move fast

Patient Education and Psychosocial Care

Critical Nursing Counseling Topics

  • Immunosuppression Awareness: Patient with CD4 < 200 is severely immunosuppressed; teach avoidance of crowded areas, poor sanitation, foods with bacterial risk (undercooked meats, unpasteurized dairy)
  • Environmental Precautions: Avoid cleaning pet litter boxes (Toxoplasma risk), keep home environment clean, don't allow sick visitors during advanced immunosuppression
  • Oral Assessment Importance: Oral manifestations (thrush, herpes, ulcers, candidiasis) are common with CD4 drop; regular oral exams detect early problems
  • Cognitive Changes: AIDS dementia complex can develop with advanced HIV; patient/family need to understand this is neurological manifestation, not mental illness
  • Infection Types: Manifestations can be fungal, viral, bacterial, or cancerous; educate on recognition of each type
  • Sensitive Discussions: Nurse must initiate discussions about sexual practices, drug use, adherence, disclosure; patients are often eager to discuss if nurse brings it up; practice these conversations to become comfortable
  • Coping with Initial Diagnosis: Many patients discover diagnosis unexpectedly; support them through emotional processing; connect with mental health resources, support groups
  • Stigma and Mental Health: Living with HIV is lifelong battle; stigma causes depression/anxiety; screening for depression essential; refer to mental health counseling; express empathy and non-judgment
💡 CRITICAL NURSING PRINCIPLE: Living with HIV and AIDS is a lifelong battle. As a nurse, express empathy and maintain a judgment-free approach. The patient's emotional and mental health support are as important as medical treatment. Often the nurse is the person who brings up sensitive topics that others avoid — make that conversation safe and supportive.

Adherence Strategies and Outcomes

Promoting Treatment Success

  • Patient Motivation: Ensure patient is motivated BEFORE prescribing ART; unmotivated patients will not adhere; take time to discuss why treatment matters
  • Social Support — PRIMARY PRIORITY: Patients with strong social support have better outcomes; help identify/strengthen support systems (family, friends, partner, support groups)
  • Autonomy and Negotiation: Give patient input on treatment decisions; simple plans work better than complex ones; patient has better outcomes when feeling control over decisions
  • Side Effect Anticipation: Discuss likely side effects, how to manage them, when they improve; unprepared patients stop taking meds when side effects occur
  • Trust-Building: Ongoing relationships with consistent care providers build trust; patient must trust healthcare team to move forward successfully
  • Monitoring Labs: CD4 and viral load every 3-4 months (usually); helps patient understand if treatment is working; provides objective feedback
  • Drug Interactions: Keep vigilant about potential interactions with OTC medications, supplements, foods; educate patient on what to avoid

Prevention of HIV Transmission

Risk Reduction Strategies

  • Needle Exchange Programs: Reduce transmission among IV drug users; provide clean needles, education
  • Perinatal Transmission Prevention: Pregnant women on ART with undetectable viral load have < 1% risk of transmission to baby; early prenatal care critical
  • Routine HIV Testing: Encourages early diagnosis; allows early treatment initiation; reduces transmission window
  • Universal Precautions: Blood and certain body fluids are potentially infectious (blood, semen, vaginal secretions, CSF, synovial fluid, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, pericardial fluid); NOT transmitted through saliva, sweat, tears, urine, feces
  • Occupational Exposure Protocol: Know your facility's protocols for needle stick incidents; blood-borne pathogen training mandatory; report exposures immediately
  • Patient Education: Teach safe sex practices, consistent condom use, disclosure to partners, avoiding sharing of items with blood contact; patients who receive this education from nurse often report feeling empowered
