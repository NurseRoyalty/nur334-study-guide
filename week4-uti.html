Urinary Tract Infections (UTI)

← Back to Study Guide

Overview

UTIs account for almost one-third of all healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). When discussing UTIs, it's critical to understand the distinction between upper and lower UTIs, as this classification determines severity and treatment approach.

  • Upper UTI (Pyelonephritis): Involves the kidney parenchyma and collecting system. More serious with systemic manifestations (fever, chills, flank pain).
  • Lower UTI (Cystitis): Localized bladder infection without systemic signs. More common in women.
  • Related Terms: Urosepsis occurs when UTI progresses to bacteremia—a life-threatening condition.

Pathophysiology & Risk Factors

Why UTIs Develop

The urinary tract above the urethra is normally sterile. Most UTIs result from gram-negative bacteria (typically E. coli) ascending the urethra in a retrograde fashion.

Major Risk Factors

  • Urinary Stasis: Neurogenic bladder, kidney stones obstructing ureter, severe constipation impeding flow
  • Anatomical Factors: Females are more prone due to shorter urethra and proximity of anus to urethral opening
  • Immune Compromise: Aging, diabetes, immunosuppression
  • Behavioral Factors: Poor perineal hygiene, habitual delay in urination ("nurse's bladder"), inadequate fluid intake
  • Medical Factors: Pregnancy (hormones relax ureters; fetus compresses bladder), indwelling catheters (CAUTIs)
Critical Point: Catheters are a major risk factor. The saying goes: "Catheters are bad"—they must be removed as soon as absolutely no longer needed. Nurses often play a key role in advocating for catheter removal.

Clinical Manifestations: Lower UTI

Patients with lower UTI experience problems with bladder emptying or storage.

Emptying Problems (Dysuria Syndrome)

  • Hesitancy: Delay before urine stream begins
  • Intermittency: Stream stops and starts
  • Post-void dribbling: Loss of urine after urination appears complete
  • Urinary retention: Inability to fully empty bladder
  • Dysuria: Discomfort or pain with urination

Storage Problems (Irritative Symptoms)

  • Urgency: Persistent urge to void (feeling you won't make it to bathroom)
  • Frequency: Frequent voiding, sometimes every 10 seconds
  • Incontinence: Involuntary loss of urine
  • Nocturia: Waking multiple times at night to urinate
  • Nocturnal Enuresis: Bedwetting

If you've had a bladder infection, you know exactly what this feels like!

UTI in Special Populations

Women

  • Most common bacterial infection in women
  • Typically caused by gram-negative bacteria (E. coli)
  • Bacteria ascend via urethra in retrograde fashion

Older Adults

  • Often presents WITHOUT the classic symptoms you'd expect
  • Confusion is often the FIRST and PRIMARY sign of UTI in elderly patients—this is real and happens frequently.
  • No fever, no dysuria, no urgency—just acute confusion
  • This is why UTI should be ruled out in any older adult with acute mental status changes

Diagnosis

Diagnostic Findings

  • Urinalysis: Gold standard—shows pyuria (WBCs in urine) and bacteriuria
  • Dipstick (Point of Care): Quick bedside test
    • Positive nitrites (bacteria produce nitrite from dietary nitrate)
    • Positive leukocyte esterase (enzyme in WBCs indicating pyuria)
  • Urine Culture: Gold standard for diagnosis; confirms bacterial species and antibiotic sensitivities
  • CBC: Elevated WBC count may be present

Specimen Collection

  • Clean Catch: Standard method for outpatient diagnosis
    • Cleanse perineal area first
    • Begin voiding, then midstream collection
    • Reduces contamination from skin flora
  • Straight Catheterization: Used for hospitalized patients when accuracy critical
  • Suprapubic Aspiration: Gold standard for sterile specimen (bypasses urethra entirely)

Treatment & Management

Pharmacological Management

  • First-line Antibiotics:
    • TMP-SMX (Bactrim, Septra)—still effective in many regions
    • Nitrofurantoin (Macrodantin)—excellent for uncomplicated cystitis
    • Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin)—for complicated UTIs
  • Duration: Typically 3-7 days for uncomplicated UTI
  • Approach: Often start broad-spectrum antibiotic; narrow based on culture results

Supportive Care

  • Hydration: Increased fluid intake (not restricted) to help flush bladder
  • Comfort Measures: Phenazopyridine (Pyridium) for dysuria relief (turns urine orange)
  • Frequent Voiding: Helps empty bladder completely

Prevention

  • Adequate Hydration: Dilute urine, promote frequent voiding
  • Regular Voiding: Don't hold urine; empty bladder completely
  • Post-intercourse Urination: In women—flushes bacteria introduced during intercourse
  • Proper Perineal Hygiene: Wipe front to back to prevent fecal contamination
  • Catheter Removal: Remove indwelling catheters ASAP—every day they stay increases HAI risk
  • Cranberry Products: May contain compounds that prevent bacterial adherence (some evidence)

Key Takeaways

  • UTIs are the most common bacterial infection in women
  • E. coli ascending the urethra is the typical mechanism
  • Lower UTI symptoms are bladder-related (dysuria, frequency, urgency)
  • Older adults may present ONLY with confusion—don't miss this
  • Urinalysis and culture are diagnostic standards
  • Short course of antibiotics (3-7 days) is typical
  • Prevention centers on hydration, hygiene, and prompt catheter removal
