Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

← Back to Study Guide

Overview

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a progressive loss of kidney function that develops over months to years, unlike AKI which develops over hours to days. CKD is particularly prevalent in med-surg settings and is a very complex condition affecting patient outcomes significantly.

  • CKD progresses through 6 stages (Stage 1 through Stage 5, with Stage 3 subdivided into 3A and 3B)
  • Two primary causes account for most CKD: hypertension and diabetes mellitus
  • Early detection and treatment are critical for slowing disease progression
  • Kidney has incredible physiologic reserves—patients often lose significant function before symptoms appear
  • Stage 5 (ESRD) requires dialysis or kidney transplant for survival

End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) — Clinical Manifestations

When kidneys fail, they can no longer maintain fluid and electrolyte homeostasis. This leads to multiple systemic complications:

Fluid and Electrolyte Imbalances

  • Fluid Overload: Edema (peripheral and pulmonary), hypertension, shortness of breath
  • Hyperkalemia: Elevated potassium accumulation (life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias)
  • Elevated Phosphorus & Magnesium: Mineral accumulation from inability to excrete
  • Metabolic Acidosis: From accumulation of acidic waste products (urea, creatinine)
  • Hypocalcemia: From hyperphosphatemia and decreased vitamin D activation

Hematologic and Metabolic Manifestations

  • Severe Anemia: Loss of erythropoietin production by kidneys → decreased RBC production
  • Renal Osteodystrophy: Bone disease from mineral imbalances (brittle bones)
  • Dyslipidemia: Elevated lipid levels (patients often on statin drugs)
  • Uremia: Accumulation of urea and waste products in blood causing CNS changes

Nutritional and Systemic Manifestations

  • Anorexia and Malnutrition: Patient doesn't feel like eating; hypermetabolic state
  • Pruritus (Itching): Severe itching—one of the most uncomfortable symptoms
  • CNS Changes: Confusion, altered mental status from uremia
  • Nausea and Vomiting: From waste accumulation
  • Fatigue and Weakness: From anemia and overall metabolic dysfunction
Critical Point: The kidney has an incredible ability to compensate with physiologic reserves. Patients can lose over 50% of kidney function before noticing symptoms. This is why early detection (Stages 1-3) is so important.

Prevention of CKD Progression

Early Detection and Control

  • Two Main Causes: Hypertension and diabetes mellitus account for the MAJORITY of CKD cases
  • If patient has both HTN and diabetes: Extensive teaching required—both must be maintained at steady levels to prevent kidney damage
  • Goal: Catch CKD in Stages 1-3A when progression can be slowed significantly

Risk Reduction Strategies

  • Control blood pressure aggressively
  • Maintain tight glucose control in diabetic patients
  • Early screening for at-risk populations
  • Patient education on lifestyle modifications

Conservative Management (Non-Dialysis)

Blood Pressure Control

  • Most important intervention to slow CKD progression
  • Aggressive management required

Glucose Control

  • Maintain tight glycemic control in diabetic CKD patients

Contrast Dye Precautions (Nephrotoxicity Prevention)

  • Issue: Many diagnostic procedures require contrast dye (CT, cardiac catheterization, etc.)
  • Contrast dye is NEPHROTOXIC and can worsen CKD
  • Interventions to reduce nephrotoxicity:
    • Acetylcysteine (N-acetylcysteine): Given prior to procedure to decrease severity of contrast nephrotoxicity
    • Aggressive hydration ("force fluids"): Dilutes contrast and promotes excretion (if not contraindicated)
    • Check baseline creatinine before procedure to assess renal function
    • Consider alternative non-contrast imaging when possible
  • Clinical Pearl: Acetylcysteine is also the antidote for Tylenol overdose

Avoid Nephrotoxic Medications

  • NSAIDs: Very hard on the kidneys—generally AVOIDED in CKD patients
  • Aminoglycosides: Antibiotic class that is nephrotoxic
  • Alternative: Acetaminophen instead of NSAIDs (though hard on liver if overused)

Monitor Labs and Hydration

  • Monitor fluid and electrolyte levels (labs often "all over the place" in CKD)
  • Daily weights (more reliable than I&O)
  • Strict intake and output monitoring

Management of CKD Complications

Excess Fluid Volume

  • Problem: Kidneys can't excrete fluid
  • Interventions:
    • Fluid restrictions (type and amount individualized)
    • Diuretics (if kidneys still have some function)
    • Monitor I&O and daily weights
    • Low sodium diet (reduces fluid retention)
    • Monitor for signs of fluid overload: HTN, edema, shortness of breath, JVD, pulmonary edema

Hyperkalemia

  • Goal: Keep potassium between 3.5-5.0 mEq/L
  • If approaching 6.0: Anticipate orders to lower potassium (same methods as AKI)
  • Interventions:
    • Potassium-binding resins
    • Calcium gluconate for cardiac protection
    • Insulin + dextrose to drive K+ into cells
    • Low potassium diet

Hypertension

  • Aggressive treatment required to slow CKD progression
  • Pharmacological and lifestyle management

Anemia

  • Cause: Loss of erythropoietin (EPO) production
  • Interventions:
    • Administer anti-anemic agents (erythropoietin/EPO)
    • Monitor hemoglobin and hematocrit closely
    • Be aware benefits/risks of EPO (can increase risk of thrombosis if Hgb too high)
    • Nutritional support with adequate iron and folate

Mineral and Bone Disorders

  • Problem: Hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia leading to renal osteodystrophy (brittle bones)
  • Interventions:
    • Monitor phosphorus and calcium levels carefully
    • Calcium supplements
    • Vitamin D supplements (active form)
    • Phosphate binders if needed
    • Fall prevention (brittle bones = fracture risk)

Metabolic Acidosis

  • Cause: Inability to excrete acidic waste products
  • Monitor: pH and bicarbonate levels

Dyslipidemia

  • Elevated lipid formation: Patients often on statin drugs

Malnutrition

  • Major problem: Patients don't eat well, leading to severe malnutrition
  • Interventions:
    • Nutritional assessment and monitoring
    • Monitor serum protein levels, Hgb, and Hct
    • Assess for nausea and vomiting
    • Monitor weight trends
    • Provide appealing foods that meet restrictions
    • Nutritional consults

Nutritional Management

Protein Intake — A Paradox

  • The Problem: BUN and creatinine are protein metabolism byproducts; if kidneys can't excrete them, protein levels rise
  • Solution (Pre-Dialysis): Protein RESTRICTION required to minimize waste accumulation
  • The Paradox: This puts already-malnourished patients on further dietary restriction
  • Post-Dialysis Change: Once patient starts dialysis (Stage 5), dietary restrictions become more LIBERAL because dialysis filters out impurities—protein intake can INCREASE

Dietary Restrictions

  • Pre-Dialysis: Low protein, low sodium, low potassium, low phosphorus, fluid restriction
  • Post-Dialysis: Can be more liberal with protein; still restrict sodium, potassium, phosphorus based on individual needs
  • Carbohydrates: Slow-release carbs preferred
  • Calories: High-calorie diet needed to prevent further malnutrition

Renal Nutrition Products

  • Nephroshake or Novasource Renal: Specialized tube feeds for CKD patients
  • Composition: High-calorie, high-protein (especially for dialysis patients), LOW in potassium, LOW in phosphorus, slow-release carbs
  • Can be used for PO supplementation or tube feeding

Fluid and Electrolyte Dietary Needs

  • Fluid Restriction: Required when patient is oliguric
  • Calculation Method: Many facilities calculate daily fluid allowance based on YESTERDAY'S urine output
  • Sodium Restriction: Important to reduce fluid retention
  • Potassium Restriction: Essential due to hyperkalemia risk
  • Phosphorus Restriction: To prevent mineral disorders and renal osteodystrophy
  • Magnesium Restriction: Often needed

Nursing Management Priorities

Fluid and Electrolyte Monitoring

  • Monitor labs frequently—they are "all over the place" in CKD
  • Daily weights (most reliable indicator of fluid status)
  • Strict I&O measurement
  • Watch for signs of electrolyte imbalances (muscle weakness for hyperkalemia)

Symptom Management

  • Monitor for signs and symptoms of fluid volume excess
  • Assess for nausea, vomiting, anorexia
  • Address pruritus (itching)—one of the most distressing symptoms
  • Monitor for CNS changes (confusion, altered mental status)

Compliance Support

  • Teach dietary restrictions and rationales: Patients need to understand WHY restrictions exist
  • Address thirst management: Restricted patients are very thirsty—offer alternatives (mouth swabs, ice chips, lozenges)
  • Medication education: Teach about common side effects and importance of adherence
  • Weight monitoring: Teach to report weight gain >4 pounds in 2 days (indicates fluid gain)
  • Report red flags: Shortness of breath, increased fatigue, confusion, swelling, rapid weight gain

Risk Management

  • Risk for Infection: CKD causes immunosuppression (toxic accumulation, immune cell dysfunction, chronic inflammation)
  • Monitor for signs of infection; teach disease transmission prevention
  • Maintain aseptic technique with dialysis access or peritoneal catheter
  • Risk for Injury: From renal osteodystrophy (brittle bones)—implement fall prevention
  • Activity Intolerance: Patients are severely anemic; ensure adequate rest; monitor for fatigue

Psychosocial Support

  • CKD causes significant lifestyle changes, especially if dialysis required
  • Encourage family and social support systems
  • Acknowledge difficulties in modifying diet and fluid intake
  • Discuss dialysis type options and lifestyle impact
  • Provide written resources
  • Encourage nutritional consults and follow-ups
  • Recognize that lifelong treatment (if dialysis) significantly impacts quality of life

Key Takeaways

  • CKD is IRREVERSIBLE but PROGRESSIVE—unlike AKI which is reversible
  • 6 stages based on GFR; Stage 3 is the critical detection point to slow progression
  • Kidney's physiologic reserves allow loss of >50% function before symptoms appear
  • Hypertension and diabetes are the two main causes of CKD
  • Stage 5 (GFR <15) is ESRD and requires dialysis or transplant for survival
  • End-stage manifestations include anemia, mineral/bone disorders, metabolic acidosis, malnutrition, pruritus, CNS changes
  • Conservative management focuses on BP/glucose control and avoiding nephrotoxic drugs
  • Contrast dye precautions: use acetylcysteine and aggressive hydration to prevent nephrotoxicity
  • AVOID NSAIDs in CKD; use acetaminophen instead
  • Protein restriction PRE-dialysis; can increase protein POST-dialysis when treatment begins
  • Dietary restrictions are strict (low K, low Na, low phosphorus, fluid restriction) and are major compliance challenge
  • Nutrition support with renal products (nephroshakes, Novasource Renal) is critical
  • Daily weights and strict I&O are essential nursing interventions
  • CKD patients are immunosuppressed—watch for infection
  • Brittle bones from osteodystrophy—implement fall prevention
  • Multiple medications required for symptom management—teaching about side effects essential
  • Psychological support is critical; lifestyle changes are significant, especially with dialysis

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What are the two main causes of CKD?
Hypertension and diabetes mellitus.
Why is CKD often found late?
The kidneys have large reserves; more than 50% of function can be lost before symptoms appear.
A client's GFR is 22. Which stage is this?
Stage 4 (15–29). Symptoms usually appear and planning for dialysis or transplant begins.
What defines stage 5 CKD (ESRD)?
GFR <15. The client needs dialysis or a kidney transplant to survive.
What is the most important intervention to slow CKD progression?
Aggressive blood pressure control (plus tight glucose control in diabetes).
A client with CKD asks for ibuprofen. What should the nurse recommend?
Avoid NSAIDs because they are nephrotoxic; acetaminophen is the preferred alternative.
What should the nurse anticipate before a contrast CT in a client with CKD?
Check baseline creatinine, give acetylcysteine as ordered, and hydrate aggressively if not contraindicated.
Why are clients with CKD anemic, and what is the treatment?
Kidneys stop making erythropoietin. Treat with an ESA (EPO), and monitor Hgb because too high a level raises thrombosis risk.
What causes renal osteodystrophy, and what is the nursing priority?
High phosphorus and low calcium (plus less vitamin D activation) make bones brittle. Fall prevention is the priority.
How does protein intake change once a client starts dialysis?
Protein is restricted before dialysis, but can INCREASE once dialysis starts because dialysis clears the waste.
A client with CKD gains 5 lb in 2 days. What should the nurse do?
Report it. A gain of more than 4 lb in 2 days signals fluid retention.
How can the nurse help a thirsty client on fluid restriction?
Offer mouth swabs, ice chips, or lozenges, and explain why the restriction is needed.
