Electrolyte Imbalances

Content Status: This page covers sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus imbalances from Week 3 lectures. Review key concepts, clinical significance, and treatment approaches below.

Overview: Electrolytes & Osmolality

  • Electrolytes are minerals dissolved in blood that carry electrical charges. They regulate fluid balance, nerve function, and muscle contraction.
  • Osmolality is the concentration of dissolved particles in blood (normal: 280–300 mOsm/kg). Too high = hypernatremia; too low = hyponatremia.
  • The kidneys and thirst center control osmolality by regulating water and electrolyte excretion.
  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) released by the posterior pituitary increases water reabsorption to concentrate urine and conserve water when osmolality is high.

Sodium Imbalances (Na)

HYPONATREMIA (<135 mEq/L): Dilutional or depletional (distinguish by fluid status — euvolemic, hypervolemic, hypovolemic). SIADH, diuretics, heart failure, cirrhosis, renal disease, or excessive water intake.

Potassium Imbalances (K)

HYPERKALEMIA (>5.5): Dangerous — threatens cardiac rhythm. EKG shows peaked T waves, widened QRS, absent P waves. TREAT IMMEDIATELY: Calcium gluconate (stabilize heart) → insulin + glucose + β-agonists (shift K into cells) → dialysis if renal failure.
ConditionEKG ChangesTreatment
Hypokalemia (<3.5)Flattened T, U waves; prolonged QTKCl PO or IV (max 10 mEq/hr IV)
Hyperkalemia (>5.5, especially >6.5)Peaked T, widened QRS, absent PCa gluconate IV → insulin+glucose → β-agonists → diuretics → dialysis

Calcium Imbalances (Ca)

  • Hypocalcemia (<8.5): Tetany, paresthesias, muscle cramps, seizures. Causes: hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, renal/liver failure, massive transfusion (citrate binds Ca).
  • Hypercalcemia (>10.5): "Stones, bones, groans, and psychiatric overtones." Nephrolithiasis, bone pain, GI upset, confusion, arrhythmias. Causes: hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, vitamin D toxicity.
  • Always check ionized calcium (free Ca2+); total Ca can be falsely low if albumin is low.

Fluid Volume Imbalances

HYPOVOLEMIA (Dehydration): ↓ BP, ↑ HR, ↑ BUN/Cr ratio, thirst. HYPERVOLEMIA (Overload): Edema, rales, JVD, weight gain. EUVOLEMIA: Normal fluid status — hyponatremia here means dilutional (SIADH, excessive water).
  • Third spacing: Fluid shifts from intravascular to interstitial (burns, sepsis, liver disease) → hypovolemia despite fluid intake.
  • Assess fluid status: Skin turgor, mucous membranes, orthostatic vitals, CVP, daily weights, I&Os.
  • Maintenance fluids: ~30 mL/kg/day for average adult. Adjust for losses (GI, urine, insensible).

Study Flashcards

What is the most serious complication of severe hyponatremia (<120)?
Cerebral edema and seizures. Correct slowly at 8 mEq/L per 24 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination.
EKG shows peaked T waves and widened QRS. What electrolyte abnormality?
Hyperkalemia (>5.5). This is a cardiac emergency. Treat with calcium gluconate immediately.
"Stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones." What condition?
Hypercalcemia. Nephrolithiasis, bone pain, GI upset, and mental status changes.
How do you distinguish hypovolemic vs. euvolemic hyponatremia?
Check fluid status (BP, HR, skin turgor, JVD). Hypovolemic = signs of dehydration. Euvolemic = normal fluid status.
A patient on furosemide develops hypokalemia. First treatment?
KCl PO (preferred) or IV. Max IV rate is 10 mEq/hr in peripheral line to avoid phlebitis.
