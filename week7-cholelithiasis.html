Cholelithiasis & Cholecystitis

← Back to Week 7 Study Guide

Gallstones and gallbladder inflammation: risk factors, manifestations, obstructive jaundice, non-surgical and surgical care, post-op teaching, and the T-tube.

Cholelithiasis vs Cholecystitis

Cholelithiasis

  • Gallstones in the gallbladder
  • Becomes a problem when the gallbladder gets inflamed or a stone blocks a duct

Cholecystitis

  • Inflammation of the gallbladder
  • Acute and chronic forms

Risk factors

  • Middle-aged female
  • Fair skin
  • Overweight with a high-fat diet
  • Oral contraceptive use

Clinical Manifestations

  • Episodic, vague upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder (visceral pain)
  • Pain triggered by high-fat or large meals — they make the gallbladder contract harder
  • Nausea, vomiting, dyspepsia (indigestion), eructation (belching), flatulence (gas)
  • May be febrile with inflammation
  • Jaundice, clay-colored stools, dark urine, steatorrhea — most common with chronic cholecystitis
Obstructive jaundice: a stone blocks the common bile duct. The liver still conjugates bilirubin, but it can't reach the GI tract. Result: clay-colored stool (no bilirubin in it), dark urine (bilirubin leaves through the kidneys), yellow skin.

Collaborative Care

Non-surgical

  • Avoid fatty foods
  • NPO during a major flare — rest the gut
  • Opioids for biliary pain, antiemetics, antispasmodics
  • Lithotripsy — rarely used
  • ERCP with sphincterotomy — the sphincter is opened wider so stones can pass; not common

Surgical

ProcedureKey points
Laparoscopic cholecystectomy ("lap chole") — most commonInstruments and a camera enter through small punctures; the peritoneum is inflated with air to see. No large incision — just small bandaged sites
Open cholecystectomySubcostal incision; heals by primary intention; may or may not have a T-tube

Post-op care: lap chole

  • Remove puncture-site bandages the day after surgery; then the patient may shower
  • Report: redness, swelling, purulent or bile-colored drainage, increasing pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, chills
  • Increase activity gradually; back to work in about 1 week
  • Low-fat diet is tolerated best for the first few weeks, then a regular diet once the body adapts

T-tube

  • T-shaped tube placed in the common bile duct
  • Only purpose: keep the common bile duct patent until inflammation goes down
  • Drains to a bile bag
  • Occasionally the patient goes home with it and must learn to empty the bile bag
After a cholecystectomy, these are NOT expected: bile-colored or purulent drainage, redness or swelling at the sites, increasing pain, nausea/vomiting, fever, or chills. Teach the patient to notify the provider.

Self-Test Flashcards

What is the difference between cholelithiasis and cholecystitis?
Cholelithiasis = gallstones in the gallbladder. Cholecystitis = inflammation of the gallbladder.
Who is at highest risk for gallstones?
A middle-aged, fair-skinned woman who is overweight, eats a high-fat diet, or takes oral contraceptives.
After a large, fatty meal, a client has upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder. Why does the meal trigger it?
High-fat or high-volume meals make the gallbladder contract harder, causing biliary (visceral) pain.
Define dyspepsia, eructation, and flatulence.
Indigestion, belching, and gas — all common with gallbladder disease.
A client with gallstones has clay-colored stools, dark urine, and yellow skin. What is happening?
Obstructive jaundice: a stone is blocking the common bile duct, so bilirubin can't reach the GI tract and is excreted in the urine instead.
Why are the stools clay-colored in obstructive jaundice?
Bilirubin gives stool its brown color, and it isn't reaching the intestines.
A client is admitted with an acute cholecystitis flare. Which orders should the nurse anticipate?
NPO to rest the gut, opioids for pain, antiemetics, and antispasmodics.
What is the most common surgical treatment for gallbladder disease?
Laparoscopic cholecystectomy.
What discharge teaching follows a lap chole?
Remove bandages the day after surgery, then shower; increase activity gradually; return to work in about a week; low-fat diet for the first few weeks.
Which findings should a client report after a cholecystectomy?
Redness, swelling, purulent or bile-colored drainage, increasing pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, or chills.
What is the purpose of a T-tube?
To keep the common bile duct open (patent) until inflammation goes down; it drains into a bile bag.
Can a client eat a regular diet without a gallbladder?
Yes, eventually. A low-fat diet is better tolerated for the first few weeks, then a regular diet once the body adapts.
