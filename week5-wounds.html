Major Wound Complications

Hemorrhage and Hematoma

Overview and Pathophysiology

  • Timing: Greatest risk is up to 48 hours post-injury or post-surgery; highest concern in immediate post-operative period
  • Causes: Clot coming loose, sutures slipping, blood vessel damage from surgical trauma, inadequate hemostasis during closure
  • Internal Bleeding Presentation: Swelling and distension in the surgical area, sanguineous (bloody) drainage on dressings
  • Vital Sign Changes: Initial increase in heart rate (compensatory mechanism to maintain stroke volume), tachycardia is earliest sign
  • Hematoma: Local collection of blood appearing as blue or red bruise; may be self-limiting or require drainage
  • Severity Assessment: Monitor ongoing bleeding, assess for signs of hypovolemic shock (decreased BP, increased HR, decreased urine output)
🚨 EMERGENCY ALERT: Severe hemorrhage requires immediate direct pressure to wound and provider notification. Be prepared for return to OR for surgical hemostasis.

Dehiscence and Evisceration

Dehiscence: Wound Separation

  • Definition: Separation of wound layers, usually affects few underlying skin layers; appears as wound pulling apart/gap opening
  • Presentation: Visible separation along surgical line, increase in sanguineous or serosanguineous drainage, possible drainage on dressings
  • Common Timeline: Often occurs 5-8 days post-op as initial inflammation resolves and before adequate collagen deposition
  • Risk Factors for Dehiscence: Advanced age, obesity, abdominal cancer, infection present at time of surgery, vomiting, poor wound care
  • Additional Risk Factors: Inadequate suturing technique, dehydration, malnutrition, immunosuppression, anything causing increased abdominal pressure

Evisceration: EMERGENCY Complication

  • Definition: Protrusion of internal organs/viscera through the wound opening; most commonly occurs with abdominal incisions
  • Clinical Presentation: Significant increase in flow of sanguineous or serosanguineous fluid from wound, patient reports feeling something "pop" or "give way" during activity
  • Visual Findings: Actual visualization of abdominal contents (bowel loops, omentum, etc.) protruding through wound; clear indication of organ displacement
  • Precipitating Events: Sudden straining (getting up, coughing, sneezing, vomiting, hiccuping, straining for bowel movements), increased abdominal pressure
  • Critical Timing: This is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY requiring immediate operative intervention; patient will almost certainly require return to surgery
🚨 EVISCERATION NURSING MANAGEMENT:
  • Immediately notify provider — patient needs emergency surgery
  • Stay with patient — provide reassurance and keep calm
  • Cover wound and organs with sterile dressings soaked in sterile normal saline (MUST memorize for exams)
  • DO NOT attempt to reinsert organs — risk of contamination and tissue damage
  • Reposition patient: Bend hips and knees to reduce tension/stretch on abdominal wound
  • Keep patient NPO — surgery is highly likely; also maintain calm environment
  • Monitor vitals closely — watch for signs of shock, maintain IV access, prepare for operating room

Wound Infection and Sepsis

Infection Development and Assessment

  • Critical Timing: Infection does NOT develop within first 24-48 hours; manifestations typically appear 2-11 days post-surgery/injury
  • Why This Matters: Low-grade fever in first 24-48 hours is not infection; low post-op fever is normal response to tissue trauma; allows adequate time for bacterial colonization and inflammatory response
  • Risk Factors for Infection: Impaired circulation (PVD, diabetes with poor perfusion), malnutrition, chronic disease (diabetes, renal disease), poor wound care, contaminated wound
  • Key Manifestations: Erythema (redness) and swelling around wound, purulent (pus-like) drainage with possible odor/malodorous smell, pain/tenderness increasing
  • Systemic Signs: Fever and chills, elevated vital signs (HR, RR, BP may increase), leukocytosis (elevated WBC count indicating infection fight)
  • Drainage Assessment: Change from normal serous/sanguineous to purulent indicates bacterial colonization and active infection

Infection Prevention and Treatment

  • Meticulous Aseptic Technique: "Asepsis is an absolute" — either sterile or not sterile, no gray area in wound care
  • Nutritional Optimization: Ensure adequate protein, vitamin C, vitamin A, zinc, calories — nutrition enhances immune function and collagen synthesis
  • Adequate Rest: Sleep and rest are critical for immune function; educate patient on importance
  • Antibiotic Administration: Draw cultures and sensitivities BEFORE starting antibiotics when possible; if empiric treatment needed, start broad-spectrum then tailor after C&S results
  • Culture-Driven Therapy: Once C&S results return, switch from broad-spectrum to organism-specific antibiotic to reduce resistance and side effects
  • Wound Care Specialists: Wound care nurses exist in hospitals — they are wealth of knowledge on wound management and should be consulted/followed when available

Necrotizing Fasciitis

Rapidly Progressive Deep Infection

  • Definition: Acute, rapidly spreading, potentially life-threatening infection of the deep fascial layers beneath muscles; often polymicrobial
  • Common Causative Organisms: Group A Streptococcus (beta-hemolytic), Clostridium perfringens (gas-forming), often polymicrobial with aerobic and anaerobic bacteria
  • Clinical Presentation: Severe pain out of proportion to visible wound findings (hallmark sign), rapid spread beyond apparent wound borders, systemic toxicity
  • Physical Findings: Erythema and swelling that progresses rapidly, skin may develop purple/black discoloration (necrosis), possible crepitus (air/gas in tissues), bullae formation
  • Systemic Signs: High fever, severe pain, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status, possible septic shock
  • Critical Diagnosis: High mortality rate (10-40%); requires immediate surgical intervention with aggressive debridement of necrotic tissue
  • Management: Multiple debridements often necessary (may need 2-3+ return visits to OR), IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, aggressive fluid resuscitation, ICU-level care

Compartment Syndrome

Pressure-Related Tissue Damage

  • Pathophysiology: Increased pressure within a closed compartment (fascial space) compromises microvascular perfusion, leading to ischemia and tissue necrosis
  • Causes: Tight dressings/casts, extensive edema from trauma/inflammation, crush injuries, vascular injury with bleeding into compartment, electrical burns
  • Classic Signs (5 P's): Pain (especially with passive stretching of muscles in compartment — HALLMARK), Pressure (tense compartment), Pallor, Pulselessness (late finding), Paresthesias, Paralysis (very late)
  • Critical Finding: Pain with passive stretching of fingers/toes is earliest and most sensitive sign; do not wait for other signs
  • Diagnosis: Compartment pressure measurement (>30 mmHg or within 30 of diastolic BP indicates emergency); clinical suspicion often sufficient to warrant surgery
  • Management: Remove constrictive dressings/casts immediately; if suspected, urgent fasciotomy required to decompress compartment and restore perfusion
  • Timing: Tissue damage is irreversible after 6-8 hours of ischemia; early recognition and fasciotomy are critical for limb salvage

Scarring and Contractures

Hypertrophic and Keloid Scarring

  • Hypertrophic Scars: Excessive collagen deposition within wound boundaries; appears raised and discolored; may limit ROM if over joints
  • Keloid Scars: Collagen extends beyond original wound borders; more common in darker skin tones; can become disfiguring; painful and itchy
  • Prevention Strategies: Minimize tension on wound (proper closure technique, avoid excessive movement early), keep wound clean, UV protection, pressure garments (12+ hours daily for 12-18 months)
  • Management: Silicone gel sheeting, pressure garments, steroid injections (for keloids), possible surgical revision, laser therapy
  • Contractures: Myofibroblast contraction of wound during healing phase; normal up to point but can limit ROM if excessive; PT/OT for ROM exercises
