Acute Wound Complications

Hemorrhage and Hematoma

BLEEDING (Hemorrhage): Bright red drainage persisting >48-72 hours post-wound. Assess for vessel bleeding vs. capillary ooze (usually stops with pressure). Manage: Direct pressure (15-20 min continuous), elevation, ice if appropriate, check coagulation studies (PT/INR, PTT), notify provider if bleeding continues. Risk of anemia/hypovolemia.
HEMATOMA: Localized collection of blood under wound edges; appears as purple/blue swelling. Small hematomas reabsorb; large ones risk infection and may require aspiration. Assess trending size; report if enlarging or fluctuant.

Dehiscence and Evisceration

Partial and Full Thickness Separation

  • Dehiscence: Separation of wound edges without evisceration (superficial layers separate). Usually happens days 5-8 post-op when type III collagen weak. Increased serosanguineous drainage suddenly.
  • Evisceration: EMERGENCY - full thickness separation with protrusion of internal organs (bowel, abdominal contents). Dramatic and frightening; high risk of infection and shock.
  • Risk Factors: Poor nutrition, coughing/straining, infection, obesity, advanced age, diabetes, steroid use, inadequate closure technique.
  • Nursing Management of Dehiscence: Notify provider immediately; may require re-suturing; prevent contamination; elevate HOB if abdominal; restrict activity; teach splinting during cough.
  • Nursing Management of Evisceration: EMERGENCY - cover WITH STERILE SALINE GAUZE (not dry), position supine with knees bent (decrease abdominal tension), NPO, establish IV access, prepare for emergency surgery, keep patient calm, monitor vitals closely.

Infection and Sepsis

Signs of Wound Infection and Systemic Response

  • Local Signs (3-5 days post-wound): Purulent drainage (thick, yellow/green), foul odor, increased pain/tenderness, erythema extending >2cm, warmth, edema, fever may occur.
  • Systemic Signs of Sepsis: Fever (>38.5°C), tachycardia (>90), tachypnea (>20), mental status changes, hypotension, elevated WBC/left shift, elevated lactate, positive blood cultures.
  • Management: Obtain wound culture before antibiotics; start broad-spectrum antibiotics promptly; increase dressing changes; consider debridement; monitor for septic shock.

Necrotizing Fasciitis

NECROTIZING FASCIITIS (FLESH-EATING DISEASE): Rapidly spreading deep tissue infection; polymicrobial. EMERGENCY presentation: Severe pain OUT OF PROPORTION to wound appearance, edema extending beyond erythema, crepitus (gas in tissues), hemorrhagic bullae, shock, multi-organ failure. Requires IMMEDIATE aggressive debridement, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, ICU monitoring. Mortality 20-40%. Early recognition critical.

Compartment Syndrome

Crushing Injury and Increased Pressure

  • The 5 P's: Pain (out of proportion, severe), Pressure (tense compartment), Paresthesias (tingling), Pallor (pale/dusky), Pulselessness (LATE).
  • Early Recognition Critical: Pain out of proportion = RED FLAG; don't wait for late signs (pulselessness indicates muscle necrosis already occurring).
  • Nursing Management: Elevate to heart level (NOT above), remove constrictive dressings/casts, notify provider URGENTLY, assess distal perfusion, administer analgesia, prepare for emergency fasciotomy.
  • Prevention: Avoid circumferential tight dressings, monitor for risk (crush injury, burns, fractures), educate patient on signs.

Scarring and Contractures

Abnormal Tissue Response and Prevention

  • Hypertrophic Scar: Excessive collagen within wound boundaries; raised, firm, red/purple, itchy; may regress slightly over time.
  • Keloid Scar: Collagen extends BEYOND original wound; grows larger over time; permanent; higher incidence in darker skin; very itchy.
  • Contracture: Shortening of scar tissue from myofibroblast contraction; results in joint limitation; preventable with early ROM exercises.
  • Prevention: Pressure garments (12+ hours daily for 12-18 months), ROM exercises, silicone sheeting, sun protection, scar massage, avoid secondary trauma.
  • Treatment: Topical silicone, compression garments, steroid injections, laser therapy, surgical revision (after 12-18 months).
