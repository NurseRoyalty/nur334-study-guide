Wound Healing

Comprehensive coverage of wound assessment, healing phases, dressing selection, and complication management.

Wound Healing Phases

Three Phases of Wound Healing

PhaseTimelineCellular ActivityNursing Actions
Hemostasis/Inflammatory0-4 daysPlatelets aggregate, coagulation cascade, neutrophils infiltrate, macrophages remove debris, cytokines releasedControl bleeding, assess for infection signs, monitor pain, promote rest and elevation
Proliferative4-21 daysCollagen (Type III) deposition, angiogenesis (new blood vessels), fibroblasts active, epithelialization, granulation tissue formsMaintain moist wound environment, prevent infection, promote nutrition (protein, vitamin C), assess for signs of healing
Maturation/Remodeling21 days-2 yearsCollagen Type III converts to Type I, cross-linking and reorganization, wound strengthens, scar remodeling, angiogenesis decreasesMonitor for contractures, manage scar appearance (massage, pressure garments), continue PT/OT, support psychosocial adjustment

Wound Strength Progression

Percentage of Tensile Strength Over Time

  • 1 Week: ~10% of pre-wound strength; still very fragile
  • 3 Weeks: ~25% of strength; sutures often removed but wound still vulnerable
  • 1 Month: ~50% of strength; continuing collagen deposition
  • 2-3 Months: ~70-80% of strength
  • 6 Months: ~90% of strength; plateau thereafter (never 100%)
  • Clinical Implications: Avoid heavy lifting/strenuous activity during early healing; scar tissue is more fragile than original tissue

Factors Affecting Wound Healing

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Factors That Impair Healing

Age and Physiologic Changes

  • Elderly Patients: Skin becomes thinner and more fragile; collagen production decreases; inflammatory response diminished; wound tensile strength develops more slowly; healing timeline extends significantly
  • Pediatric Patients: Generally heal faster due to better circulation and robust immune response; however, thicker scarring possible

Circulation and Perfusion

  • Peripheral Vascular Disease: Reduced blood flow to wound → decreased oxygen delivery → prolonged healing, increased infection risk
  • Venous Insufficiency/Edema: Fluid accumulation prevents normal tissue perfusion; chronic venous insufficiency wounds notoriously difficult to heal
  • Positioning: Elevation promotes venous return and decreases edema; proper positioning critical for healing

Medications and Medical Conditions

  • Steroids: Suppress inflammatory response and collagen production; impair immune function; even topical steroids can affect healing
  • Anticoagulants: Increase bleeding risk; may complicate hemostasis during initial phase
  • NSAIDs: Interfere with inflammatory phase; may delay collagen deposition
  • Diabetes: Hyperglycemia impairs immune function and collagen synthesis; increased infection risk
  • Renal Disease: Uremic toxins and electrolyte abnormalities impair healing; anemia reduces oxygen capacity
  • Malignancy: Direct effect of cancer and chemotherapy on healing; protein depletion
  • Immunosuppression (HIV, chemotherapy, transplant meds): Impaired immune response prolongs inflammatory phase, increases infection risk

Lifestyle and Habits

  • Smoking: Nicotine causes vasoconstriction reducing oxygen delivery; carbon monoxide decreases hemoglobin's oxygen-carrying capacity; major risk factor for healing complications
  • Nutrition Deficiencies: Protein (collagen synthesis), Vitamin C (cross-linking of collagen), Vitamin A (epithelialization), Zinc (enzyme cofactor); deficiencies dramatically slow healing
  • Alcohol Use Disorder: Malnutrition, liver disease, impaired immune function, poor wound care compliance

Wound Assessment & Measurement

Comprehensive Wound Evaluation Technique

Systematic Assessment Parameters

  • Location: Body area affected; affects blood supply (face heals faster, sacrum slower), healing rate, infection risk, functional outcome
  • Dimensions: Length × Width × Depth in centimeters; measure at deepest point; trendy with photography at each assessment to objectively track healing
  • Color Assessment (Critical for Wound Status): RED = healthy granulation tissue (good blood supply, appropriate healing); YELLOW = slough/fibrin (dead tissue, may need debridement); BLACK = eschar/full-thickness necrotic tissue (must be debrided); PALE/WHITE = poor perfusion (may indicate vascular compromise)
  • Edges: Well-approximated (good for healing) vs. rolled/inverted edges (delays healing, may need surgical revision) vs. undermined (tissue extends beyond visible wound, indicates deeper infection/poor healing)
  • Base Tissue Composition: Estimate percentage of granulation tissue, slough, eschar; affects treatment approach
  • Drainage Assessment: Type (serous vs. sanguineous vs. purulent), amount (scant <5mL, small 5-10mL, moderate 10-25mL, large >25mL/8-hour shift), color, odor, consistency
  • Surrounding Skin: Erythema (may indicate infection), induration (tissue firmness), maceration (white, waterlogged tissue from moisture), dryness (may need different dressing)
  • Tunneling/Undermining: Use sterile cotton-tipped applicator or probe; gently explore wound margins; note direction and depth; indicates deeper infection tract or poor healing; requires different management (cavity dressings)
  • Pain Assessment: Rate pain on scale; note if pain increases with dressing changes (may need pre-medication or different dressing choice)
  • Signs of Infection: Increased purulent drainage, erythema, warmth, odor, pain, fever, elevated WBC; culture if suspected

Wound Dressing Types & Selection

Dressing Characteristics and Use

Modern Moist Wound Healing Dressings

  • Transparent Film (Tegaderm): Allows visualization, maintains moisture, for clean/dry wounds; not for infected/draining wounds
  • Hydrocolloid (DuoDerm): Absorbs moderate drainage, maintains moisture, promotes autolytic debridement, provides insulation for comfort
  • Hydrogel (AquaForm): Maintains moisture, promotes debridement, for dry wounds and burns; absorbs minimal drainage
  • Foam Dressings (Lyofoam): Absorbs moderate-to-heavy drainage, provides cushioning, maintains moisture, good for pressure ulcers
  • Alginate (Kaltostat): Absorbs heavy drainage, promotes hemostasis (calcium alginate), converts to gel; for draining wounds, cavity wounds
  • Collagen Dressings (Biostep): Promotes collagen synthesis, provides scaffold for healing, for chronic wounds and burns
  • Vacuum-Assisted Closure (VAC): Negative pressure therapy, removes exudate, promotes angiogenesis, for large/complex wounds, grafts
  • Antimicrobial Dressings: Silver (Silvadine), iodine (Iodoform) for infected wounds; use short-term as bacteria can develop resistance

Dressing Selection Principles and Clinical Decision-Making

Choosing the Right Dressing

  • Wound Assessment First: Type of wound (acute vs chronic), location, drainage amount, presence of infection guides selection; reassess frequently
  • Moist Wound Environment (Evidence-Based): Modern principle; accelerates healing up to 2x faster vs. traditional wet-to-dry dressings; promotes autolytic debridement (body's own enzymes remove dead tissue); reduces pain; improves epithelialization
  • Wet-to-Dry Dressings (OUTDATED): Was standard for infected wounds; now recognized as traumatizing to healing tissue; damages new granulation and epithelial cells with each change; increases pain; NOT recommended for new wounds unless specific institutional protocol
  • Frequency of Changes: Transparent film/hydrocolloid q2-3 days; foam/alginate daily or per drainage (change when saturated); VAC per protocol (typically q3 days if clean, more often if infected)
  • Cost-Benefit Analysis: Modern dressings more expensive upfront but reduce overall time-to-healing, fewer dressing changes, less nursing time; cost-effective despite higher unit cost
  • Patient Comfort and Compliance: Minimize pain with dressing changes; choose non-adherent options when possible; assess for allergies (iodine, latex, adhesives); patient compliance improves with less painful care

Pain Management During Wound Care

Analgesia and Comfort Strategies for Dressing Changes

Pharmacologic and Non-Pharmacologic Approaches

  • Timing of Medications: Pre-medicate 30-45 minutes BEFORE dressing change when using opioids (allows peak effect); shorter interval for fast-acting agents
  • Opioid Selection: Use appropriate analgesics for moderate-to-severe pain; dressing changes can be extremely painful; dose adequately
  • Anxiolytics: Benzodiazepines (midazolam) useful for patients with anxiety about wound care; reduces anticipatory pain
  • Nitrous Oxide: Rapid onset, useful for quick procedures; dissociative effect helps with anxiety
  • Topical Anesthetics: Lidocaine spray or cream on smaller wounds; useful for procedures like wound cleaning or suture removal
  • Non-Pharmacologic Strategies: Distraction (music, TV, conversation), relaxation techniques, positioning comfort, warm normal saline for cleansing (vs. cold saline), gentle technique to minimize trauma
  • Dressing Selection Impact: Choose non-adherent dressings to reduce pain with removal; some patients tolerate certain dressings better than others

Drain and Tubing Assessment

Monitoring Drains, Jackson-Pratt Tubes, and Output Management

Drain Management Principles

  • Purpose of Drains: Remove fluid collection (seroma, hematoma) that could compromise healing; prevent abscess formation; maintain wound apposition
  • Output Monitoring: Measure amount each shift; note color, consistency, odor; excessive output (>100 mL/8 hours) may indicate bleeding or infection; decreasing output indicates ready for removal
  • Jackson-Pratt (JP) Drains: Passive gravity drain; keep bulb compressed (creates suction) for optimal drainage; note if drain becomes clogged (output stops) and notify provider
  • Penrose Drains: Passive gravity drainage; keep sterile gauze around drain to prevent skin damage from drainage
  • Drain Placement Assessment: Ensure drain enters at lowest point of wound; verify it hasn't migrated (shifted position); assess surrounding skin for signs of irritation or infection
  • Drain Removal Criteria: Typically removed when output <25-30 mL per 24 hours and clear/serous (not purulent); provider order required; patient taught about increased monitoring for seroma after removal
  • Complications: Monitor for obstruction (no drainage when there should be), erosion through skin, infection (purulent output, fever)

Suture and Staple Removal Timing

Location-Based Removal Schedules

Optimal Timing for Suture/Staple Removal

  • Face/Scalp: 3-5 days (excellent blood supply, heals quickly, cosmesis important)
  • Trunk: 7-10 days (intermediate healing, under tension)
  • Extremities/Joints: 10-14 days (slower healing, higher tension/movement)
  • Over Joints: 14 days minimum (movement interferes with healing; remove late to maintain strength)
  • Principles: Never remove sutures early (wound will dehisce); assess wound integrity before removal; if dehiscent, notify provider before removal; approximately 50% of wound strength at removal time
  • Technique: Clean suture line with sterile technique; cut suture at skin level and pull gently to remove; if resistance, do not force (may indicate inadequate healing); use suture removal kit (special scissors and tweezers)

Wound Complications & Prevention

Common Complications and Nursing Management

INFECTION/SEPSIS: Increased purulent drainage, erythema, warmth, odor, fever, elevated WBC. Culture wound; start antibiotics empirically; escalate care for systemic signs.
DEHISCENCE vs EVISCERATION: Dehiscence = wound separation (outer layers); Evisceration = organs protrude through wound (EMERGENCY—cover, NPO, OR). Both risk infection.
HYPERTROPHIC SCARS: Raised scars within wound boundaries; pressure garments, massage, silicone gel, possible steroid injection.
KELOID SCARS: Extend beyond original wound; permanent; more common in darker skin; difficult to treat (steroid injection, cryotherapy, laser).

Nutritional Support for Wound Healing

Specific Nutritional Requirements to Optimize Healing

Critical Nutrients and Healing Impact

  • Protein (PRIMARY): Essential for collagen synthesis; requirement increases with wound size; goal 1.5-2 g/kg/day for healing wounds (vs. 0.8 g/kg maintenance); insufficient protein dramatically delays healing
  • Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid): Essential cofactor for collagen cross-linking; required for immune function; deficiency causes wound reopening; goal 500-1000 mg daily during active healing phase
  • Vitamin A: Promotes epithelialization; required for immune function; goal 10,000-15,000 IU daily during healing phase; caution with excessive doses (toxicity risk)
  • Zinc: Enzyme cofactor for collagen synthesis and immune function; deficiency impairs healing; goal 15-30 mg daily; found in protein sources (meat, poultry, legumes, nuts)
  • Calories: Increased metabolic demand for healing; may need 1.5-2x maintenance calories depending on wound size
  • B Vitamins: Coenzymes in metabolism; support immune function
  • Hydration: Critical for all metabolic processes; adequate fluid intake essential
  • Nutritional Assessment: Screen at admission and weekly; consider supplements if intake inadequate; dietitian consult for complex cases

Wound Cleanliness Categories and Infection Risk

Surgical Wound Classification and Implications

Clean vs. Contaminated vs. Infected Wound Management

  • Clean Wounds: Elective surgery, untraumatic, no break in sterile technique, no infection present; low infection risk (<2%); primary closure, normal healing expected
  • Clean-Contaminated Wounds: Minor break in sterile technique, controlled surgical procedure with minor trauma; higher infection risk; may require prophylactic antibiotics; monitor closely
  • Contaminated Wounds: Significant break in sterile technique, traumatic wounds <12 hours old, gross spillage of GI contents; infection risk 10-15%; prophylactic antibiotics recommended
  • Infected Wounds: Visibly infected at time of assessment; purulent drainage, signs of active infection; require culture, systemic antibiotics, possible surgical intervention
  • Nursing Implications: Cleaner wounds can use different care protocols than contaminated wounds; infection prevention strategies escalate with contamination level
