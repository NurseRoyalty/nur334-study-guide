Colorectal Cancer

A common cancer in med-surg, and it is increasingly showing up in younger people.

Risk factors

  • Diet high in red or processed meat; low in fruits and vegetables
  • Overweight and inactive
  • Alcohol; smoking
  • History of inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's or UC)
  • Genetics / family history

Manifestations

  • Usually insidious — often not apparent until advanced
  • Anemia (low H&H found for another reason)
  • Rectal bleeding, abdominal pain
  • Change in bowel habits
  • Can cause intestinal obstruction or perforation
Tumor locationWhat you'd expect (think it through — don't memorize)
RectumRectal discomfort; blood in the stool
Descending (left) colonChange in bowel habits; bright red blood (still fresh)
Ascending (right) colonPain, change in bowel habits — can be more subtle (e.g., anemia; a slowly falling H&H)

Know the anatomy by quadrant: the ascending colon is on the patient's right, the descending colon on the left.

Screening and Diagnosis

  • Regular screening and removal of pre-cancerous polyps
  • Colonoscopy is the gold standard: average risk → start at 45, every 10 years; higher risk (strong family history, IBD) → earlier and more often
  • Fecal occult blood / stool DNA test (e.g., Cologuard): less favorable than colonoscopy but acceptable if it's all the patient will do; done yearly

Collaborative Care: Surgery

First, surgery removes the cancer. Whether the patient needs a colostomy depends on the situation. Radiation and chemotherapy are also used (covered in oncology).

Bowel Ostomies

Location determines output. Ileostomy (end of the ileum, right lower quadrant) → very liquid, continuous, caustic to skin (no water reabsorbed yet). Sigmoid/descending colostomy (left lower quadrant) → formed, close to a normal bowel movement. The farther along the colon, the more formed the stool.

Ostomies can be temporary or permanent — know which one your patient has before you go in.

Colostomy irrigation

  • For colostomies near the rectum with solid stool
  • Like a small enema given through the stoma
  • Every day or two, or per the patient's bowel pattern; a large bag collects the return
  • Helps keep the patient regular

Stoma care

  • Assess stoma color, swelling, bleeding
  • Wash surrounding skin with mild soap and warm water, then apply a skin barrier and pouch
  • Cut the wafer to the exact size of the stoma — no skin exposed to caustic drainage
  • Empty the pouch often; vent gas (there's no sphincter)
  • Get the ostomy nurse involved — a fantastic resource

Psychosocial and practical points

  • With a double-barrel stoma, old stool and mucus may still pass from the anus — expected
  • Odor, gas, and output changes are key teaching points; control your facial expressions — the patient is embarrassed
  • Ostomy surgery is emotionally devastating, especially when permanent. Teaching may go slowly, and it isn't unusual for a patient to not want to look at the stoma at discharge — plan follow-up and support
Report after colon/ostomy surgery: stoma color changes, new drainage or wound opening, no stool or gas from the stoma or rectum for more than 24 hours, high ostomy output with dark or no urine, fever, vomiting, or increasing pain.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

A client asks when to start colorectal cancer screening. They have no special risk factors. What should the nurse say?
Colonoscopy starting at 45, repeated every 10 years. Earlier and more often with a strong family history or IBD.
A client refuses a colonoscopy. What is an acceptable alternative, and how often?
A fecal occult blood/stool DNA test (e.g., Cologuard) every year — less favorable but acceptable.
Which risk factors for colorectal cancer came up in lecture?
Diet high in red/processed meat, low fruits and vegetables, overweight and inactive, alcohol, smoking, IBD history, and genetics.
Why is colorectal cancer often found late?
It's insidious. It may show up only as anemia (falling H&H), bleeding, pain, or a change in bowel habits once advanced.
How does an AP resection differ from a low anterior resection?
AP resection removes the rectum and leaves a permanent colostomy. LAR preserves the sphincter; after a temporary colostomy heals, the colon is re-anastomosed and normal defecation resumes.
What is a Hartmann's pouch?
The distal bowel segment sewn closed and left inside, making a later takedown (re-anastomosis) possible.
A stoma is in the right lower quadrant. What output should the nurse expect?
An ileostomy: very liquid, near-continuous, and caustic to the skin.
A stoma is in the left lower quadrant. What output should the nurse expect?
A descending/sigmoid colostomy: formed stool, close to a normal bowel movement.
In a loop or double-barrel colostomy, what comes out of each opening?
Proximal opening = stool; distal opening = mucus (mucus fistula).
What holds a loop stoma in place?
A small plastic rod under the loop of bowel.
Which stoma type is two completely separate stomas?
A double-barrel stoma — proximal (stool) and distal (mucus fistula), with no rod.
Why is ileostomy output so hard on the skin?
No water has been reabsorbed yet (that happens in the colon), so it's liquid and caustic.
How should the nurse size the ostomy wafer?
Cut it to the exact size of the stoma so no skin is exposed to drainage.
What should the nurse assess on every stoma?
Color, swelling, and bleeding.
Who is colostomy irrigation for?
Clients with a colostomy near the rectum (formed stool). It works like an enema through the stoma to keep the bowel regular.
A client with a new double-barrel colostomy passes mucus from the rectum. Is this expected?
Yes — old stool and mucus can still pass from the anus.
A client being discharged still won't look at their new colostomy. How should the nurse respond?
This isn't unusual. Teaching may go slowly; arrange follow-up, the ostomy nurse, and support systems.
After colon surgery, what should the client report?
Stoma color changes, new drainage or wound opening, no stool/gas for more than 24 hours, high output with dark or no urine, fever, vomiting, or increasing pain.
