Burns

Comprehensive coverage of burn etiology, classification, Rule of Nines assessment, three phases of management, critical complications, and rehabilitation strategies for burn injury.

Types of Burns

Thermal Burns (Most Common)

Flame, Scalding, and Contact Burns

  • Thermal Burns (80% of all burns): Flame injury (most common cause of severe burns), scalding water/hot liquids, contact with hot surfaces; depth depends on temperature and exposure duration
  • Flame Burns: Typically result from house fires, explosions, occupational exposure; can involve clothing combustion and deeper tissue damage
  • Scalding Burns: Common in pediatric population; hot water/beverages; usually partial-thickness; depth depends on water temperature and duration
  • Contact Burns: Hot metal, cooking surfaces, cigarettes; usually circumscribed burns with clear demarcation
  • Clinical Pearl: Flash burns from rapid combustion produce superficial burns despite high temperature due to brief exposure

Chemical Burns

Acid vs. Alkali Mechanisms and Management

  • Alkali Burns (MORE DESTRUCTIVE): Penetrate deeper than acids; cause liquefactive necrosis (protein dissolution); damage continues over hours if not rinsed; examples: lye, ammonia, bleach
  • Acid Burns (LESS DESTRUCTIVE): Cause coagulative necrosis (protein precipitation forms eschar barrier); limits penetration; examples: hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid
  • Emergency Management: Immediate dilution with copious water for 15-20 minutes MINIMUM; remove contaminated clothing; never apply neutralizing agents (exothermic reaction); cover with dry dressing; systemic toxicity possible (cyanide from some compounds)
  • Common Industrial Chemicals: Hydrofluoric acid (HF) - systemic fluoride toxicity with cardiac dysrhythmias; phenol - CNS toxicity, cardiac dysrhythmias

Electrical Burns

Current Pathway and Internal Damage

  • Mechanism: Electrical current follows path of least resistance through body; creates entry AND exit wounds; internal damage along current path (muscle, organs, nerves)
  • Tissue Damage: Heat generation proportional to resistance; muscle tissue has high resistance → high heat → deep muscle necrosis (rhabdomyolysis) despite minimal skin injury
  • High-Voltage (>1000V): Severe internal injury, rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuria; Arc burns from contact across joints; compartment syndrome from muscle swelling
  • Low-Voltage (<1000V): Often household current; risk of cardiac dysrhythmias even with small contact area
  • Ventricular Fibrillation Risk: Most dangerous immediate complication; can occur with as little as 100mA of current; monitor continuously for dysrhythmias

Inhalation Injuries

Carbon Monoxide and Thermal Airway Injury

  • Carbon Monoxide (CO) Poisoning: CO binds hemoglobin with 200x greater affinity than oxygen → carboxyhemoglobin; patient appears pink/flushed (not cyanotic); headache, confusion, loss of consciousness
  • Carboxyhemoglobin Levels: <10%: mild toxicity; 10-20%: headache, dizziness; 20-40%: confusion, agitation; >40%: loss of consciousness, death possible
  • Management: High-flow oxygen immediately (FiO2 100%); hyperbaric oxygen if carboxyhemoglobin >10%; monitor carboxyhemoglobin levels
  • Thermal Airway Injury: Inhalation of superheated air causes thermal injury to upper/lower airways; edema develops gradually (peak at 24-48 hours); risk of upper airway obstruction, bronchospasm, pulmonary edema, ARDS
  • Smoke Inhalation: Toxic gases from incomplete combustion (cyanide, ammonia, acrolein); chemical injury to airways; parenchymal damage
  • ARDS Risk: Inhalation injury increases ARDS risk significantly; bilateral infiltrates, refractory hypoxemia; may require intubation and mechanical ventilation

Radiation and Frostbite Burns

UV, Ionizing Radiation, and Cold Injury

  • Radiation Burns: UV burns (sunburn) - superficial, painless initially; ionizing radiation (occupational/nuclear) - deep penetrating injury, delayed effect possible
  • Frostbite (Cold Injury): Vasoconstriction → tissue hypoxia; ice crystal formation damages cell membranes; occurs in extremities, nose, ears; staged by depth similar to burn classification (1st-4th degree)
  • Frostbite Management: Rapid rewarming (NOT massage - damages crystals); immersion in 40-42°C water; avoid re-freezing; monitor for compartment syndrome; tissue demarcation takes weeks

Re-epithelialization and Collagen Synthesis

Healing Mechanisms and Timeline

  • Partial-Thickness Healing: Re-epithelialization occurs from skin appendages (hair follicles, sweat glands) at margins; collagen in dermis provides structural support; can regenerate functionally intact skin
  • Full-Thickness Healing: No skin appendages remain; scarring inevitable; only healing possible is contraction (myofibroblasts pull edges together) or skin grafting; without graft, chronic wound results
  • Collagen Synthesis: Begins during proliferative phase (3-5 days post-burn); peak synthesis at 1-2 weeks; collagen type III (weak, more compliant) initially → gradually replaced by type I (stronger); remodeling continues for years

Rule of Nines for Body Surface Area Assessment

Rapid Severity Determination and Fluid Resuscitation Calculation

Adult Rule of Nines

  • Head/Neck: 9% (face 4.5%, posterior scalp 4.5%)
  • Each Arm: 9% (anterior 4.5%, posterior 4.5%); including hands
  • Trunk Anterior: 18% (chest 9%, abdomen 9%)
  • Trunk Posterior: 18% (upper back 9%, lower back 9%)
  • Each Leg: 18% (anterior 9%, posterior 9%); including foot
  • Perineum/Genitalia: 1%

Pediatric Modifications

Age-Specific Proportions

  • Head (Proportionally Larger): Infants/toddlers: 18% (vs. 9% adults); decreases to adult proportions by adolescence
  • Legs (Proportionally Smaller): Infants/toddlers: 13.5% each (vs. 18% adults)
  • Arms/Trunk: Similar to adults (9% and 18% respectively)
  • Clinical Use: Critical for accurate Parkland formula calculation; larger head burns in children require more aggressive fluid resuscitation

Burn Severity Classifications

American Burn Association Criteria

  • Major/Severe Burns: >20% TBSA (15% in elderly/children <10 years); all full-thickness >5%; inhalation injury; electrical burns; complicated by trauma; involves special areas (face, joints, genitals)
  • Moderate Burns: 10-20% TBSA partial-thickness; <10% full-thickness; no inhalation injury
  • Minor Burns: <10% TBSA partial-thickness; <2% full-thickness; can manage outpatient
  • Burn Center Referral Indicated: All major/severe burns; moderate burns in extremes of age; special considerations; circumferential burns

Three Phases of Burn Management

Emergent Phase (0-72 hours)

Burn Shock and Immediate Resuscitation

  • Pathophysiology of Burn Shock: Massive inflammatory response triggered by thermal injury; increased capillary permeability; fluid shifts from intravascular to interstitial space (edema); loss of plasma proteins; hypovolemia → decreased cardiac output → tissue hypoperfusion → shock
  • Fluid Shift Timing: Greatest fluid loss in first 8 hours; continues for 24-36 hours; peaks at 24 hours post-burn
  • Parkland Formula Fluid Resuscitation: 4 mL LR × %TBSA × weight(kg) = total LR in 24 hours; administer half in first 8 hours, half over next 16 hours; CRITICAL: adjust based on urine output (0.5 mL/kg/hr for adults, 1 mL/kg/hr for children)
  • Fluid Resuscitation Monitoring: Large-bore IV lines (2-3 lines); place Foley catheter for accurate urine measurement; vital signs q15 minutes initially; trending of vital signs critical; under-resuscitation → shock, organ failure; over-resuscitation → pulmonary edema, compartment syndrome
  • Escharotomy (Surgical Opening of Eschar): Performed during emergent phase if circumferential full-thickness burns present; incisions through eschar (not into viable tissue) to relieve constrictive eschar; prevents compartment syndrome, allows chest wall expansion, restores distal circulation
  • Fasciotomy (Deeper Incision): If escharotomy insufficient; incisions through fascia to reduce compartment pressure in muscle compartments; more invasive, higher morbidity
  • Inhalation Injury Management: High-flow oxygen (100% FiO2); aggressive airway management; intubation early if significant inhalation injury (stridor, carbonaceous sputum, singed nasal hairs); carboxyhemoglobin monitoring; hyperbaric oxygen if level >10%; mechanical ventilation with PEEP if ARDS develops
  • Tetanus Prophylaxis: All burn patients unless current; IV tetanus immune globulin if none previously; booster if >5 years since last

Acute Phase (72 hours to Weeks/Months)

Hypermetabolism, Wound Management, Grafting, and Infection Prevention

  • Hypermetabolism: Metabolic rate increases to 1.5-2x normal; persists until major wounds healed; fever, tachycardia, tachypnea; driven by inflammatory mediators and catecholamines
  • Nutritional Support: Massive caloric demands; high protein (1.5-2.5 g/kg) for collagen synthesis and immune function; high carbohydrates for energy; vitamins A, C, zinc essential for healing; often requires enteral feeding (TPN if unable to tolerate PO)
  • Burn Wound Infection Risk: Eschar becomes necrotic tissue → bacteria colonize → proliferate exponentially → invade into viable tissue (invasive burn wound infection) → bacteremia → systemic sepsis/septic shock → death (leading cause of burn mortality)
  • Infection Prevention Strategies: Meticulous aseptic technique with dressing changes; topical antimicrobials (silver sulfadiazine = Silvadene, silver nitrate, mafenide acetate = Sulfamylon); daily wound inspection for signs of invasive infection; blood cultures if fever/sepsis suspected; broad-spectrum antibiotics empirically if sepsis; surgical debridement of necrotic tissue
  • Topical Antimicrobial Agents: Silvadene (silver sulfadiazine) - most common, painless application, poor eschar penetration; Silver Nitrate - good penetration, stains everything black, electrolyte imbalance; Mafenide Acetate - excellent penetration, painful application, metabolic acidosis
  • Wound Debridement: Removal of necrotic tissue (eschar); promotes healing; can be mechanical (wet-to-dry dressings), enzymatic (collagenase), or surgical (most effective); surgical debridement often done in OR
  • Skin Grafting: Autograft (own skin, gold standard, no rejection) preferred; harvested from unburned areas (donor sites); takes 7-14 days to adhere; allograft (cadaver skin, temporary) or xenograft (pig skin) for temporary covering/biological dressing; homograft used until autografts available; skin grafts are fragile initially - careful wound care post-graft
  • Curling's Ulcer Prophylaxis: Stress ulcer from severe burn; acute gastric/duodenal ulceration; prophylaxis with H2 blockers (famotidine) or PPI (omeprazole); monitor for coffee-ground emesis, tarry stools, abdominal distention
  • Pain Management During Dressing Changes: Most painful part of burn care; pre-medicate 30-45 minutes before; use opioids, anxiolytics (benzodiazepines), nitrous oxide; consider topical anesthetics on smaller burns

Rehabilitation Phase (Weeks to Years)

Contracture Prevention, Scar Management, and Psychosocial Recovery

  • Contractures: Progressive shortening of scar tissue → joint contracture → permanent loss of function; prevented with range-of-motion exercises (active > passive), positioning to oppose contracture, splinting/orthoses during sleep and periods of inactivity
  • Hypertrophic Scarring: Excessive collagen deposition within wound boundaries (raised, firm, itchy); occurs in deep partial and full-thickness burns; managed with pressure garments (worn 12+ hours daily for 12-18 months), scar massage, silicone gel sheeting, sun avoidance, possible steroid injection, laser therapy, or surgical revision
  • Physical Therapy: ROM exercises to prevent contractures; strengthening; gradual return to ambulation; progression of activity; address functional deficits from graft sites and donor sites
  • Occupational Therapy: Activities of daily living retraining; hand function; self-care skills; return to work/school planning; adaptive equipment as needed
  • Psychological Impact: PTSD, depression, anxiety extremely common; body image disruption; social isolation; difficulty with relationships; screen for mental health concerns early and often; refer for psychological counseling; support groups; cognitive behavioral therapy; family involvement critical
  • Long-term Follow-up: Monitor graft durability; watch for late contractures; assess need for reconstructive surgery; vision screening (cataracts can develop years post-burn); continued scar management; psychosocial support; return to activities/work gradual and supported

Critical Burn Complications

Life-Threatening Emergencies

HYPOVOLEMIC SHOCK: Severe fluid shift in first 24-48 hours; leads to decreased cardiac output, tissue hypoperfusion, multi-organ failure, death. Manage with aggressive Parkland formula resuscitation; monitor CVP, urine output (goal 0.5 mL/kg/hr); avoid both under-resuscitation (shock) and over-resuscitation (pulmonary edema, compartment syndrome).
ACUTE RESPIRATORY DISTRESS SYNDROME (ARDS): From inhalation injury or sepsis; characterized by bilateral infiltrates, severe hypoxemia refractory to oxygen, poor lung compliance. Manage with early intubation if indicated; mechanical ventilation with PEEP; fluid management; prone positioning considered; mortality high if develops.
ACUTE KIDNEY INJURY (AKI) / MYOGLOBINURIA: Electrical burns → rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown) → myoglobin in urine (dark/cola-colored); myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules → acute tubular necrosis. Manage with aggressive fluid resuscitation (goal urine output 1 mL/kg/hr in electrical burns, higher than normal); monitor creatinine, BUN, K+; may require dialysis.
BURN WOUND SEPSIS: Bacterial invasion beyond burn eschar into viable tissue and bloodstream; fever, hemodynamic instability, confusion, thrombocytopenia, hyperglycemia. Take blood cultures; start broad-spectrum antibiotics empirically; aggressive surgical debridement; sometimes requires fasciotomy or amputation of non-viable tissue. Leading cause of death in burn patients.
CURLING'S ULCER (STRESS ULCER): Acute gastric/duodenal ulceration from severe burn stress; manifests as GI bleeding (coffee-ground emesis, tarry stools, anemia). Prophylaxis with H2 blockers (famotidine) or PPI (omeprazole) started on admission; monitor Hgb/Hct; consider proton pump inhibitor over H2 blocker; endoscopy if active bleeding.

Additional Critical Complications

  • Cardiac Dysrhythmias (Electrical Burns): Ventricular fibrillation (VF) most immediately lethal; can occur even with small current; other dysrhythmias including asystole, bradycardia possible. Continuous cardiac monitoring essential; ACLS protocol if dysrhythmia; even if initial rhythm normal, monitor for delayed dysrhythmias for 24+ hours
  • Compartment Syndrome: Circumferential full-thickness burns or electrical burns cause constrictive effect; increased pressure within muscle compartments → compromised perfusion → muscle necrosis → rhabdomyolysis; presents with pain out of proportion, paresthesias, decreased distal pulses, muscle necrosis. Escharotomy/fasciotomy relieves pressure; monitor compartment pressures if suspected
  • Hyperkalemia (Electrical Burns and Rhabdomyolysis): Cell destruction releases intracellular potassium; peaked T-waves on ECG, ventricular dysrhythmias possible. Manage with calcium (stabilizes myocardium), insulin + glucose (shifts K intracellularly), albuterol, sodium bicarbonate if acidotic, diuretics, possible dialysis
  • Inhalation Injury Complications: Upper airway obstruction from edema (stridor, difficulty breathing); lower airway obstruction from bronchospasm or pulmonary edema; ARDS from direct thermal injury or sepsis; acute lung injury may require intubation
  • Cataract Formation: Can develop months to years post-severe burn; monitor vision closely; increased risk with very high-temperature burns or prolonged recovery
  • Acute Hemolysis: Transient RBC destruction from burn heat; anemia possible; monitor CBC; transfuse if Hgb <7 (or symptomatically)

Special Considerations and Clinical Pearls

Burn Center Transfer and Management Decisions

When to Refer and Special Populations

  • Burn Center Referral Criteria: All patients with major burns (>15-20% TBSA); all full-thickness burns >5%; inhalation injury; chemical/electrical burns; circumferential burns; burns involving special areas (face, hands, feet, genitals, joints); concurrent trauma; age extremes (<10 or >50 years); medical comorbidities
  • Pediatric Considerations: Higher metabolic rate; larger proportional head requires larger fluid volumes; different caloric requirements; pain control challenging; psychological trauma greater; family separation issues
  • Elderly Considerations: Lower survival rates; delayed healing; increased infection risk; comorbidities complicate management; depression/isolation post-injury
  • Transport: Cover burns with clean dry dressing; establish IV access during transport; avoid hypothermia; do not apply ice directly (causes further damage); rapid transfer to burn center in major burns
