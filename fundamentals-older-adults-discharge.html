Older Adults & Discharge Planning

Combines two lecture sets: care of the older adult (aging myths, physiological/functional/cognitive changes, care settings, acute-care complications, falls, polypharmacy, atypical illness presentation, elder mistreatment) and discharge planning (why it matters, the critical teaching aspects, coordinating the discharge team).

Aging Myths, Ageism & Body System Changes

  • "Old age" is often defined starting at 65; the 65+ population is projected to roughly double by 2060. Chronological age says very little about an individual — avoid myths/stereotypes (assuming older adults are ill, forgetful, rigid, uninterested in learning, etc.).
  • Ageism = prejudice/discrimination based on age — can undermine an older patient's self-confidence and limit their access to care. Recognize it and don't buy into it.
  • Cardiovascular: decreased myocardial contractility (↓ cardiac output), cardiac hypertrophy, stiffer heart valves, lower resting heart rate with a blunted exercise response, and reduced perfusion to the extremities (cold hands/feet). Hypertension is common with aging but is not a normal/expected part of it — it's modifiable and treatable.
  • Respiratory: decreased respiratory muscle strength and lung expansion → a weaker cough (higher pneumonia/infection risk), increased AP diameter, and fewer alveoli/cilia. Kyphosis (upper spine curvature, from osteoporosis/disc degeneration/cancer) further reduces rib mobility and chest wall recoil.
  • GI: increased trunk/abdominal fat, slower peristalsis, decreased saliva, faster gastric emptying → reduced hunger and food tolerance.
  • Urinary: prostate hypertrophy in men (urinary retention, frequency, incontinence, UTI risk); decreased bladder capacity and pelvic floor muscle tone (stress incontinence, more common in women but possible in men too).
  • Integumentary: decreased turgor and subQ tissue (feel cold more easily), decreased skin resilience/moisture, thinning/graying hair.
  • Musculoskeletal: muscle strength declines with muscle mass loss (regular exercise slows this); osteoporosis is more common in postmenopausal women but also affects men, especially with poor nutrition/mobility.
  • Immune: slower immune response and wound healing, reduced antibody production, decreased response to immunizations.

Functional & Cognitive Changes

  • Functional status = capacity to safely perform ADLs (activities of daily living) and IADLs (instrumental ADLs — transportation, meal prep, shopping). ADL performance is a sensitive indicator of overall health; PT/OT are often part of a comprehensive functional assessment. A decline in function often signals an underlying problem — nurses should identify and report it.
  • Cognitive changes: significant memory loss, confusion, or disorientation are NOT a normal part of aging and always warrant further assessment — a common misconception is that they are. Subtle cognitive changes (from reduced brain cells/altered neurotransmitters) can occur normally, but anything beyond that needs evaluation.
  • Psychosocial changes: retirement, social isolation, changes in sexuality, housing/environment changes (having to leave a home they've long managed independently), and loss/grief from the death of loved ones.

Care Settings & Acute Care Basics

  • Older adults use a range of settings: community/home-based care, acute care (hospital), restorative care, and long-term/institutional care. Nurses help patients and families make placement decisions — encourage collaborative family involvement, and know that Medicare's Care Compare website helps evaluate facility quality.
  • What to look for in a facility: private rooms if desired, Medicare/Medicaid certification, adequate/background-checked staffing, quality of food and meal options, recreational activities, and family-involvement policies.
  • In acute care, prioritize comfort, safety, nutrition, hydration, and skin integrity — identify and treat the underlying cause of any problem, promote independence and dignity, and include the patient in their own care.

Acute Care Complications in Older Adults

  • Delirium: risk increases with an unfamiliar environment, sleep deprivation, sensory impairment, new medications, anesthesia, hypoxia, and infection. Nursing care: encourage family visits, provide memory cues, reorient frequently, compensate for sensory deficits (adequate lighting, etc.), and use reality orientation techniques.
  • Dehydration/malnutrition: watch for medications that suppress appetite and physical inability to reach/eat food — these seemingly minor issues can become life-threatening.
  • Hospital-acquired infections and urinary incontinence: incontinence causes include medications, restricted mobility, inability to reach the bathroom in time, confusion, or an untreated UTI.
  • Falls: both intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors are elevated in hospitalized older adults (see the dedicated falls section below).
  • Restorative care (home or long-term care setting) focuses on continued recovery from acute illness or support for chronic conditions, aiming to regain/improve ADLs and IADLs. Remember: aging alone does not inevitably cause disability or dependence — it's highly individualized.

Leading Health Concerns & Nutrition

  • Leading causes of death in older adults: heart disease (#1), cancer (#2), chronic lung disease (#3, strongly tied to smoking), stroke (#4). Smoking is a shared risk factor for all four — cessation counseling is always worthwhile. Alcohol abuse is also a notable concern.
  • Nutrition: encourage calorie-dense, high-nutrient foods; home-delivered meals are valuable for those living alone. Assess dental health/denture fit. Factors affecting nutrition: lifelong habits, cultural/religious preferences, situational factors (grocery access, finances, physical/cognitive ability to prepare food), chronic illness (e.g. limiting fluids due to incontinence — counterproductive), and medications altering taste/appetite.
  • Improving intake in care settings: eat with others, make food accessible, ensure the patient is comfortable/positioned well before the tray arrives, provide mouth care/handwashing before meals, eliminate unpleasant odors, involve a dietitian, and promote activity (increases appetite).
  • Pain is not a normal part of aging — it's a symptom signaling something is wrong and requires good assessment skills, especially since older adults often underreport it.
  • Medication use/polypharmacy is one of the greatest challenges for older adults (see below).

Falls: Risk Factors & Prevention

  • Falls are the leading cause of fatal injury in older adults and can be a life-changing event (e.g. hip fracture).
  • Intrinsic risk factors (inside the body): medication side effects, chronic conditions, mobility issues (arthritis), peripheral neuropathy (diabetes), gait instability (Parkinson's disease).
  • Extrinsic risk factors (environment): poor lighting, missing handrails/grab bars, poorly designed stairwells, slippery/uneven surfaces, not using assistive devices correctly, improper footwear.
  • Prevention: medication review, toileting plan, adequate lighting, appropriate footwear, call bell within reach, reduce clutter, ensure alarms are set as needed, answer call lights promptly, frequent rounding, and reorient confused patients — always treat the underlying cause when one is identified.

Polypharmacy, Assessment & Atypical Illness Presentation

In an older adult, new-onset confusion is often the first (or only) sign of infection or acute illness — a UTI, pneumonia, or even an MI may present primarily as confusion rather than the classic textbook symptoms (fever, bladder pain, chest pain). Always investigate new confusion.
  • Polypharmacy = concurrent use of multiple medications, affecting about 40% of older adults — increases drug-drug interaction risk. Best practice: one provider coordinates/manages all medications; communicate every medication to every specialist seen; perform medication reconciliation at the start and end of any hospital stay; review long-term-care medications monthly.
  • Assessment considerations: allow extra time and rest periods, account for vision/hearing deficits, and recognize early indicators of acute illness — new mental status change, new falls, appetite loss, dizziness, new incontinence.
  • Atypical presentation by setting: ambulatory patients may report only fatigue or decreased activity tolerance without classic acute symptoms — ask specifically about bowel habits, confusion, and increasing dyspnea (a cardiac history + new dyspnea warrants urgent evaluation). Depression is common and may show as loss of interest or a recent significant loss. In home care, investigate every fall and note that loss of appetite can be an early sign of worsening heart failure.
  • Watch for drug toxicity from polypharmacy combined with age-related decline in kidney/liver function.

Psychosocial Health: Elder Mistreatment, Communication & Teaching

  • Elder mistreatment = an intentional act or failure to act that causes or risks harm to an older patient — includes physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, financial exploitation, and neglect/abandonment. If suspected, interview/assess the patient privately, away from the caregiver. Kentucky requires mandatory reporting.
  • Reality orientation: frequently reinforces correct person/place/time (clocks, calendars, whiteboards) for a confused patient.
  • Validation therapy: an alternative approach that does not insist the confused patient agree with objective reality, but instead reflects sensitivity to the meaning behind their statements/behaviors — used when reality orientation isn't appropriate or effective.
  • Teaching older adults: assess readiness to learn first (not if anxious/in pain), speak clearly/slowly in a normal tone, present one idea at a time, minimize distractions, and use the teach-back method to confirm understanding. For hearing deficits: get their attention first, reduce background noise, don't shout, be ready to repeat, ensure good lighting (for lip-reading).

Discharge Planning Basics

  • Discharge planning determines what level of care a patient needs next (home, home health, rehab, etc.). The provider authorizes discharge, but nurses play a huge advocacy role — speak up if bedside assessment suggests a patient isn't ready, even if the provider has cleared them.
  • Why it matters: good discharge planning improves health outcomes and reduces readmissions (hospitals aren't fully reimbursed for same-diagnosis readmissions within a defined window). Poor planning risks medication mismanagement, health decline, and unsafe self-care.
  • Key considerations: adequate time to teach and get a return demonstration (never rush this), how much home assistance is needed, the patient's learning style (verbal/written/hands-on — always send home written instructions too), and ensuring the caregiver is present to hear discharge instructions.
  • What makes discharge planning difficult: time pressure to free up beds, family hesitancy/discomfort taking over care tasks (e.g. wound packing) — listen to these concerns; and social issues like transportation delays.
  • The care team decides discharge readiness together, but the nurse can and should appeal/request reevaluation if the patient or caregiver isn't truly ready.
  • Post-discharge sites of care: home, home health (e.g. IV antibiotics, wound care), skilled nursing facility (based on ADL independence level), inpatient vs. outpatient rehab (condition-specific units, e.g. spinal cord injury or stroke), long-term acute care (24-hour need), or hospice (end-of-life care).

Critical Aspects of Discharge Teaching

  • Medication reconciliation: review home meds vs. new hospital meds, teach the purpose/administration/side effects of anything new, coordinate with pharmacy on drug interactions and which old medications to stop/continue, and address access (insurance, cost, generic options). UK's "meds to beds" program sends patients home with about 30 days of medication. Always use teach-back ("tell me which medications you take in the morning...").
  • Diagnosis & when to seek help: ensure understanding of the diagnosis and treatment given, and be specific about exactly when to call the provider vs. go to the ED immediately (e.g. difficulty breathing, chest pain, BE-FAST stroke signs).
  • Procedures/surgeries: brief description of what was done, home/follow-up care, provider-specific instructions for showering/dressing changes, and clear guidance on which wound changes are normal healing vs. an ED-worthy concern (e.g. minor redness/itching vs. active bleeding or dehiscence).
  • Diet: clarify if the discharge diet differs from their usual one, give specific food guidance, involve a dietitian for complex needs (e.g. newly diagnosed diabetes, texture-modified diets) and caregiver teaching for any special preparation (e.g. thickened liquids).
  • Activity: weight-bearing status, physical therapy follow-up frequency, lifting restrictions (a practical reference: a gallon of milk is about 9 lbs), and any durable medical equipment (DME — walkers, crutches) with PT/OT involvement; assess the home layout (stairs, bedroom/laundry location) for safety.

Coordinating Discharge: Case Management, Social Services & Follow-Up

  • Case management/social services coordinate durable medical equipment, home health referrals (wound care, ADL assistance, PT/OT/speech), and financial assistance for medications. They also help assess practical home-life needs (running water, electricity) — uncomfortable but important questions, ideally asked on admission so they're already known by discharge.
  • Follow-up appointments: generally schedule primary care follow-up within a week, plus any relevant specialist follow-up (e.g. a cardiac surgeon after heart surgery). Consider access barriers (distance, satellite clinics, telehealth options) and ensure the patient has contact numbers for questions and a transportation plan.
  • Discharge planning is highly interdisciplinary (social work, case management, dietary, pharmacy, PT/OT, speech-language pathology) — the nurse isn't responsible for doing everything personally, but is very often the one coordinating across the whole team.

Is hypertension a normal part of aging?
No — it's common with aging but not a normal/expected part of it. It's modifiable and treatable.
Are significant memory loss and confusion a normal part of aging?
No — these are not normal aging changes and always warrant further assessment. Only subtle cognitive changes are expected with normal aging.
What is the single most common early sign of infection or acute illness (UTI, pneumonia, even MI) in an older adult?
New-onset confusion — often appearing before or instead of classic symptoms like fever or pain.
What percentage of older adults are affected by polypharmacy, and what's a key best practice to manage it?
About 40%. Best practice: one provider coordinates all medications, and every medication is communicated to every specialist seen (medication reconciliation at hospital admission and discharge).
What's the difference between reality orientation and validation therapy?
Reality orientation reinforces correct person/place/time for a confused patient. Validation therapy does not insist on agreement with objective reality — it reflects sensitivity to the meaning behind the patient's statements/behaviors.
If elder mistreatment is suspected, how should the patient be interviewed, and what's Kentucky's reporting requirement?
Interview/assess the patient privately, away from the caregiver. Kentucky requires mandatory reporting of suspected elder mistreatment.
Is pain a normal part of aging?
No — pain is a symptom signaling something is wrong, not a normal part of aging, and older adults often underreport it.
What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic fall risk factors, with an example of each?
Intrinsic (inside the body): medication effects, gait instability, neuropathy. Extrinsic (environment): poor lighting, missing handrails, improper footwear.
Who ultimately authorizes a patient's discharge, and what should a nurse do if they believe the patient isn't ready?
The healthcare provider authorizes discharge, but the nurse should speak up and advocate for reevaluation if bedside assessment suggests the patient isn't safe to go home.
What is medication reconciliation, and when is it performed?
Comparing home medications against new hospital medications to check for interactions and decide what to continue/stop/add — performed at both the start and end of a hospital stay.
Why is poor discharge planning costly to hospitals, beyond the direct impact on the patient?
Hospitals are not fully reimbursed for readmissions with the same diagnosis within a defined time window — poor discharge planning increases readmission risk.
Roughly how soon after discharge should a patient typically follow up with their primary care provider?
Within about a week — plus any relevant specialist follow-up related to their hospitalization.
