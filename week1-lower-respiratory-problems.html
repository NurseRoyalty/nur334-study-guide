Lower Respiratory Problems

Pneumonia

Pneumonia is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma — the functional unit of the lung (e.g. the alveoli, where O2/CO2 exchange happens).

Risk Factors

  • Older age.
  • An altered level of consciousness or a weakened cough — either can put a patient at risk for aspiration pneumonia, since they can't effectively expectorate secretions.
  • Immobility / prolonged bed rest, a debilitating illness, malnutrition, and smoking.
  • Tracheal intubation — a patient on a ventilator in the ICU (this is why VAP is an ICU concept, not med-surg).
ClassificationDefinition
CAP (Community-Acquired)Occurs in people who haven't been hospitalized, or who've been in a long-term care facility for <14 days of symptoms.
HAP (Hospital-Acquired)Begins 48+ hours after admission — not present at the time of admission.
VAP (Ventilator-Associated)HAP that develops in an intubated ICU patient on a ventilator. If the patient isn't intubated, it's classified as HAP instead.
TypeKey Features
ViralThe most common type. Usually mild/self-limiting (resolves in 3–4 days on its own) — but can still cause life-threatening problems.
BacterialUsually more serious than viral, and often requires hospitalization.
AspirationAbnormal entry of material from the mouth or stomach into the trachea/lungs, where it doesn't belong. Risk factors: decreased LOC, dysphagia (thin liquids are especially risky), and NG/feeding tubes. Can be silent — no vomiting or even coughing.
OpportunisticOccurs in immunocompromised patients (e.g. HIV) — covered further when opportunistic diseases come up later.

For a patient with an NG/feeding tube, placement matters: those tiny yellow feeding tubes ("dobhoff" tubes) should sit post-pyloric (past the pyloric sphincter). If a tube migrates back up out of the stomach and up the esophagus, the tube feeding can go down the lungs instead — and cause aspiration.

In an elderly patient, watch for new-onset confusion as an early sign of infection — including pneumonia. It's often one of the first things you'll notice, sometimes before the more "typical" symptoms show up (the same pattern comes up again with UTIs).

Signs & Symptoms

  • Fever, chills, cough, malaise.
  • Pain with deep breaths and coughing — so the patient may avoid taking good deep breaths, which becomes its own problem.
  • Dyspnea (shortness of air), fatigue, myalgia (achy muscles).
  • Bacterial pneumonia: typically a productive, purulent cough. Viral: usually scanty or non-productive.
  • Severe cases can progress to respiratory distress and respiratory failure.
MNEMONIC: Leukocytosis = an increase in white blood cells (leukocytes) — seen with an acute bacterial infection. Leukopenia = a decrease. Easy to mix up on an exam — worth committing to memory on its own.
Diagnostic FindingWhat It Means
Chest X-ray shows infiltratesHaziness where the film should be clear — suggests infection (e.g. in the left lower lobe).
CBC with diff shows leukocytosis, with a "shift to the left"An increase in neutrophils — and specifically an increase in immature neutrophils (the mature ones normally circulate; immature ones get released early to help fight an acute bacterial infection).
Sputum culture & sensitivity is positiveIdentifies the bacteria and which antibiotics will kill it. A viral infection won't grow any bacteria on culture — helps differentiate viral from bacterial.

Prevention & Nursing Care

  • Pneumococcal vaccine for anyone at risk or of the recommended age; don't smoke; balanced diet.
  • For a hospitalized patient: identify aspiration risk and act on it (positioning, diet texture, tube placement).
  • Turn, cough, deep breathe; ambulate; pulmonary mobilization — keeps secretions moving.
  • Strict hand hygiene and standard precautions.
  • Monitor vital signs and pulse ox regularly, and trend the data — a new fever or a dropping O2 sat is a meaningful change.
  • Listen to lung sounds at the start of your shift to set a baseline — so you can tell whether new crackles a few hours later are actually new.
  • Supplemental O2 as needed to keep pulse ox > 95%; incentive spirometer (very few contraindications — most patients should be using one, even if it's just sitting unused by the window); chest physiotherapy (percussion/postural drainage) to help loosen and mobilize secretions.
  • Increase fluids to thin secretions, unless contraindicated (e.g. severe heart failure).
  • Energy conservation — bundle care for a weak patient with activity intolerance, but still get them up and moving.
  • Antibiotics if bacterial; analgesics/antipyretics for pain or fever; discharge teaching.

COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)

COPD is an umbrella term covering chronic bronchitis and emphysema (asthma is technically an obstructive disease too, but it's covered separately in patho-pharm, not here). Most patients with COPD have some of both — collectively, this course focuses on emphysema and bronchitis.

Emphysema

  • Alveolar damage — the alveoli get overinflated/stretched and don't work as well.
  • Impaired gas exchange at the alveolar level (where O2/CO2 actually exchange) — this is the critical piece.

Chronic Bronchitis

  • Different pathology than emphysema — its key feature is excessive secretion production.

COPD is preventable and treatable. The most common cause of an exacerbation (a flare-up) is a respiratory infection — a patient might get a mild cold, and the next thing you know they've developed a full flare requiring hospitalization. Diagnosis is based on history, symptoms, and spirometry — remember, with obstructive disease the problem is getting air out, not in.

Exam Findings

  • Barrel chest — years of working hard to exhale expands the thorax; the AP diameter moves from the normal 2:1 ratio toward 1:1.
  • Decreased/diminished breath sounds, often with adventitious sounds — wheezing, rhonchi, crackles (especially during an exacerbation).
  • Prolonged expiration — it takes longer to get the air out, because (unlike a normal passive exhale) they have to push it out.
  • Pursed-lip breathing in advanced disease — purses the lips while blowing air out, creating positive pressure between the lips and alveoli that helps push the trapped air out. It's a compensatory mechanism most patients learn on their own.
  • Advanced disease: neck vein distention and peripheral edema (from pulmonary hypertension — high blood pressure inside the lungs makes blood back up systemically) and cachexia (excessively malnourished — breathing simply takes an enormous number of calories).

Example from the lecture: a 63-year-old male with a history of COPD presented with 2 weeks of worsening cough, yellow sputum, and dyspnea; O2 sat was 87% in the ED. His chest X-ray showed pneumonia (infiltrates/consolidation), some pleural effusions, a widened AP diameter, and a flattened diaphragm — a fairly typical picture of a COPD exacerbation.

Nursing Problems to Anticipate

  • Activity intolerance — poor exercise tolerance; O2 sat may even drop while talking or eating.
  • Ineffective breathing pattern — too fast, too slow, too shallow, or too deep. Breathing too fast and deep is called hyperventilation.
  • Ineffective airway clearance — thick secretions the patient is too weak or too congested to cough up.
  • Impaired gas exchange — from the alveolar damage itself.
  • Anxiety — very common; patients can be jittery or agitated because they're having such a hard time breathing.
  • Poor nutritional status (see cachexia, above).
Nursing Intervention / TeachingWhy
Smoking cessationThe single most effective, cost-effective intervention to reduce COPD risk and slow its progression.
Flu shot and other indicated vaccinesFor high-risk patients.
Early detection/treatment of respiratory infectionsInfections are the most common trigger for an exacerbation.
Inhaler therapy adherenceThe mainstay of COPD self-management — patients who can't afford their medication or skip it are the ones who often end up hospitalized with an exacerbation.
Tripod positioning (leaning forward, hands on legs)Expands the thorax and increases the surface area available for alveoli to exchange oxygen. Most patients adopt this on their own.
Pursed-lip breathing, huff coughingHuff coughing: a series of small coughs followed by a hard cough, to help produce and expectorate secretions.
Energy conservation and relaxation techniques—
Nutritional supportThese patients burn an extraordinary number of calories just breathing.
Psychosocial supportNot being able to catch your breath is genuinely scary.
Drug ClassEffect
Long-Acting Beta Agonist (LABA)Bronchodilates. A mainstay of treatment.
Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonist (LAMA)Also bronchodilates.
Inhaled SteroidsPotent anti-inflammatories — decrease the inflammatory response and dilate the bronchi.

MDI (Metered Dose Inhaler)

  • What the patient is using at home, most of the time.

Nebulizer

  • Used when the patient is sick enough to be hospitalized.
  • Hooked up to a machine with humidification, with medication in it as well.
CO2 narcosis — never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient, even a "CO2 retainer." A CO2 retainer is a COPD patient whose body has adapted, over a long period of time, to chronically high CO2 — so their stimulus to breathe becomes a low oxygen level instead of an elevated CO2 level (the normal trigger). In theory, giving a CO2 retainer too much oxygen (a very high SpO2 target) could take away their drive to breathe, causing CO2 narcosis. But this does not mean withholding oxygen — a hypoxic patient still needs oxygen. The nuance is just: don't over-target. In the hospital, COPD patients are typically kept around 88–90% (provider preference), not the high-90s. The only way to actually diagnose someone as a CO2 retainer is an ABG.

Exacerbation management: worsening dyspnea, cough, and sputum beyond the patient's typical day-to-day variation, requiring a change in treatment plan. The mainstay of treatment is oxygen to treat the hypoxemia — but always titrated with the CO2-retention nuance above in mind, never withheld. Noninvasive ventilation (BiPAP) may be used if the patient becomes hypercapnic. Chest physiotherapy (percussion and postural drainage, typically done by respiratory therapy) and antibiotics (if bacterial) round out treatment.

Tuberculosis (TB)

Caused by an infectious organism, Mycobacterium tuberculosis. If it's resistant to isoniazid (INH) and rifampin, it's called MDR-TB (multi-drug-resistant TB).

Risk Factors

  • Disproportionately affects poor, underserved, and minority populations.
  • IV drug users; people in crowded living situations or with less-than-ideal sanitation.
  • Immunosuppression, for any reason.
ClassificationWhat's Happening
Primary TBBacteria is inhaled and triggers an inflammatory reaction; the immune system responds and encapsulates the organism — hopefully for the rest of the patient's life.
Latent TBA persistent immune response to the bacterium, with no clinical manifestations of active disease. Asymptomatic and non-contagious — but will test positive on a skin or blood test. Can convert to active TB at any point, even years later.
Active / Reactivated TBDevelops from latent TB in about 5–10% of patients, sometimes years afterward — risk is much higher in the elderly and immunosuppressed. This is the point at which the patient becomes contagious.

How the TB skin test and IGRA are actually done is covered on Respiratory Labs & Diagnostics — this page covers what the results mean, not how to give the test

Reading a TB skin test: come back 48–72 hours later and check for induration (a raised area) — not just redness (erythema). Redness alone isn't considered positive.

Patient GroupInduration Considered Positive
Healthy, no risk factors≥ 15 mm.
Higher-risk (immigrants, IV drug users, nursing home residents, young children)≥ 10 mm.
Immunosuppressed (HIV, recent contact with active TB, organ/lung transplant)≥ 5 mm.

If either the skin test or the IGRA is positive, the next step is a chest X-ray. A clear CXR (no Ghon nodule) means latent TB. A positive CXR (nodules noted) means the patient needs further testing to confirm active disease.

DrugNotable Side Effect
Isoniazid (INH)Hepatotoxicity (along with acetaminophen — the two drugs in med-surg most associated with liver toxicity) and peripheral neuropathy (numbness/tingling in the fingers).
RifampinTurns excretions red or orange — including urine. Important to recognize this isn't hematuria (blood in the urine).
EthambutolAffects visual acuity and can cause difficulty differentiating between colors.

Strict adherence to drug therapy is essential — treatment for reactivated TB can run for months to years. For patients at high risk of non-adherence (e.g. a homeless patient), DOT (Direct Observational Therapy) means a provider directly supervises the patient taking each dose, to increase adherence and decrease the risk of MDR-TB.

Precaution / TeachingWhy
Airborne precautions, negative-pressure room, HEPA maskA negative-pressure room pushes air out rather than recirculating it to other patients' rooms — prevents aerosolized secretions from spreading. Staff must be fit-tested for the HEPA mask.
Home: prophylactic medication for high-risk household contactsE.g. a TB-positive patient's spouse may also be started on medication.
Teach the patient to cover their mouth/nose when coughing and wear a mask in crowdsReduces transmission.
Sputum for AFB every couple of weeks; 3 negative cultures = no longer infectiousConfirms the patient is no longer contagious with the reactivated version.

Lung Cancer

One of the most prevalent lung cancers, particularly in Kentucky — worth its own voiceover. Often somewhat asymptomatic early on: 10% of the time it's discovered incidentally on an X-ray taken for some other reason. Symptoms are frequently masked by the fact that many patients are smokers who already have a chronic cough, so they don't notice a change until the disease is fairly advanced. The most common symptom is a persistently productive cough.

Chest X-ray, CT, MRI, sputum for cytology, and the three ways to biopsy the lung are all covered on Respiratory Labs & Diagnostics — this page only covers what's specific to a lung cancer workup

  • Chest X-ray: may show a mass or an infiltrate (also called a consolidation, or just haziness).
  • CAT scan or MRI: used to assess for metastasis (spread).
  • Sputum for cytology: only about 20–30 out of 100 patients who actually have lung cancer will test positive on cytology alone.
  • A biopsy is the definitive diagnostic test — actual tissue, not just cells in the sputum.

Nursing Problems

  • Ineffective breathing pattern.
  • Anxiety — very common, compounded by the diagnosis and prognosis.
  • Malnutrition and fatigue.
  • Knowledge deficit — helping the patient understand the diagnosis and how to take care of themselves.

Collaborative care includes health promotion (smoking cessation) and anxiety reduction — much of nursing care in oncology is simply establishing rapport to help reduce a patient's anxiety.

Surgical OptionWhat It Involves
PneumonectomyThe entire lung is removed.
LobectomyJust a lobe is removed (the right lung has 3 lobes, the left has 2).
Wedge / Segmental ResectionAn even smaller portion removed. Which option is used comes down to surgeon preference, depending on how the cancer has spread.

Radiation and/or chemotherapy are also often used in addition to surgery — the cancer-specific details of those are covered later, in oncology, not in this lecture.

Post-pneumonectomy assessment: expect no breath sounds on the side where the lung was removed — there's no lung there to move air. On a chest X-ray, that side will appear white instead of black, since air shows up black on X-ray and there's no air moving through that side. Be cautious right near the midline — sounds heard there may just be transmitted from the remaining lung; true absence of breath sounds is clearer further from the midline.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What's the key difference between CAP, HAP, and VAP?
CAP: acquired outside the hospital. HAP: begins 48+ hours after admission. VAP: HAP that occurs in an intubated patient on a ventilator.
In an elderly patient, what's often the first sign of an infection like pneumonia?
New-onset confusion — sometimes before the more "typical" symptoms appear.
Why should an NG/feeding tube be placed post-pyloric in a patient at aspiration risk?
Prevents the tube feeding from migrating back up the esophagus and being aspirated into the lungs.
Which term means an increase in white blood cells, and which means a decrease?
Leukocytosis = increase. Leukopenia = decrease.
What's the key difference between emphysema and chronic bronchitis?
Emphysema: alveolar damage and impaired gas exchange. Chronic bronchitis: excessive secretion production.
Why does a COPD patient purse their lips when breathing?
It creates positive pressure between the lips and alveoli, helping push trapped air back out — a compensatory mechanism.
What is a CO2 retainer's stimulus to breathe, and should oxygen ever be withheld from one who is hypoxic?
Their stimulus is a low oxygen level (not high CO2, like a typical person). No — never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic patient; just avoid targeting unnecessarily high SpO2.
What's the difference between latent and active/reactivated TB?
Latent: asymptomatic and non-contagious, but tests positive. Active/reactivated: contagious and symptomatic (fever, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis), with a positive sputum AFB.
Name two notable side effects of isoniazid (INH).
Hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy.
What color does rifampin turn a patient's excretions, and why does this matter?
Red or orange — important not to mistake this for hematuria (blood).
What's the most common symptom of lung cancer?
A persistently productive cough.
What's the definitive diagnostic test for lung cancer?
A biopsy — actual tissue, not just cells from sputum cytology.
