Perioperative Nursing: Preoperative, Intraoperative & Postoperative

Including Malignant Hyperthermia Recognition and Management

🏥 PREOPERATIVE PHASE: Before Surgery

Timeline: Days to Hours Before Surgery

  • Day before or morning of: Preop teaching, assessment
  • Night before: NPO (nothing by mouth)
  • Morning of: Preop checklist, premedication

NPO (Nothing by Mouth) Guidelines - CRITICAL

Rationale: Prevent aspiration during anesthesia
  • Solid food: NPO 6+ hours before surgery
  • Clear liquids: NPO 2 hours before surgery
  • Exception: Medications with small sip of water usually allowed
  • Nursing role: Reinforce NPO; verify patient understands

Informed Consent

  • Surgeon responsible: For explaining procedure, risks, benefits
  • Nurse responsible: Verify consent form is signed; answer questions; clarify if needed
  • Patient must be: Alert, understanding, not heavily sedated
  • Red flag: Unsigned consent form = cannot proceed to OR

Preop Physical Exam & Health History

  • Review of Systems (ROS): Any recent illness, cough, fever?
  • Medications: Which ones to take? Hold? (warfarin, aspirin often held)
  • Allergies: Especially latex and anesthesia agents
  • Baseline vitals: BP, HR, RR, temp, O2 sat
  • Dental work: Recent crowns/caps? (loose teeth risk during intubation)

Preop Checklist (Before Patient Goes to OR)

  • ✓ Signed consent form
  • ✓ ID band on patient
  • ✓ NPO status verified
  • ✓ Medications given/held per orders
  • ✓ Vital signs documented
  • ✓ Preop teaching completed
  • ✓ Void before premedication
  • ✓ Remove jewelry, makeup, nail polish, dentures, hearing aids, prosthetics
  • ✓ Change into hospital gown
  • ✓ Hair covered with surgical cap

Preop Teaching Topics

  • Pain management: PCA pumps, medications available
  • Deep breathing & coughing: Prevent pneumonia
  • Leg exercises: Prevent blood clots (DVT)
  • Splinting incision: Hold pillow when coughing
  • Early ambulation: Important to prevent complications
  • What to expect: Recovery room, monitoring, tubes/drains

⚕️ INTRAOPERATIVE PHASE: During Surgery

OR Nursing Roles

  • Circulating nurse: Liaison between OR team and outside; documents care; manages equipment
  • Scrub nurse: Assists surgeon; passes instruments; maintains sterile field

Anesthesia Types

Type How It Works When Used
General Complete unconsciousness; patient intubated (tube in trachea) Major surgery; patient must be unaware
Regional/Spinal Numbs specific body area; patient awake Lower extremity surgery; cesarean section
Local Numbs only surgical area; patient conscious Minor procedures; biopsies
Twilight/Conscious Sedation Light sedation; patient partially responsive Diagnostic procedures; some surgeries

Patient Positioning During Surgery

  • Critical for: Circulation, nerve protection, respiratory function
  • Supine: Back; chest surgery
  • Prone: Facedown; back surgery
  • Trendelenburg: Head down; abdominal surgery
  • Lithotomy: Legs in stirrups; pelvic surgery
  • Nursing concern: Pressure areas, nerve compression from prolonged positioning

Maintaining Sterile Field

  • Critical principle: Sterile field must remain sterile
  • Scrubbed staff: Remain in sterile gowns/gloves
  • Non-sterile staff: Do not reach across sterile field
  • If contamination: Field must be redraped/disinfected

Safety Measures

  • Timeout procedure: Before incision - verify patient identity, procedure, site
  • Sponge count: Count all sponges before/after to prevent leaving one inside
  • Instrument count: Count all instruments before/after
  • Specimen labeling: All specimens labeled correctly
  • Blood loss monitoring: Estimate fluid loss

🔴 MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA (MH): Life-Threatening Emergency

THIS IS AN EXAM PRIORITY TOPIC

Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic disorder triggered by anesthetic agents. It's a medical emergency requiring immediate recognition and action.

What IS Malignant Hyperthermia?

  • Type: Pharmacogenetic disorder (inherited susceptibility)
  • Trigger: Exposure to certain anesthetic agents
  • Mechanism: Abnormal calcium release in muscle cells → uncontrolled muscle contraction
  • Result: Hypermetabolism, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, potential death

Risk Factors & Predisposing Conditions

  • Family history: Most important risk factor
  • Muscular dystrophies: Duchenne, myotonia congenita
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: History of reaction to antipsychotics
  • Previous unexplained anesthetic reaction
Screening question: "Has anyone in your family had problems with anesthesia or surgery?"

Triggering Agents

Inhalational anesthetic agents (most common):
  • Halothane
  • Isoflurane
  • Sevoflurane
  • Depolarizing muscle relaxants: Succinylcholine (most common trigger)

EARLY Signs of Malignant Hyperthermia - MUST RECOGNIZE

⚠️ LATE SIGN: Fever is a LATE sign! Do NOT wait for fever to suspect MH!

EARLY signs that appear DURING surgery:

  • Muscle rigidity/tightness: Jaw clenching (trismus), muscle stiffness
  • Tachycardia (fast heart rate): Often first sign
  • Tachypnea (fast respirations)
  • Skin mottling/flushing
  • Increased end-tidal CO2: Carbon dioxide increasing (from hypermetabolism)
  • Dark/cola-colored urine: Myoglobinuria from muscle breakdown

LATE signs (after initial signs if not caught):

  • Fever: Can reach 104-106°F+
  • Rhabdomyolysis: Muscle breakdown releasing myoglobin
  • Acidosis
  • Hyperkalemia: Potassium release from muscle damage
  • Coagulopathy: Bleeding from clotting dysfunction
  • Acute kidney injury: From myoglobin in urine
  • Cardiac arrest: Potential outcome

IMMEDIATE Actions if MH Suspected (MNEMONIC: STOP-MH)

S: STOP anesthesia immediately
T: Tell anesthesiologist/surgeon immediately
O: OXYGEN 100% begin
P: Prepare dantrolene sodium
M: Monitor vitals, labs, urine closely
H: Hyperventilate to blow off CO2

Specific interventions:

  • Discontinue triggering agents: Stop inhaled anesthetic and succinylcholine
  • Switch to safe anesthesia: Non-triggering agents
  • Administer dantrolene sodium: 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeat q5 min until signs resolve (max 10 mg/kg)
  • Hyperventilate: 100% oxygen, high minute ventilation to blow off CO2
  • Core cooling: Ice packs, cold IV saline, cooling blankets if hyperthermic
  • Monitor:
    • Continuous cardiac monitoring
    • Frequent vitals
    • ABGs (acid-base status)
    • Electrolytes (especially potassium)
    • CPK levels (muscle enzyme)
    • Urine output and color
  • Maintain urine output: >1 mL/kg/hr to prevent myoglobin-related kidney damage

Postoperative Monitoring After MH Crisis

  • ICU admission: For close monitoring
  • Dantrolene continuation: 1 mg/kg IV q4-6h for 24-48 hours
  • Monitor for recrudescence: Symptoms can return even after initial recovery
  • Fluids: Aggressive IV hydration to prevent kidney damage
  • Labs: Repeat CPK, electrolytes, urine myoglobin

Patient/Family Education After MH Exposure

  • Genetic testing: Recommend for patient and family members
  • MedAlert bracelet: Must wear for life
  • Inform all healthcare providers: Dentists, other surgeons, ERs
  • Medication list: Avoid triggering agents (keep written list)
  • Safe alternatives available: Non-triggering anesthetics can be used for future surgeries

🏨 POSTOPERATIVE PHASE: Recovery Period

PACU (Post-Anesthesia Care Unit): First 1-2 Hours

  • PRIORITIES: Airway, Breathing, Circulation
  • Monitoring: Continuous cardiac monitoring, SpO2, BP, temp
  • Position: Side-lying (if not contraindicated) to prevent aspiration
  • Oxygen: Usually given in PACU
  • Pain management: Assess pain regularly; give medications
  • Nausea/vomiting: Common; antiemetics available
  • Monitoring output: Urine (Foley catheter if present), drains

Common Postop Complications (Know These!)

  • Respiratory: Hypoventilation, atelectasis (collapsed lung), aspiration pneumonia
  • Cardiovascular: Hypotension, hypervolemia, DVT
  • GI: Nausea, vomiting, ileus (no bowel movement)
  • Urinary: Urinary retention, UTI
  • Integumentary: Wound infection, hematoma, seroma
  • Pain: Inadequate management

Postop Nursing Interventions

  • Deep breathing & coughing: Every 1-2 hours; splint incision
  • Leg exercises: Prevent DVT; encourage movement
  • Early ambulation: Usually 1st postop day; helps prevent complications
  • Pain management: Regular assessment; don't let patient fall behind on pain
  • Monitor vitals: Especially temp (watch for infection)
  • Wound care: Keep clean and dry; monitor for signs of infection
  • Hydration: Progress from clear liquids to diet as tolerated

Discharge Teaching

  • Wound care: Keeping clean, signs of infection
  • Activity restrictions: No heavy lifting, driving while on narcotics
  • Medication instructions: Including pain management
  • Follow-up appointments: Surgeon, specific dates
  • When to call doctor: Fever, redness, drainage, dehiscence (opening)
