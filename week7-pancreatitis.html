Pancreatitis

← Back to Week 7 Study Guide

Acute pancreatitis (auto-digestion, why each manifestation happens, complications, and care) and chronic pancreatitis (steatorrhea, enzyme replacement, nutrition, and preventing flares).

Acute Pancreatitis

Premature activation of pancreatic enzymes that auto-digest the pancreas, leading to fibrosis. Ranges from mild to severe. Gallstones and alcohol are the two most common causes. CT is the best imaging for pancreatitis and its complications.

Cullen's and Turner's signs (severe)Enzymes leak into the subcutaneous tissue → bluish discoloration
Cullen's sign = bluish discoloration around the umbilicus. Turner's sign = bluish discoloration of the flank. Both mean advanced disease and a poor prognosis.

Complication: pseudocyst

  • Cavity filled with necrotic products on the outside of the pancreas
  • Often resolves on its own
  • Worst case: perforates into the peritoneum → severe peritonitis

Complication: abscess

  • Forms within the pancreas
  • Often needs surgical drainage

Collaborative Care

  • Relieve pain first — it can cause hemodynamic compromise. IV opioids (morphine)
  • Position with knees bent (fetal position) — less stretch on the peritoneum
  • NPO during a flare, usually with NG to low wall suction — rest the gut and the pancreas
  • Aggressive IV hydration for hypovolemia
  • Monitor vital signs frequently (they can be labile) and fluid/electrolyte balance (hypovolemia, hypocalcemia)
  • Monitor stools for steatorrhea
  • Monitor for hyperglycemia — the endocrine (insulin) function can be affected
  • Encourage the patient to stop drinking alcohol
  • When no longer NPO: carbohydrates encouraged (less stimulating to the pancreas), fat restricted

Chronic Pancreatitis

Progressive and destructive, with remissions and flares, from inflammation and fibrosis of pancreatic tissue.

Manifestations

  • Intense abdominal pain (tenderness less than in acute)
  • A mass is likely a pseudocyst or abscess
  • Ascites, which can cause respiratory compromise
  • Steatorrhea: fatty stool that floats, looks greasy, and is very malodorous — fat isn't being digested and absorbed
  • Dark urine from increased bilirubin

Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT)

Pancrelipase (amylase, lipase, protease) — the standard of care to prevent malnutrition, malabsorption, and excessive weight loss.

  • Take with the first bite of every meal or snack — when the body would normally release enzymes
  • May be ordered with an antacid or H2 blocker (pH)
  • Swallow without chewing; wipe any off the lips — enzymes irritate the mucosa
  • Powder form: mix in applesauce or fruit juice; do not mix with protein-containing foods
  • Do not crush enteric-coated preparations
  • Evaluate by recording the number and consistency of stools each day — goal: fewer, less fatty stools

Other care and teaching

  • Opioids are used but more cautiously — some can increase pain by causing spasm
  • Weight loss can be significant; oral needs may reach 4,000–6,000 calories a day, so the patient may need TPN
  • Prevent flares: avoid alcohol, nicotine, and caffeinated beverages; eat bland, low-fat, high-protein, high-carbohydrate meals; avoid gastric stimulants such as spices
  • Small meals, high in calories; take enzymes as prescribed; rest after eating
Odd but testable in acute pancreatitis: the patient is hypovolemic (fluid leaks out of the vessels), hypocalcemic, may be hyperglycemic, and is at risk for a paralytic ileus. Pain control is the first priority.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What is the pathophysiology of acute pancreatitis?
Pancreatic enzymes activate too early and auto-digest the pancreas.
What are the two most common causes of acute pancreatitis?
Gallstones and alcohol.
A client with acute pancreatitis has HR 124 and BP 92/58. Why is the client hypovolemic?
Inflammatory mediators increase vascular permeability, so fluid leaks out of the vessels. Treat with aggressive IV hydration.
Which electrolyte imbalance is expected in acute pancreatitis, and why?
Hypocalcemia — calcium deposits in areas of fat necrosis.
What do Cullen's and Turner's signs look like and mean?
Bluish discoloration around the umbilicus (Cullen's) and the flank (Turner's) from enzyme leakage; severe disease with a poor prognosis.
What is the priority intervention for acute pancreatitis?
Pain relief (IV opioids such as morphine) — the pain can cause hemodynamic compromise. Position with knees bent.
Why is a client with an acute pancreatitis flare kept NPO with an NG tube?
To rest the gut and the pancreas; the NG to low wall suction also manages a paralytic ileus.
How do a pancreatic pseudocyst and an abscess differ?
A pseudocyst sits outside the pancreas, often resolves, but can perforate and cause peritonitis. An abscess is within the pancreas and often needs surgical drainage.
What is steatorrhea?
Fatty stool that floats, looks greasy, and smells very foul, because fat isn't being digested.
When should pancrelipase be taken?
With the first bite of every meal or snack.
Which administration rules apply to pancreatic enzymes?
Swallow without chewing, wipe the lips, don't crush enteric-coated forms, mix powder in applesauce or juice, and don't mix with protein foods.
How does the nurse know pancreatic enzyme therapy is working?
Fewer and less fatty stools (record the number and consistency daily).
