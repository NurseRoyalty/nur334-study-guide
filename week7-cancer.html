Cancer

← Back to Week 7 Study Guide

Nursing care of the patient with cancer: prevention and the CAUTION warning signs, diagnosis, treatment goals, surgery, chemotherapy and extravasation, radiation safety, managing side effects, and psychosocial support.

Prevention and Early Detection

  • Teach patients to avoid carcinogens: excess alcohol, poor dietary habits, tobacco, too much sun exposure
  • Promote health: diet, exercise, rest
  • Regular health exams and cancer screenings
  • Teach self-exams (breast, testicular)
  • Know the seven warning signs of cancer (American Cancer Society)
CAUTION — seven warning signs: Change in bowel or bladder habits · A sore that does not heal · Unusual bleeding or discharge · Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere · Indigestion or difficulty swallowing · Obvious change in a wart or mole · Nagging cough or hoarseness

Diagnosing Cancer

  • Health history
  • Cytology: annual Pap test (cervical cancer), sputum (lung cancer)
  • Tissue biopsy — very important in diagnosis
  • Lab values; chest X-ray; radiologic studies such as mammograms
  • Tumor markers (e.g., PSA, CEA): substances made by tumor cells; used for diagnosis and to follow progression
  • Bone marrow exam: for cancers that start in the marrow (leukemia, lymphoma)
  • Waiting for results causes a great deal of anxiety — support the patient and family

Treatment Goals and Modalities

Goals, in order: Cure → if not, Control → if not, Palliation (palliative care). Modalities: surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, biologic and targeted therapy.

Surgery (about half of people with cancer)

  • Prophylaxis — e.g., double mastectomy in someone with a high-risk breast cancer gene
  • Biopsy (diagnosis)
  • Cure — remove the cancer
  • Control — debulking: remove part of the tumor to improve comfort or make chemo/radiation more effective; not curative
  • Palliation; a second look to see how things look after treatment
  • Reconstruction and rehabilitation

Chemotherapy

The mainstay of cancer therapy, used for solid tumors and hematologic malignancies. Chemo is systemic and kills rapidly dividing cells.

Nursing implications

  • The nurse must be chemotherapy certified
  • Agents are very toxic — strict measures protect both the patient and the nurse
  • Double-check with two RNs
  • Central venous catheters are preferred (tunneled/non-tunneled catheters, implanted ports) but not required
  • Premedicate as ordered: antiemetics, anti-inflammatories, anti-allergy drugs. Anticipatory nausea and vomiting can happen before a known treatment day
  • Check labs first (WBC, platelets, H&H). Chemo is held if WBCs or platelets are too low and given once counts recover
  • Nadir = the lowest point of blood counts after chemo; dosing intervals are timed so counts recover before the next dose
  • Provide patient and family support throughout treatment

Extravasation

Extravasation is infiltration of a vesicant — a drug that destroys tissue (serious necrosis, possible loss of the limb) if it leaks out of the vein. Prevention is the best treatment.

Prevent and recognize

  • Monitor the IV site very closely
  • Ensure a patent, free-flowing IV with good blood return
  • Pain is the cardinal symptom (not always present)
  • If blood return is lost: stop the chemotherapy

If it happens

  • Stop the infusion immediately
  • Attach a syringe and aspirate slowly and gently to remove as much drug as possible
  • Remove the cannula
  • Elevate the arm
  • Give the intradermal antidote per agency protocol (pharmacy is a resource)

Radiation Therapy

About 60% of patients with cancer receive radiation. Unlike chemo, radiation is local — a precise, carefully defined area — so side effects depend on the site treated. Used alone or with chemo and/or surgery.

Time · Distance · Shielding. The closer you are and the longer you stay, the more exposure you get. Spend as little time as possible, as far away as possible.

Caring for a patient with a sealed implant

  • Private room with a sign on the door
  • Nurse wears a dosimeter — it measures exposure, gives no protection, and is never shared
  • No pregnant women and no children under 16
  • Visitors limited to 30 minutes a day, staying 6 feet from the source
  • Never touch a dislodged source with your hands — use forceps and place it in the lead container kept in the room
  • Save all dressings and bed linens until the source is removed, then discard as usual. Other equipment can leave the room at any time

Skin care teaching for the radiated area

  • Wash the area daily with water and a little mild soap; use the hand, not a washcloth; rinse well
  • Do not wash off the skin markings — they guide the radiation
  • Pat dry; don't rub
  • No powders, lotions, or creams unless prescribed by the radiologist
  • Soft, non-constrictive clothing over the site
  • Avoid direct sun and temperature extremes

Neutropenic precautions (protect the patient)

  • Follow agency protocol; maintain isolation technique
  • Screen visitors
  • No fresh flowers or plants in the room
  • Monitor for signs and symptoms of infection
  • Teach about the nadir (often around days 7–9): the patient feels worst and should limit contact with other people

Neutropenic diet

  • Avoid raw fruits and vegetables that can't be peeled (blueberries, strawberries)
  • Peelable produce is OK (oranges, bananas, apples, pineapple)
  • Food and flatware are wrapped for delivery
  • No raw peanuts or seeds

Side Effects: GI, Mouth, Skin, and Other

GI effects

  • Anorexia and taste changes — focus on foods the patient likes
  • Nausea and vomiting, including anticipatory
  • Weigh twice weekly; monitor albumin and prealbumin
  • Small, frequent, high-protein, high-calorie meals; nutritional supplements
  • Bowel changes — usually diarrhea, often from radiation to the bowel

Stomatitis

  • Painful inflammation and ulcers of the mouth (mucositis = mucous membranes anywhere)
  • Thorough oral assessment; regular, gentle brushing and flossing
  • Saline gargles; swish-and-spit or swish-and-swallow mixtures (numbing, anti-inflammatory, antifungal)
  • No alcohol-based mouthwash
  • Artificial saliva; soft, non-irritating foods
  • Skin reactions: localized with radiation, generalized with chemo. Dry desquamation (dry, irritated) and wet desquamation (moist, very tender) — wash gently by hand, pat dry, no unprescribed lotions
  • Fatigue — from anemia and the cancer itself
  • Hair loss — depends on the drug; not always predictable
  • Pulmonary and cardiac damage from radiation or drug effects
  • Infertility in young people

Psychosocial Care

  • Complementary/alternative therapy: support the patient's right to choose, while encouraging them to continue the prescribed treatment plan
  • Support a healing environment; be calm, quiet, and present
  • Concerns: hair loss, loss of a body part, secondary cancers, recurrence (patients become very body-vigilant), disability, death
  • Hope is a form of positive reappraisal — nurses can enable it
  • Spirituality may have a positive or negative effect (anger at God, or renewed faith)
  • Social support is one of the most important things a patient can have: listen, and teach about community support groups
Safety priorities in cancer care: lost blood return or pain at a chemo site → stop the infusion. ANC <500 → protective precautions. Platelets <20,000 → expect a transfusion. A dislodged radiation source → forceps and lead container, never hands.

Self-Test Flashcards

A client asks what the warning signs of cancer are. Which mnemonic does the nurse use?
CAUTION: Change in bowel/bladder habits, A sore that doesn't heal, Unusual bleeding or discharge, Thickening or lump, Indigestion or difficulty swallowing, Obvious change in a wart or mole, Nagging cough or hoarseness.
What are the three goals of cancer treatment, in order?
Cure; if not possible, control; if not possible, palliation.
A surgeon plans a debulking procedure. How should the nurse explain it?
Part of the tumor is removed to improve comfort or make chemo/radiation work better. It controls the cancer; it isn't curative.
A client's WBC and platelet counts are very low on the day chemo is due. What should the nurse expect?
Chemo will be held until the counts recover, because of the risk of infection and bleeding.
During a vesicant infusion, the IV loses its blood return. What does the nurse do first?
Stop the infusion. Then aspirate gently with a syringe, remove the cannula, elevate the arm, and give the antidote per protocol.
Which patient is radioactive: external radiation, a sealed implant, or swallowed I-131?
External: not radioactive. Sealed implant: emits radiation while active, but excretions are not radioactive. Unsealed (I-131): the patient and all excretions are radioactive.
What are the visitor rules for a client with a sealed radiation implant?
No pregnant women or children under 16; visits limited to 30 minutes a day; stay 6 feet from the source.
A sealed radiation source falls onto the bed. What should the nurse do?
Never touch it by hand. Pick it up with forceps and put it in the lead container kept in the room.
What skin care should a client receiving external radiation follow?
Wash gently with the hand and mild soap, rinse, pat dry, don't remove the markings, no lotions or powders unless prescribed, soft clothing, avoid sun and temperature extremes.
A client's ANC is 400. What does this mean and what is needed?
Severe neutropenia (below 500) — protective precautions: screen visitors, no fresh flowers or plants, neutropenic diet, watch closely for infection.
At what platelet counts does bleeding risk rise and are platelets usually transfused?
Serious bleeding risk below 50,000; transfusion is typically ordered below 20,000 (normal 150,000–400,000).
Which mouth care is appropriate for stomatitis?
Gentle brushing and flossing, saline gargles, prescribed swish mixtures, artificial saliva, soft non-irritating foods — and no alcohol-based mouthwash.
