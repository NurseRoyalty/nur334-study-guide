Upper GI Bleed

An upper GI bleed can be slow and insidious (happens without you noticing) or sudden. What the blood looks like tells you how fresh it is.

FindingWhat it looks likeWhat it means
Hematemesis — bright red (frank/gross blood)Visible red blood in the vomitRecent or ongoing bleeding
Hematemesis — coffee-groundLooks like coffee grounds; very malodorousBlood has been partly digested → older bleeding, or bleeding has stopped
MelenaBlack, tarry, malodorous stoolBlood has had up to about 8 hours to be digested; helps locate the bleed
Occult bloodNot visible; positive guaiacNever an expected finding unless a GI bleed is already known

Causes

  • Esophageal varices — protrusions (like hemorrhoids in the esophagus) from portal hypertension in end-stage liver disease; bleeding can be uncontrollable
  • Ulcers — about 50% of upper GI bleeds (stomach or duodenal)
  • H. pylori and NSAIDs — the two most common causes of bleeding ulcers
  • Drug-induced ulcers: NSAIDs, salicylates (aspirin), steroids
  • Stress-related mucosal disease (SRMD): mucosal injury in 70–90% of ICU patients → why ICU patients are placed on a PPI (e.g., IV pantoprazole)

Blakemore tube

  • Emergency for bleeding esophageal varices
  • Tube passed like an NG; a balloon is inflated to put pressure on the bleeding varices
  • An ICU device — occasionally placed on med-surg in an emergency; the patient ends up in ICU

Nursing Interventions

  • Watch vital sign trends against the baseline; check for orthostatic hypotension (hypovolemia)
  • Signs of hypovolemic shock: rising heart rate, intense thirst, cool clammy skin, restlessness (always investigate a newly restless patient)
  • Assess for a tense, rigid, board-like abdomen (perforation), distention, and guarding (peritonitis)
  • Labs: H&H and BUN — blood digested in the GI tract raises BUN, so a high BUN isn't always a kidney problem
  • Start an 18-gauge IV — anticipate PRBCs and fluid resuscitation
  • NG lavage (irrigation) may be ordered; evidence for it is questionable
  • Check emesis and stool for occult blood; give ordered medications
  • Prepare for endoscopy: hemostasis (e.g., cauterizing the bleed) is first-line — nothing else helps if the bleed isn't stopped
  • Teaching: each medication (purpose, side effects, when to call) and the signs of a recurrent bleed
High BUN + falling H&H + rising heart rate in a client with GI symptoms points to a GI bleed, not the kidneys. Protein from digested blood raises the BUN.

Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Crohn's vs Ulcerative Colitis

Lower GI = small and large intestines. Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (UC) together are called inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Compare and contrast them.

FeatureCrohn's diseaseUlcerative colitis
LocationAnywhere mouth to anus; most often the terminal ileum (and colon)Colon only; starts in the rectum and moves up (distal → proximal)
PatternSkip lesions — cobblestone appearance (normal mucosa between diseased areas)Uniform and continuous
DepthTransmural (through the whole bowel wall) → more likely to perforateMucosal/submucosal
BleedingSometimesMore common
Malabsorption / nutritionYes — small intestine is involvedUsually not (colon absorbs water, not nutrients)
ComplicationsAbscesses, fistulas, strictures, perforationToxic megacolon (inflammation into deep layers → colon stops working, dilates, can rupture), perforation
Colon cancer riskIncreasedIncreased
SurgeryUsually for strictures, fistulas, obstruction, bleeding. Not curative; recurrence is highRemoving the colon is curative

What they share

  • Chronic inflammation of the intestines with remissions and exacerbations (flares)
  • Cause unknown; autoimmune; no cure (except colectomy for UC)
  • Symptoms: diarrhea, weight loss, abdominal pain, fever, fatigue
  • Treatment: medications to decrease inflammation and maintain remission; surgery

Treatment Goals During a Flare

  • Rest the bowel: NPO with IV hydration during the acute phase
  • Control inflammation with medications; treat any infection
  • Correct malnutrition — these patients can be very malnourished (poor intake, higher metabolic rate, steroids, malabsorption, protein-losing enteropathy: protein leaks from the blood into the gut)
  • Reduce stress; relieve symptoms; improve quality of life
  • When eating resumes: high-calorie, high-protein, LOW-residue (low fiber keeps the gut calm) with vitamin and iron supplements
  • No universal food triggers — keep a food diary (possible triggers: cold or hot foods, high-residue foods, lactose)
  • Quit smoking
  • If the gut can't be used: parenteral nutrition (TPN)

TPN (Total Parenteral Nutrition)

If the gut works, use it. A malnourished patient with a working gut (even a comatose one) gets tube feedings, not TPN. TPN is for when the gut can't be used — severe nausea/vomiting, a flare that requires bowel rest, recovery from bowel resection.
  • Parenteral = outside the GI tract (IV). Nutritionally complete: glucose, protein, vitamins, minerals, electrolytes → positive nitrogen balance while the bowel rests
  • Very hypertonic (D10–D25) → requires a central line (e.g., internal jugular or PICC), not a peripheral IV
  • Dedicated line; lipids (milky fat emulsion) usually infused separately
  • Blood glucose checks about every 6 hours; the patient may need insulin coverage
  • Start slowly and taper slowly (e.g., 10 → 20 → 30 → 50 mL/hr; stop by stepping down) so the pancreas can adjust insulin output
  • Not used forever — an unused gut atrophies
  • PPN (partial parenteral nutrition) exists but is rarely used

IBD Drug Therapy and Surgery

Medications (details in patho-pharm)

  • Sulfasalazine — GI anti-inflammatory; drug of choice for IBD
  • Aminosalicylates — decrease inflammation
  • Antimicrobials — prevent/treat secondary infection
  • Corticosteroids — strong anti-inflammatories, many side effects
  • Immunosuppressants (autoimmune disease)
  • Immunomodulators (e.g., anti-TNF) — prevent leukocytes from moving into inflamed tissue
  • UC: drugs induce remission about 80% of the time; about 1 in 5 eventually need surgery. Crohn's: most need surgery at some point
  • Hospitalize if there's no response to drugs or complications develop

Surgery

  • Total proctocolectomy with ileal pouch–anal reservoir (J-pouch): most common UC surgery; two stages — temporary ileostomy while the J-pouch heals, then a takedown so stool passes through the rectum again (liquid stool)
  • Total proctocolectomy with permanent ileostomy: very liquid, caustic output
  • Continent ileostomy (Kock pouch): internal pouch the patient catheterizes; dressing over the stoma, no bag
  • Crohn's: intestinal resection with anastomosis of healthy bowel — not curative

Patient Teaching

Living with IBD

  • Rest and diet management
  • Perianal care with frequent diarrhea
  • Medications and their side effects
  • Signs of recurrence and when to seek care
  • Diversional activities to reduce stress

After colon surgery — call the provider for:

  • New drainage, the wound opening, redness, or a change in the stoma
  • No stool or gas from the rectum or stoma for more than 24 hours
  • Increasing abdominal pain, vomiting, swelling
  • High ostomy output, dark urine or no urine
  • Fever, or unable to take anything by mouth for 24 hours

Intestinal Obstruction

Mechanical

  • Most often in the small intestine
  • Adhesions, hernias, strictures (as in Crohn's)
  • Surgical adhesions are the #1 cause — days to years after surgery
  • Most obstructions are mechanical

Non-mechanical

  • About 1 in 10 obstructions
  • Paralytic ileus: peristalsis stops, most often after the bowel has been manipulated
  • Treatment: NG to low wall suction and wait for peristalsis to return (otherwise the patient keeps vomiting)

Collaborative care

  • Decompress the intestines: NG to low wall suction
  • Correct and maintain fluids and electrolytes
  • Pain control as applicable
  • Remove the obstruction if needed: bowel resection with end-to-end anastomosis (the diseased piece, e.g., a tumor, is removed and the ends are sewn together — no stoma; bowel function resumes when peristalsis returns)

A client vomits bright red blood. How is this different from coffee-ground emesis?
Bright red (frank) blood means recent or ongoing bleeding. Coffee-ground emesis is partly digested blood — older bleeding, or bleeding that has stopped.
What is melena, and what does it tell you?
Black, tarry, foul-smelling stool — blood that has been digested for up to about 8 hours, pointing to an upper GI source.
What causes about half of all upper GI bleeds?
Stomach or duodenal ulcers (most often from H. pylori or NSAIDs).
Why are ICU patients routinely put on a PPI?
Stress-related mucosal disease affects 70–90% of ICU patients; a PPI (e.g., IV pantoprazole) helps prevent bleeding ulcers.
What is a Blakemore tube used for?
Bleeding esophageal varices — a balloon is inflated to put pressure on the bleeding site. The patient goes to ICU.
A client with a GI bleed has a BUN of 38 and normal creatinine. How should the nurse interpret the BUN?
Digested blood in the GI tract raises BUN — this isn't necessarily a kidney problem.
Which IV gauge should the nurse start for a client with an active GI bleed, and why?
18-gauge — to give PRBCs and fluid resuscitation.
Which early signs suggest hypovolemic shock from a GI bleed?
Rising heart rate, orthostatic hypotension, intense thirst, cool clammy skin, and restlessness.
What is the first-line management of an upper GI bleed?
Hemostasis via endoscopy (e.g., cauterizing the bleed). Nothing else works if the bleed isn't stopped.
Where does Crohn's disease occur compared with ulcerative colitis?
Crohn's: anywhere mouth to anus, most often the terminal ileum. UC: colon only, starting in the rectum and moving up.
What does 'cobblestone' or 'skip lesions' describe?
Crohn's disease — patches of diseased bowel with normal mucosa in between. UC is continuous.
Why is Crohn's more likely than UC to cause fistulas and perforation?
Crohn's inflammation is transmural (through the whole wall); UC is mucosal/submucosal.
Which IBD causes malabsorption, and why?
Crohn's — it involves the small intestine, where nutrients are absorbed. UC affects the colon, which absorbs water.
What is toxic megacolon?
A UC complication: inflammation spreads into the deep colon layers, the colon stops working and dilates, and it can rupture.
Which IBD can be cured by surgery?
Ulcerative colitis — removing the colon (total proctocolectomy) is curative. Crohn's surgery is not; recurrence is high.
A client is admitted with a severe Crohn's flare. What are the first priorities?
Rest the bowel (NPO with IV hydration), control inflammation, treat infection, and correct malnutrition.
When a client with IBD starts eating again, what diet is recommended?
High-calorie, high-protein, LOW-residue, with vitamin and iron supplements; a food diary to find personal triggers.
A comatose client with a working gut is malnourished. TPN or tube feeding?
Tube feeding — if the gut works, use it.
Why does TPN need a central line?
It's very hypertonic (D10–D25) and would damage a peripheral vein.
How often is blood glucose checked on TPN, and why?
About every 6 hours — the high dextrose load can cause hyperglycemia needing insulin coverage.
Why is TPN started and stopped gradually?
So the pancreas can adjust its insulin output to the rising or falling glucose load.
What is the drug of choice for IBD mentioned in lecture?
Sulfasalazine — a GI anti-inflammatory.
After a J-pouch procedure, what is the 'takedown'?
The second stage: the temporary ileostomy is closed so stool passes through the rectum again.
What is the #1 cause of intestinal obstruction?
Surgical adhesions (mechanical), often days to years after surgery.
A client develops a paralytic ileus after bowel surgery and keeps vomiting. What does the nurse anticipate?
NG tube to low wall suction to decompress, and wait for peristalsis to return; manage fluids and electrolytes.
What is an end-to-end anastomosis?
The diseased section of bowel is removed and the two ends are sewn together — no stoma.
