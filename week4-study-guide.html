Renal & Urinary System Pathology

← Back to Study Guide

Week 4 covers the full spectrum of renal pathology: from common urinary tract infections to progressive kidney disease. Understanding how these conditions relate and progress will prepare you for clinical decision-making.

1. Urinary Tract Infections (UTI)

Most common bacterial infection in women — accounting for ~1/3 of healthcare-associated infections. The pathogenic organism is typically E. coli, ascending from the urethra up the urinary tract.

  • DYSURIA mnemonic: Dysuria (painful urination), frequency/urgency, Suprapubic discomfort, Urgency, Residual urine, Incomplete emptying, Absent fever (distinguishes from pyelonephritis).
  • Labs: Pyuria (WBCs in urine), bacteriuria (>10⁵ CFU/mL), positive nitrites, positive leukocyte esterase.
  • Treatment: Antibiotics (TMP-SMX, nitrofurantoin, fluoroquinolones) for 3-7 days, adequate hydration, frequent voiding.
  • Prevention: Increased fluid intake, regular voiding, post-intercourse urination, proper perineal hygiene.

2. Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI)

A serious upper UTI involving the kidneys and renal pelvis with systemic manifestations. This is more than a bladder infection — it's a systemic infection requiring prompt treatment.

  • FLANK mnemonic: Fever (high; typically >38.5°C/101.3°F), Lumbar (flank) pain, Abdominal distension/tenderness, Nausea/vomiting, Kidney tenderness on CVA percussion.
  • Diagnostic findings: Pyuria with WBC casts (indicates renal involvement), positive urine culture, elevated WBC count on CBC, imaging to rule out obstruction.
  • Treatment: IV antibiotics (fluoroquinolones or cephalosporins), adequate hydration, pain management, monitor for sepsis.
  • Nursing priority: Monitor vital signs closely; watch for signs of sepsis. Humidified O2, frequent coughing/suctioning, comfort measures.
Red Flag: Systemic symptoms (fever, chills, malaise) distinguish pyelonephritis from uncomplicated cystitis. Immediate treatment required to prevent sepsis and kidney damage.

3. Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)

Stone formation in the kidneys — can obstruct urine flow and cause acute, severe pain. Treatment depends on stone type, size, and location.

Stone Type (C-CUP)FrequencyAssociated Conditions
Calcium oxalate~75% (most common)Hypercalciuria, dehydration
Calcium phosphate~5-10%Alkaline urine, hyperparathyroidism
Uric acid~5-10%Gout, acidic urine
Struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate)~10-15%Chronic UTIs, alkaline urine
  • Clinical presentation: Sudden onset severe colicky pain radiating from flank to groin, hematuria, dysuria, nausea/vomiting, urinary urgency/frequency.
  • Diagnosis: CT noncontrast (gold standard), ultrasound, X-ray for radiopaque stones.
  • Conservative management: Pain control (NSAIDs, opioids), hydration, strain urine for analysis. Stones <5mm have high passage rate.
  • Intervention: ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy), ureteroscopy, percutaneous nephrolithotomy for large or obstructing stones.
  • Prevention: Increase hydration, dietary modifications based on stone type, thiazide diuretics for hypercalciuria, allopurinol for uric acid stones.

4. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

Rapid loss of kidney function occurring over hours to days, characterized by rising creatinine and declining GFR. Causes are classified by location: before the kidneys, within the kidneys, or after the kidneys.

AKI TypeUrine NaFENaUrine AppearanceResponse to Fluids
PRE-renal (decreased perfusion)<20 mEq/L<1%Concentrated (oliguria)Improves with IV fluids
RENAL (intrinsic damage; ATN)>40 mEq/L>2%Muddy, brown (casts, cells)No response
POST-renal (obstruction)VariableVariableClear or hematuriaResolves with decompression
  • PRE-renal causes: Shock, dehydration, heart failure, sepsis.
  • RENAL causes: Acute tubular necrosis (ATN), glomerulonephritis, drug toxicity (aminoglycosides, NSAIDs, contrast agents).
  • POST-renal causes: Kidney stones, enlarged prostate, tumors blocking urine flow.
  • Management: Address the cause, monitor daily labs (Cr, BUN, K+), manage complications (hyperkalemia, fluid overload, acidosis). Consider dialysis if K+ >6.5, severe acidosis, or uremia.
  • Prognosis: Most pre-renal and post-renal AKI is reversible if caught early. Intrinsic renal damage (ATN) has longer recovery and higher mortality.
Clinical Pearl: Early identification and treatment are crucial. Most AKI from prerenal and post-renal causes is reversible if recognized early. Intrinsic renal damage requires supportive care during the recovery period.

5. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)

Progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function over months to years. Staged by GFR to guide interventions and predict outcomes. Early management slows progression.

StageGFR (mL/min/1.73m²)DescriptionKey Action
1≥90Normal/high GFR, kidney damage presentPrevent progression
260-89Mildly decreased GFRMonitor, manage risk factors
3a45-59Mildly to moderately decreasedEvaluate complications
3b30-44Moderately to severely decreasedPrepare for renal replacement
415-29Severely decreasedPlan for dialysis/transplant
5<15Kidney failure (ESRD)Renal replacement therapy
  • UREMIA mnemonic (advanced CKD): Uremia (elevated BUN/Cr), Restlessness/insomnia, Encephalopathy (confusion), Metabolic acidosis, Itching (pruritus), Anemia.
  • Conservative management (Stages 1-4): BP control (<130/80 mmHg; ACE-I/ARB preferred), proteinuria reduction, glucose control in diabetics, dietary modifications (protein, sodium, phosphorus), medication adjustments for renal function.
  • Mineral-bone management: Monitor calcium, phosphorus, PTH; vitamin D supplementation, phosphate binders.
  • Anemia management: ESAs (erythropoiesis-stimulating agents) if Hgb <10 g/dL.
  • Referral timing: Nephrology at GFR <30; dialysis centers at GFR <15 for access planning and patient education.
Red Flag: Rapid decline in GFR (>5 mL/min/year) warrants urgent investigation for superimposed AKI, drug toxicity, or acute glomerulonephritis.

Big Picture: How These Connect

  • Infection to CKD: Repeated or severe pyelonephritis → chronic renal scarring → progressive CKD.
  • Stones to AKI: Obstructive nephrolithiasis → acute tubular damage → reversible AKI (if decompressed early).
  • AKI to CKD: Severe/repeated AKI → incomplete renal recovery → progressive CKD.
  • Metabolic factors: Hypertension and diabetes damage glomeruli → proteinuria → progressive CKD.
  • Prevention is key: Catch UTI early to prevent pyelonephritis. Decompressing stones quickly prevents AKI. Aggressive management of CKD risk factors slows progression and delays need for dialysis/transplant.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What does DYSURIA stand for in UTI?
Dysuria, frequency/urgency, Suprapubic discomfort, Urgency, Residual urine, Incomplete emptying, Absent fever.
What is the most common UTI pathogen?
E. coli (gram-negative bacteria).
What does FLANK stand for in pyelonephritis?
Fever, Lumbar pain, Abdominal distension, Nausea/vomiting, Kidney tenderness on CVA percussion.
How does pyelonephritis differ from UTI?
Pyelonephritis has systemic symptoms (fever, chills) and involves the kidneys; UTI is localized to the bladder/urethra.
What is the C-CUP mnemonic for kidney stones?
Calcium oxalate (75%), Calcium phosphate, Uric acid, Phosphate (struvite).
What percentage of kidney stones are calcium oxalate?
~75% — the most common type.
What is the gold standard imaging for kidney stones?
CT noncontrast (CT without contrast).
What stone size has a high passage rate?
Stones <5mm have a high spontaneous passage rate with conservative management.
What does PRE-RENAL-POST stand for in AKI classification?
PRE-renal (decreased perfusion), RENAL (intrinsic damage), POST-renal (obstruction).
How do you distinguish pre-renal from renal (ATN) AKI?
PRE-renal: Urine Na <20, FENa <1%, responds to fluids. RENAL (ATN): Urine Na >40, FENa >2%, no response to fluids.
What is the most common cause of pre-renal AKI?
Decreased perfusion from shock, dehydration, heart failure, or sepsis.
What is the most reversible type of AKI?
Pre-renal AKI — if treated early, reverses quickly with fluid replacement or correction of the perfusion problem.
What is CKD?
Progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function over months to years, staged by GFR.
What is a normal GFR?
≥90 mL/min/1.73m² (Stage 1, or normal).
What does UREMIA stand for?
Uremia, Restlessness/insomnia, Encephalopathy, Metabolic acidosis, Itching (pruritus), Anemia.
What is the target BP in CKD?
<130/80 mmHg — achieved with ACE-I or ARB as first-line agents.
At what GFR should a patient be referred to nephrology?
GFR <30 (Stage 4 CKD) for specialist management.
At what GFR should dialysis/transplant planning begin?
GFR <15 (Stage 5 CKD) — patient education and vascular/peritoneal access planning.
How can repeated UTIs lead to CKD?
Repeated or severe pyelonephritis causes chronic renal scarring, which progressively damages kidney function over time.
What is the most important intervention to slow CKD progression?
Blood pressure control (especially with ACE-I/ARB) and proteinuria reduction — these have the strongest evidence for slowing decline.
