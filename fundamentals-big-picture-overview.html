Big Picture Overview

This page doesn't teach anything new — it's a map of the other 11 Fundamentals Review pages, so you can see how they fit together instead of studying each one like it's unrelated to the rest. A handful of big ideas (immobility, nutrition, oxygenation, communication, and the older adult who doesn't present by the textbook) keep resurfacing across different topics. This page shows where each idea is actually taught in full, and where it just comes back as a supporting detail. No flashcards or quiz questions here — just the map.

Five Clusters, Not Eleven Separate Topics

Eleven pages is a lot to hold in your head at once. All of them fall into one of five groups — knowing the group can matter more than memorizing all eleven titles.

ClusterTopicsThe Common Thread
The Professional FrameLegal & Ethical Issues; Nursing Process, Clinical Judgment & SBARNot body systems at all — the rules you practice under and the thinking process you run every patient through. Every other topic quietly assumes both.
Airway, Breathing & CirculationOxygenation & Tracheostomy; Cardiovascular; Neurosensory & PainThe assessment you perform on everyone: getting oxygen in, moving it around the body, and reading the neuro status and pain that tell you whether it's working. What you assess first, and what goes wrong fastest.
Intake & MetabolismObesity & Metabolic Syndrome; Diabetic Care & NutritionWhat the body takes in and how it uses it. These two overlap more than any other pair in the review — metabolic syndrome is diagnosed off the same waist, glucose, lipid and blood pressure numbers that drive diabetes care.
Elimination & SkinUrinary & Bowel Elimination; IntegumentaryThe two systems that go wrong when a patient stops moving. Immobility, incontinence, and poor nutrition feed the same set of complications from both directions.
Care Beyond the AdmissionOlder Adults & Discharge Planning; Palliative, Hospice & End of LifeBoth look past this shift — who the patient is, what happens when they leave, and what happens when they aren't going to get better. Both are far heavier on communication than on procedure.

If you're not sure where to start a review pass, working through the clusters in this order touches every recurring theme once before moving on, instead of jumping between unrelated systems.

High-Alert Safety Rules — All in One Place

Every danger callout across the 11 pages, condensed to a phrase. This is meant for a quick scan, not a replacement for the full explanation — click through to a topic for the details behind any rule.

TopicThe Hard Rules
Legal & Ethical IssuesA DNR order does not mean "do not care" — it means only that CPR won't be started if breathing or the heart stops. Every other aspect of care continues as normal.
Oxygenation & TracheostomyOxygen toxicity is a real complication of too much supplemental oxygen — use the lowest FiO2 that achieves the result. Watch for chest pain or heaviness, coughing, dyspnea, muscle twitching, nausea, seizures, and visual changes.
CardiovascularHypertensive crisis — systolic over 180 and/or diastolic over 120 — is a medical emergency requiring the patient to see a provider immediately.
Diabetic Care & NutritionA random glucose over 300 mg/dL is a medical emergency. Insulin is high-alert: never give it without a current blood glucose, and first confirm the insulin's onset/peak/duration, the diet order and NPO status, and how you'd recognize hypoglycemia.
IntegumentaryEvisceration is an emergency — stay with the patient, cover the organs with a sterile saline-soaked dressing (never reinsert), position supine with knees bent, keep NPO, and notify the provider; surgery is likely.
Neurosensory & PainOpioid respiratory depression is highest risk in opioid-naive patients and with concurrent benzodiazepines. ABCs first, then naloxone — its half-life is shorter than most opioids, so repeat dosing and close monitoring are often needed, and reversal also reverses the pain control.
Older Adults & Discharge PlanningNew-onset confusion is often the first or only sign of infection or acute illness — a UTI, pneumonia, or even an MI may present as confusion rather than fever, bladder pain or chest pain. Always investigate it.

Patterns you'll notice across these rules

  • "Know the current number before you give the drug." Insulin requires a current blood glucose, not an earlier one (Diabetic Care & Nutrition), and an opioid requires a known baseline respiratory and neuro status (Neurosensory & Pain). Two different drugs, the same reflex: verify the state you're dosing into.
  • "More isn't safer." Oxygen has a toxicity ceiling (Oxygenation & Tracheostomy), and the naloxone that reverses respiratory depression also reverses the pain control you were trying to provide (Neurosensory & Pain). Both teach that the treatment carries its own dose-dependent cost.
  • "Airway and breathing come before the thing you were called about." ABCs before naloxone, ABCs before the neuro exam, and airway patency as the first priority when a trach comes out — the same ordering appears in three separate topics.
  • "In an older adult, the textbook presentation is the exception." Confusion instead of fever with a UTI, confusion instead of chest pain with an MI. It's why atypical presentation gets its own section rather than a footnote, and why it also shows up in the elimination and neuro content.
  • "The conversation is the intervention." NURSE statements at end of life, weight-sensitive assessment in obesity care, and discharge teaching for an older adult are all places where what you say is the nursing care, not a preamble to it.
