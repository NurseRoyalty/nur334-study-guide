Hepatitis

Inflammation of the liver. Week 7 focuses on viral hepatitis A, B, and C. Many people have no symptoms: about 30% with hepatitis B and up to 80% with hepatitis C don't know they have it. Non-viral causes exist too, such as too much acetaminophen (hepatotoxic).

Jaundice

  • Yellow sclera, skin, or hard palate — always from too much bilirubin in the blood; visible when serum bilirubin is above about 2.5
  • A symptom, not a disease — find the cause
  • Hemolytic: excess RBC breakdown (e.g., a hemolytic transfusion reaction)
  • Obstructive: intrahepatic (inside the liver) or extrahepatic (e.g., a gallstone stuck in the common bile duct while the liver itself is fine)
  • Obstructive jaundice → dark urine (bilirubin excreted by the kidneys) and clay-colored stools (no bilirubin reaching the intestine)

Care and Teaching for Hepatitis

  • No specific treatment — the emphasis is rest, usually at home
  • No special diet: well-balanced with adequate calories; low-fat only if fat isn't tolerated; vitamin supplements (B complex, vitamin K)
  • Itching is common and frustrating → diphenhydramine
  • Avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs — especially acetaminophen and isoniazid
  • Notify possible contacts for testing
  • Prevention: good sanitation and hygiene; in under-developed areas drink purified or bottled water, avoid foods washed in tap water and ice
  • Don't share linens, utensils, glasses, needles, razors, nail clippers, or toothbrushes; use condoms; cover cuts
  • With hepatitis C: never donate blood, organs, or tissue

Cirrhosis

Early manifestations

  • Onset is insidious
  • Weight loss, weakness
  • GI upset: anorexia, gas, change in bowel habits, nausea and vomiting
  • Hepatomegaly

Late manifestations

  • Jaundice
  • Low albumin → fluid shifts out of the vessels → ascites
  • Low prothrombin → prolonged bleeding times → bleeding risk
  • Portal hypertension → ascites, splenomegaly, spider angiomas, caput medusae, esophageal and anorectal varices
  • Hepatic encephalopathy and asterixis (liver flap)
Portal hypertension works like heart failure of the liver: blood can't get through the scarred liver, so it backs up. Everything behind it becomes engorged — spleen, abdominal veins, esophageal and rectal veins — and fluid is pushed into the peritoneum (ascites). Low albumin makes the ascites worse.

Managing Ascites and Fluid Volume Excess

  • Rest first
  • Sodium restriction and often a fluid restriction (water follows salt)
  • Diuretics, often two: spironolactone (potassium-sparing) and a loop diuretic such as furosemide (potassium-losing)
  • Albumin may be given to raise oncotic pressure and pull fluid back into the vessels
  • Measure abdominal girth — mark the skin so the tape goes in the same place. Daily weights are the more accurate indicator
  • Paracentesis when ascites impairs breathing: informed consent, baseline vitals, have the patient void first (avoid puncturing the bladder), position supine or high Fowler's. Often repeated
  • TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt): non-surgical stent that lowers portal pressure → less ascites and less variceal bleeding

Nutrition

  • High-calorie, high-carbohydrate, moderate-to-low fat
  • Restrict protein only with severe encephalopathy — otherwise the benefits of protein outweigh the risks
  • Low sodium with fluid volume excess or ascites

Bleeding Precautions

  • Monitor coagulation labs
  • Check the gums first; watch for ecchymosis, purpura, and petechiae
  • Prevent falls (risk of head injury and intracranial bleeding)
  • No aspirin; avoid alcohol and spicy or bulky foods
  • Avoid injections; if one is given, hold pressure much longer than usual
  • Avoid vigorous nose blowing and straining with bowel movements — can rupture varices → stool softeners
  • Soft toothbrush; no rectal temperatures or enemas

Esophageal Varices: Active Bleeding

  • A bleeding varix is an emergency — you can't hold pressure on it, and clotting is already poor
  • Endoscopic sclerotherapy: a sclerosing agent is injected to stop the bleeding
  • Endoscopic variceal ligation (banding): rubber bands around the base of the varices cut off their blood supply
  • Balloon tamponade (Blakemore tube): an inflated balloon applies direct pressure to the bleeding varices; the patient goes to the ICU

Hepatic Encephalopathy

A reversible neuropsychiatric result of a failing liver, caused mainly by too much serum ammonia. The liver normally converts ammonia to urea for excretion; when it can't, ammonia crosses the blood–brain barrier and acts as a sedative. Progression: mild confusion and slowed responses → lethargy and stupor → hepatic coma.

  • Asterixis (liver flap): with the wrists flexed back, the hands flap involuntarily — indicates elevated ammonia
  • Goal: lower the ammonia
  • Lactulose, titrated to 2–4 stools per day — the stools remove ammonia. Teach the patient why, so they don't refuse a dose after one bowel movement
  • Avoid constipation — it increases ammonia
  • Control GI bleeding — blood in the gut is nitrogen-rich and drives ammonia up
  • Protein restricted only in severe (end-stage) encephalopathy
  • Monitor level of consciousness; safety precautions for confusion; patient and family teaching
Cirrhosis red flags: any bleeding from the mouth or vomiting blood (varices) · worsening confusion or asterixis (rising ammonia) · increasing abdominal girth with shortness of breath (ascites). And: no acetaminophen, no alcohol, no aspirin.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

How are hepatitis A, B, and C transmitted?
A: fecal–oral. B and C: infected blood, high-risk behaviors, and perinatal exposure.
Which types of viral hepatitis have a vaccine?
A and B. There is no vaccine for hepatitis C.
How soon must post-exposure prophylaxis be given for hepatitis A and B?
Hepatitis A: within 2 weeks. Hepatitis B: within 24 hours (no benefit after 7 days).
Which hepatitis type is most likely to become chronic, and which never does?
Hepatitis C has a strong tendency to become chronic. Hepatitis A never does (it is self-limiting).
What is the main treatment for acute viral hepatitis?
Rest at home, a balanced diet with adequate calories, and avoiding alcohol and hepatotoxic drugs (acetaminophen, isoniazid).
A client with hepatitis is very itchy. Why, and what helps?
Excess bile salts cause the itching; diphenhydramine is usually given.
How does portal hypertension lead to ascites, splenomegaly, and varices?
Blood backs up behind the scarred liver, engorging the spleen and veins and pushing fluid into the peritoneum; low albumin makes the ascites worse.
Before a paracentesis, what must the nurse have the client do?
Void, so the bladder isn't punctured. Also obtain consent and baseline vital signs.
Which two diuretics are often given together for ascites?
Spironolactone (potassium-sparing) and a loop diuretic such as furosemide (potassium-losing).
A client with cirrhosis is confused and has asterixis. What is the cause and the usual drug?
Elevated serum ammonia (hepatic encephalopathy). Lactulose, titrated to 2–4 stools per day.
When is protein restricted in cirrhosis?
Only with severe encephalopathy. Otherwise the diet is high-calorie, high-carbohydrate, moderate-to-low fat.
A client's esophageal varices start bleeding. Which treatments may be used?
Endoscopic sclerotherapy, variceal banding, or balloon tamponade with a Blakemore tube (ICU).
