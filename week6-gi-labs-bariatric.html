GI Diagnostic Tests at a Glance

The lecture groups the GI tests into four families: radiology (upper GI/small bowel follow-through, barium swallow, barium enema), nuclear imaging (gastric emptying study), CT and MRI, scopes (EGD for the upper tract, colonoscopy for the lower tract), and stool tests (occult blood, culture, ova and parasites).

Scopes: EGD and Colonoscopy

EGD (upper scope)

  • EGD = esophagogastroduodenoscopy: scope through the mouth to look at the upper GI tract
  • Looks for bleeding, tumors, inflammation, hiatal hernia, ulcers, obstruction
  • Can be therapeutic: cauterize an active bleed or esophageal varices
  • Done in the endoscopy suite under conscious sedation with the throat numbed
  • Before: NPO 8 hours; signed consent
  • After: NPO until the gag reflex returns (about 2–4 hours) because of aspiration/choking risk
  • Watch for bleeding and perforation (rare but life-threatening): monitor vital signs

Colonoscopy (lower scope)

  • Scope of the colon
  • Bowel prep is essential (e.g., GoLYTELY). A poor prep means stool is left in the colon and the test must be repeated
  • Patient is sedated; lots of air is used, so expect gas and burping afterward
  • Watch for perforation and bleeding: blood from the rectum, vital sign changes, a newly tender abdomen
  • Colonoscopy is also the gold standard for colorectal cancer screening (see page 4)

Stool Tests

  • Stool for occult blood (guaiac): patient is often told to avoid red meat 24–48 hours before; the lecture notes the evidence for this is debatable, but follow the provider's order
  • Stool culture: checks for bacteria, including C. difficile (C. diff)
  • Ova and parasites (O&P): looks for parasites
Safety after an EGD: the throat is numbed, so nothing by mouth until the gag reflex is back (about 2–4 hours). Giving fluids too early risks aspiration and choking.

Bariatric Surgery

Surgery is currently the only treatment shown to produce lasting weight loss in severe obesity. Bariatric surgery = surgery on the stomach or intestines to help a person with extreme obesity lose weight.

Who qualifies

  • BMI ≥ 40, or
  • BMI ≥ 35 with one or more severe obesity-related conditions: hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, sleep apnea
  • Psychological, physical, and behavioral screening first (can take about 6 months)

Two mechanisms

  • Restrictive: the person eats less (smaller stomach)
  • Malabsorptive: less food is absorbed (shorter small intestine)
  • Roux-en-Y is both restrictive and malabsorptive
ProcedureTypeKey points
Sleeve gastrectomyRestrictiveGreater curvature of the stomach removed and stapled → tube-shaped stomach. Permanent.
Intragastric balloonRestrictive (temporary, minimally invasive)Soft saline balloon (about 400–700 mL) takes up room and promotes satiety. Usually removed by 6 months because of leak risk. Three FDA-approved devices.
Roux-en-Y gastric bypassRestrictive + malabsorptiveGold standard and most common. Pouch is only about 15 mL. Intestines rerouted; the rest of the stomach is bypassed (left in, not removed). Stay 2–3 days; 60–70% weight loss in 1–2 years. Permanent.

Roux-en-Y: good outcomes

  • Better glucose tolerance, less diabetes
  • Lower blood pressure
  • Better cholesterol and triglycerides
  • Improved GERD
  • Improved sleep apnea (less neck adipose tissue)

Roux-en-Y: adverse outcomes

  • Malabsorption of vitamins and iron → lifelong vitamin supplementation
  • Dumping syndrome (see page 2)

Post-op Nursing Care

  • Control pain so the patient can mobilize
  • Be vigilant for wound infection, dehiscence, and evisceration: patients with severe obesity are at higher risk for both infection and dehiscence
  • NG to wall suction at first to decompress and rest the stomach
  • Diet progression in very small amounts: water and sugar-free liquids → high-protein liquids → pureed → solid food
  • Extensive teaching for the patient, family, and anyone involved in resuming foods and liquids
  • Long-term diet: DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension), in smaller portions
DASH diet (as presented): 6–8 servings of whole grains daily · lots of vegetables and fruits · 2–3 servings of low-fat or fat-free dairy · 4–5 servings per week of nuts, seeds, legumes · fewer than 6 servings a day of lean meat, poultry, fish · limit desserts, fats, and oils.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

A client with a suspected perforation needs an upper GI series. Which contrast is used, and why not barium?
Gastrografin. Barium is avoided when perforation is suspected because we don't want barium in the peritoneum.
A client returns from an upper GI with small bowel follow-through. What two things should the nurse teach?
Drink plenty of fluids (barium is constipating), and expect white stool — that's normal.
Why might a client who had a stroke be sent for a modified barium swallow?
To evaluate dysphagia with different food consistencies (thin liquids, thickened liquids, applesauce). Passing it means the client can start eating again.
What prep is needed before a barium enema?
Bowel cleared of stool: enemas until clear, clear liquids the night before, then NPO. Cathartics may be given afterward to move the barium out.
A client with diabetes is scheduled for a gastric emptying study. What is it testing and what should the nurse explain?
How quickly the stomach empties (gastroparesis). The egg has only a trace of radioactivity and isn't dangerous; the client lies flat during scanning.
A client just had an EGD and asks for a drink of water. What should the nurse do?
Keep the client NPO until the gag reflex returns (about 2–4 hours) — the throat was numbed, so there's a risk of aspiration and choking.
After an EGD, the client's heart rate climbs and the client reports new abdominal pain. What complication is the nurse worried about?
Perforation (rare but life-threatening) or bleeding. Monitor vital signs closely and notify the provider.
Why is bowel prep so important before a colonoscopy?
If stool remains in the colon, the provider can't see the mucosa and the test has to be repeated.
What is a stool culture checking for?
Bacteria, including C. difficile. Stool can also be sent for ova and parasites.
A client with a BMI of 36 and sleep apnea asks if they qualify for bariatric surgery. How should the nurse answer?
Possibly: criteria are BMI ≥40, or BMI ≥35 with at least one severe obesity-related condition (HTN, diabetes, HF, sleep apnea), after psychological, physical, and behavioral screening.
How does a Roux-en-Y differ from a sleeve gastrectomy?
Roux-en-Y is restrictive AND malabsorptive (15 mL pouch + rerouted intestine; stomach bypassed, not removed). A sleeve is restrictive only (part of the stomach removed).
Why is an intragastric balloon usually removed by 6 months?
Leak risk increases after that. It's a temporary, restrictive procedure (400–700 mL saline) that promotes fullness.
Why does a client after Roux-en-Y need vitamin supplements for life?
The procedure causes malabsorption, so vitamins and iron are lost.
Why is a client with severe obesity at higher risk after bariatric surgery?
Greater risk for wound infection and for dehiscence/evisceration — assess the incision vigilantly.
What is the diet progression after bariatric surgery?
NG to suction first, then small amounts of water and sugar-free liquids → high-protein liquids → pureed → solid food. Long-term: DASH diet in smaller portions.
Which health problems often improve after a Roux-en-Y?
Glucose tolerance/diabetes, blood pressure, cholesterol and triglycerides, GERD, and sleep apnea.
