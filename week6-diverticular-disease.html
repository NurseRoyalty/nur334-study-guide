Diverticulosis & Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis vs Diverticulitis

DiverticulosisDiverticulitis
What it isOut-pouchings (diverticula) in the intestinal mucosa — a condition you have all the timeA flare: inflammation of the diverticula (-itis = inflammation); an exacerbation of diverticulosis
SymptomsUsually asymptomatic; maybe stomach pain, bloating, gas, change in bowel habitsIncreased pain, usually in the left lower quadrant; possibly a palpable abdominal mass; signs of infection
Older adults—May not show typical signs of infection — may present with confusion
CarePrevention and diet (below)Can be very sick: NPO to rest the gut; if severe, hospitalize with IV fluids and antibiotics

Prevention and Living With Diverticulosis

To prevent diverticulosis

  • High-fiber, low-fat, low-red-meat diet
  • High level of physical activity

If you have diverticulosis

  • Continue a high-fiber diet
  • Nuts and seeds do NOT need to be avoided — older teaching said they could get stuck in the pouches, but there's no evidence. Avoid them only if they cause flares for that person
  • Weight reduction if indicated
  • Avoid anything that raises intra-abdominal pressure — e.g., constipation
Fiber flip-flop: diverticulosis → high fiber. IBD flare → low residue (low fiber) to calm the gut. Know which patient you're teaching.

Diverticulitis: Complications and Monitoring

  • Monitor for abscess, bleeding, and peritonitis
  • A ruptured diverticulum spills stool into the peritoneum → peritonitis (watch for a rigid, board-like abdomen and rebound tenderness)
  • Surgery if complications occur: bowel resection, possibly with a temporary ostomy
Don't be reassured by a normal temperature in an older adult. A confused older client with LLQ pain may have diverticulitis even without the usual signs of infection.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What is the difference between diverticulosis and diverticulitis?
Diverticulosis = out-pouchings in the intestinal wall (chronic, often asymptomatic). Diverticulitis = inflammation of those pouches (a flare).
Where is the pain of diverticulitis usually located?
Left lower quadrant.
A 79-year-old with LLQ tenderness is newly confused but has no fever. What should the nurse suspect?
Possible diverticulitis — older adults often lack typical signs of infection and may present with confusion.
What diet helps prevent diverticulosis?
High fiber, low fat, low red meat — plus a high level of physical activity.
A client with diverticulosis asks whether to stop eating nuts and seeds. How should the nurse respond?
There's no evidence they need to be avoided; avoid them only if they seem to cause flares.
Why should a client with diverticulosis avoid constipation?
It raises intra-abdominal pressure.
A client is admitted with a severe diverticulitis flare. What care should the nurse anticipate?
NPO to rest the gut, IV fluids, IV antibiotics, and monitoring for abscess, bleeding, and peritonitis.
What happens if a diverticulum ruptures?
Stool spills into the peritoneum → peritonitis. Watch for a rigid, board-like abdomen and rebound tenderness.
When is surgery needed for diverticulitis, and what might it involve?
For complications — bowel resection, possibly with a temporary ostomy.
Diverticulosis vs an IBD flare: which gets high fiber and which gets low residue?
Diverticulosis → high fiber. IBD flare (after NPO) → low residue to keep the gut calm.
Which intervention do diverticulitis, an IBD flare, and a bowel obstruction share?
Resting the gut — NPO (with IV fluids).
A client with diverticulosis is overweight. What additional teaching applies?
Weight reduction — it helps lower intra-abdominal pressure.
