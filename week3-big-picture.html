Week 3: Big Picture Overview

Perioperative Nursing & Electrolyte Management

🔍 The Big Picture: Surgical Risk & Cellular Health

Week 3 is about two things that seem separate but are deeply connected:

1. PERIOPERATIVE NURSING — preparing patients for surgery, managing them during, and supporting recovery
2. ELECTROLYTE/FLUID BALANCE — keeping cells healthy by maintaining the right osmolarity and ion concentrations

Why they're connected: Surgery creates stress. Stress causes shifts in fluids and electrolytes. Electrolyte imbalances cause cellular dysfunction and complications. Your job is to prevent those shifts BEFORE they cause damage.

The Core Principle: Homeostasis
Surgery disrupts homeostasis. Your nursing care must maintain (or restore) it through careful fluid management, electrolyte monitoring, and complication prevention.

🏥 Perioperative Nursing: Three Phases, One Goal (Safety)

Think of the perioperative period as a patient journey through THREE critical phases. Each phase has specific nursing priorities:

PHASE 1: PREOPERATIVE (Before Anesthesia)

Goal: Prepare patient SAFELY for surgery. Minimize risk of complications DURING surgery.

  • NPO Status: Solids 6+ hours, clear liquids 2+ hours (prevents aspiration during anesthesia)
  • Informed Consent: Legal requirement. Patient must understand risks, benefits, alternatives
  • Pre-op Assessment: Labs (CBC, BMP, coagulation), vitals, allergies, medications, cardiac/respiratory status
  • Medications: Hold vs give? Cardiac meds yes, herbal supplements no, anticoagulation protocol-dependent
  • Patient Education: What to expect, pain control options, breathing/coughing exercises, leg exercises
  • Emotional Support: Anxiety is normal. Calm reassurance decreases surgical stress response

PHASE 2: INTRAOPERATIVE (During Surgery)

Goal: Maintain patient SAFETY, POSITIONING, and HEMODYNAMIC STABILITY during procedure.

  • Anesthesia Types: General (asleep), regional (numb one area), local (small area), twilight (sedated)
  • Circulating Nurse Role: Coordinates OR, manages equipment, maintains sterility, documents
  • Scrub Nurse Role: Handles instruments, maintains sterile field, anticipates surgeon needs
  • Patient Positioning: Supine, prone, lateral, lithotomy — each has risks (nerve compression, pressure ulcers, breathing compromise)
  • Fluid Management: IV fluids given during surgery. Monitor input/output, electrolytes, blood loss
  • Thermoregulation: Hypothermia is common in OR (cold, anesthesia reduces heat production)

PHASE 3: POSTOPERATIVE (After Surgery - Critical!)

Goal: Support RECOVERY, PREVENT COMPLICATIONS, MANAGE PAIN.

  • PACU (Post-Anesthesia Care Unit): Immediate recovery. Monitor ABC, vitals, consciousness level, pain
  • Common Complications: Nausea/vomiting, respiratory depression, hypotension, hypothermia, pain, urinary retention, infection
  • Early Ambulation: Prevents blood clots, pneumonia, muscle weakness. Start as soon as safe
  • Breathing/Coughing: Incentive spirometer prevents atelectasis (collapsed airways)
  • Pain Management: Adequate pain control improves mobility, healing, psychological well-being
  • Wound Care: Monitor for infection, dehiscence (opening), hematoma
  • Discharge Planning: Clear instructions on activity, medications, wound care, warning signs

🚨 Malignant Hyperthermia: The OR Emergency You Must Know

⚠️ LIFE-THREATENING pharmacogenetic disorder triggered by anesthetics
Most dangerous thing that can happen in an OR. You must recognize it EARLY.

What Causes It: Patient's muscles have genetic defect → anesthetic triggers uncontrolled muscle contractions → extreme heat generation → muscle breakdown → kidney failure → death

EARLY Signs (First 30 minutes — catch it EARLY):

  • Jaw clenching, muscle rigidity
  • Tachycardia (often FIRST sign)
  • Tachypnea, increased ETCO2
  • Skin mottling, cyanosis
  • Do NOT wait for fever — FEVER IS LATE

LATE Signs (30 minutes - hours):

  • High fever (104-106°F+)
  • Dark, cola-colored urine (myoglobinuria from muscle breakdown)
  • Rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalemia, acidosis, DIC
  • Acute kidney injury, cardiac arrest
STOP-MH Action Protocol (MEMORIZE THIS):
Stop anesthesia immediately
Tell provider/call for help
Oxygen 100%
Prepare dantrolene (2.5 mg/kg IV, repeat q5min until resolved)
Monitor vitals/labs/urine closely
Hyperventilate to blow off CO2

Postoperative: ICU admission, continue dantrolene 1mg/kg q4-6h for 24-48h, watch for recrudescence (happens again)

Patient Education: Genetic testing, MedAlert bracelet for LIFE, inform all healthcare providers (dentists, surgeons)

⚡ Electrolytes: Why Cells Live or Die

Electrolytes are the CURRENCY of cellular function.

They create electrical gradients that allow nerves to fire, muscles to contract, and the heart to beat. Surgery disrupts electrolytes. Anesthesia disrupts them. Medications disrupted them. Your job is to prevent (or recognize and fix) imbalances BEFORE they cause organ dysfunction.

The Big Five Electrolytes:

Electrolyte Normal Range Hypo (Low) Problem Hyper (High) Problem
SODIUM (Na+) 135-145 Cell swelling → cerebral edema, seizures, coma Cell shrinkage → brain shrinks, intracranial bleed
POTASSIUM (K+) 3.5-5.0 Heart won't depolarize → weak heartbeat, arrhythmias Heart hyperexcitable → dangerous arrhythmias, arrest
CALCIUM (Ca2+) 8.5-10.5 Neurons fire too easily → tetany, seizures Neurons don't fire → weakness, cardiac arrhythmias
MAGNESIUM (Mg2+) 1.7-2.2 Often missed! Arrhythmias, refractory K/Ca imbalances Muscle weakness, paralysis, respiratory depression
PHOSPHATE (PO4) 2.5-4.5 Muscle weakness, RBC fragility, hemolysis Metastatic calcification, secondary hyperparathyroidism
Clinical Pearl: Refractory Hypokalemia or Hypocalcemia?
Check magnesium FIRST. You can't fix K or Ca if Mg is low. Always correct Mg in the right sequence.

💧 Fluid Balance: The Other Side of the Coin

Electrolytes need to be in fluid to work. Too much fluid or too little disrupts electrolyte function.

Fluid Volume Excess (FVE)

  • What's wrong: Too much total body water
  • Cause: Too much IV fluid, heart/kidney disease, SIADH, excessive salt intake
  • Signs: Edema, weight gain, crackles (pulmonary edema), JVD (jugular venous distension), hypertension
  • Treatment: Restrict fluids, diuretics, sodium restriction

Fluid Volume Deficit (FVD)

  • What's wrong: Not enough total body water
  • Cause: Vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, insufficient intake, bleeding
  • Signs: Dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, weak pulses, orthostatic hypotension, oliguria
  • Treatment: IV fluids, increase oral intake, treat underlying cause

Perioperative Context: Surgery causes fluid shifts. Patient loses fluid through blood loss and insensible loss (breathing, sweating). You must replace it without overdoing it (FVE). Careful IV management is KEY.

🔗 How It All Connects: NCLEX-NGN Clinical Judgment

Scenario 1: 68-year-old 4 hours post-op from abdominal surgery

  • Vital signs: HR 110, BP 95/58, RR 22, temp 36.2°C (hypothermia)
  • Labs: K+ 3.2, Na+ 128, Hgb 8.5
  • Recognition: Hypokalemia (weak heart), hyponatremia (dilutional from too much free water), anemia (blood loss)
  • Actions: IV fluids (not hypotonic!), K+ replacement, monitor cardiac rhythm, consider blood transfusion, warm patient

Scenario 2: Patient in OR suddenly develops jaw clenching, tachycardia 130, ETCO2 rising

  • Recognition: STOP — this is malignant hyperthermia until proven otherwise
  • Actions: STOP anesthesia, call for help, O2 100%, prepare dantrolene, hyperventilate, monitor everything
  • Why early recognition matters: Catch it in first 30 min = better outcome. Wait for fever = too late, damage done

Scenario 3: Patient 2 days post-op with confusion, seizure, Na+ 120

  • Recognition: Hyponatremia (from SIADH common post-op, or too much free water)
  • Danger: Cerebral edema, seizure risk
  • Action: Do NOT correct too fast (will cause osmotic demyelination). Correct SLOWLY (8-12 mEq/24h), fluid restriction, hypertonic saline if symptomatic

Scenario 4: Patient post-op with crackles, JVD, weight gain 8 lbs in 24h

  • Recognition: FVE from too much IV fluid during/after surgery
  • Actions: Restrict fluids, monitor I&O closely, diuretics as ordered, daily weights, lung sounds

📝 Key Takeaways

  • ✓ Three perioperative phases: Preop (prepare), intraop (maintain safety), postop (prevent complications)
  • ✓ NPO status prevents aspiration: Solids 6h, clear liquids 2h — this is SAFETY
  • ✓ Malignant hyperthermia = OR EMERGENCY: Jaw clenching + tachycardia = STOP, not fever
  • ✓ STOP-MH protocol: Stop anesthesia, tell, oxygen, prepare dantrolene, monitor, hyperventilate
  • ✓ Electrolytes create electrical gradients: Imbalances cause organ dysfunction (especially cardiac, neuro)
  • ✓ K+ is cardiac critical: Low K = weak heart, high K = dangerous arrhythmias
  • ✓ Na+ controls osmolarity: Hypo = cell swells (seizures), Hyper = cell shrinks (bleed)
  • ✓ Correct imbalances SLOWLY: Too fast correction causes more problems than the original imbalance
  • ✓ Refractory hypo-K or Ca? Check Mg first. Always.
  • ✓ Early ambulation prevents complications: Clots, pneumonia, weakness
  • ✓ Pain management improves recovery: Adequate analgesia = better mobility, healing, psychology
