Urinary & Bowel Elimination

Covers both elimination systems: urinary retention/incontinence, UTIs and CAUTIs, catheter types and care, then bowel elimination problems (constipation, impaction, diarrhea, C. diff, incontinence, hemorrhoids), GI assessment, and NG tube/bowel training care.

Urinary Elimination Basics & Factors Affecting It

  • Micturition (voiding) is a complex process involving the bladder, urinary sphincters, and CNS: the bladder contracts while the urethral sphincter and pelvic floor relax. The brain can respond immediately or delay the urge briefly.
  • Factors affecting urinary elimination: growth/development (bladder control develops around 18–24 months), sociocultural factors, psychological factors (anxiety preventing pelvic floor relaxation), personal habits/privacy, fluid intake (caffeine/alcohol increase output), pathologic conditions (CNS disorders, diabetes, MS, stroke, spinal cord injury, dementia, BPH), surgery/anesthesia (can cause retention), medications (diuretics, anticholinergics, sedatives/hypnotics), and diagnostic exams (cystoscopy, catheterization — infection/trauma risk).
  • Older adults: decreased nephrons, decreased muscle tone, decreased bladder capacity, a shortened interval between the first urge and an urgent need to void, increased bladder irritability/contractions during filling, and higher risk of urinary incontinence.

Urinary Retention & UTIs/CAUTIs

  • Urinary retention = inability to partially or completely empty the bladder — pressure, discomfort, pain/tenderness over the symphysis pubis, restlessness, diaphoresis. Can be acute or chronic (chronic: decreased stream volume, straining, frequency, urgency, incomplete-emptying sensation).
  • Diagnosed via post-void residual (PVR) — urine remaining in the bladder after voiding, measured by bladder scanner (ultrasonography, less invasive) or straight catheterization. Bladder scanning is an independent nursing intervention.
  • UTIs: most commonly caused by E. coli. Upper UTI = kidney infection (flank tenderness); lower UTI = bladder/urethra infection (dysuria, urgency, frequency, suprapubic tenderness, foul-smelling/cloudy urine). Bacteriuria alone (without symptoms) is usually not treated with antibiotics. Older adults may present atypically — confusion, decreased functional status, delirium, falls, with or without fever.
  • Risk factors for UTI: indwelling catheter, urinary retention, urinary/fecal incontinence, poor perineal hygiene, shorter female urethra, frequent intercourse, uncircumcised status.
  • CAUTI (catheter-associated UTI) — a major hospital-acquired complication tied directly to catheter presence and dwell time; hospitals are not reimbursed for treating a CAUTI. Prevention: hand hygiene, sterile insertion technique, proper securement, and prompt removal once no longer needed.

Urinary Incontinence

  • Urinary incontinence = involuntary loss of urine; often multifactorial.
  • Overflow: poor bladder emptying or obstruction (e.g. BPH) — dribbling/leakage.
  • Stress: leakage with effort/exertion (laughing, coughing, sneezing) — often women, related to weakened pelvic floor muscles (e.g. after childbirth) or sphincter injury.
  • Urge: an overactive bladder that involuntarily contracts, often with too little warning to reach the bathroom in time — common in older adults, sometimes idiopathic.
  • Functional: inability to reach the bathroom in time for unrelated reasons (mobility limitations, needing a cane, etc.).
  • Risk factors: more common in women and older adults; men with BPH; obesity, multiple pregnancies/vaginal births, neurologic disorders (Parkinson's, CVA, spinal cord injury, MS), certain medications, confusion/dementia, immobility, depression.

Urinary Assessment: I&O, Urine Characteristics & Testing

  • Normal urine output: greater than 30 mL/hr. Concerning if less than 30 mL/hr for more than 2 hours. Also a concern if an awake patient hasn't voided in 6 hours.
  • Color: normal is pale straw to amber (concentration-dependent — first morning void is often darker). Hematuria = blood in urine (bright red to light pink). Medications can also change color (blue, orange, green).
  • Clarity: normal is transparent — urine that sits gets cloudy, so assess freshly voided urine when possible. Thick/cloudy at voiding suggests bacteria/WBCs.
  • Odor: normal is odorless or a slight ammonia smell; an offensive odor may indicate UTI. Odor strengthens the longer urine sits.
  • Urinalysis (UA): collect a freshly voided or catheter specimen (clamp near the insertion site for a catheter sample — never take urine from the drainage bag). Urine culture and sensitivity: clean-catch midstream specimen; send within 30 minutes; preliminary results in ~24 hours; report identifies the organism and antibiotic susceptibility/resistance.
  • KUB (kidney-ureter-bladder) X-ray: evaluates size/shape/symmetry/structures and can detect urinary calculi (kidney stones); no special prep (not NPO).

Nursing Care for Urinary Problems

  • Fluid intake goal: about 2,300 mL/day if no fluid restriction is needed — helps flush solutes and limit bladder irritability. Stop fluids ~2 hours before bedtime to prevent nocturia.
  • Retention care: assess for bladder distention, assist to a normal voiding position, run water/flush the commode, apply a cold compress to the lower abdomen, encourage double voiding to help fully empty the bladder. Crede's method requires a provider order. Intermittent catheterization may be needed if the bladder won't empty.
  • Infection prevention: follow hospital protocol, assess for signs/symptoms of infection, perform good perineal hygiene, encourage regular voiding and adequate fluids, and teach front-to-back wiping for females.
  • Incontinence care: be respectful of the patient's feelings, consider pelvic floor muscle training, lifestyle changes, bladder retraining/toileting schedules, intermittent catheterization if needed, and meticulous skin care (identify/treat early, use appropriate barrier products, adequate hydration, involve wound-ostomy care as needed). Medications (anticholinergics) and surgery are later-line options, not first-line therapy.

Urinary Catheters: Types & Nursing Care

  • Single-lumen/intermittent ("in-and-out") catheter: no balloon — inserted, drains, removed.
  • Indwelling (Foley) catheter: stays in place with a balloon; some are temperature-sensing (very accurate, common in ICU).
  • Three-way/three-lumen: used for bladder irrigation. Coudé tip (curved): helps bypass an enlarged prostate in BPH patients.
  • Suprapubic catheter: placed through the abdominal wall directly into the bladder, sutured in place — used for urethral blockage, urologic surgery, or long-term catheter needs.
  • External catheters: PureWick (external suction device for females) and condom catheter (males) both reduce infection risk but are not foolproof — patients still need hourly checks to confirm they're actually dry, not a false sense of security.
  • Nursing care: regular pericare (front to back for females; retract foreskin for uncircumcised males), secure the catheter to prevent movement in either direction, empty the drainage bag when half full, prevent kinks, keep the bag below the level of the bladder and off the floor, maintain a closed drainage system, and reassess the need for the catheter every shift.
  • Post-removal: patients are at risk for retention — note the removal time; if not voided within 6–8 hours, bladder scan (in-and-out catheterize if >400 mL); monitor ability to void and measure output.

Bowel Elimination Basics & Factors Affecting It

  • Factors: age (bowel control develops around 2–3 years; older adults have more difficulty), diet/fiber, fluid intake, physical activity (sedentary → constipation risk), psychological/personal habits, positioning, pain (hemorrhoids, fissures, post-abdominal-surgery, postpartum), pregnancy, surgery/anesthesia (slows the gut; opioids compound this), and diagnostic tests.
  • Older adults: difficulty chewing, slowed esophageal emptying, impaired absorption, weakened sphincters, decreased HCl/lipase, slowed peristalsis, and decreased sensation to defecate.

Constipation & Impaction

  • Constipation is a symptom, not a disease — generally fewer than 3 bowel movements/week, but always ask the patient their normal pattern. Symptoms: infrequent stools, discomfort, bloating, hard/dry stools.
  • Causes: irregular habits, low fiber, low fluid intake, inactivity, stress, medications (especially opioids), advanced age, ignoring the urge to defecate, GI disorders. Untreated straining risks hemorrhoids, anal fissures, fecal impaction, and rectal prolapse.
  • Laxatives/cathartics (patho-pharm detail not required, but know the safety point): only bulk-forming agents (fiber/Metamucil) are safe for daily use. Emollients (docusate/Colace, stool softeners), osmotics, and stimulant cathartics should only be used short-term/PRN, not regularly — overuse can cause dependence.
  • Enema (NMA) care: position the patient left lateral Sims', have them retain the solution as long as possible, slow the infusion rate (lower the bag) if they cramp, and stop if the abdomen becomes rigid. Document amount instilled and results. Enemas are a later-line intervention (after bulk formers, then PO laxatives/softeners, then suppository). Risks: fluid/electrolyte imbalance, tissue trauma, vagal nerve stimulation (watch for bradycardia/syncope), abdominal pain/cramping, and perforation (a call-the-provider emergency).
  • Impaction = unrelieved constipation with inability to expel hardened, retained stool — can progress to bowel obstruction. Highest risk: debilitated patients, spinal cord injury, confused/unconscious patients. Watch for oozing liquid stool around a hard mass — easily mistaken for diarrhea; don't hold stool softeners/laxatives for this. Treatment may require digital removal (double-glove, lubricate, break up and remove stool in sections) — carries the same vagal/tissue-trauma risks as enemas.

Diarrhea & C. difficile

  • Diarrhea: loose, watery, frequent/urgent stools. Causes: foodborne pathogens, food intolerance/allergy, certain surgeries, diagnostic testing, and enteral tube feedings (a sign of feeding intolerance). Main complications: dehydration and skin irritation.
  • Antidiarrheals (e.g. loperamide, diphenoxylate/atropine) decrease intestinal motility — determine the cause first, since some causes (infection like C. diff, IBD flares) need treatment of the underlying cause, not just symptom control. Some antidiarrheals contain opiates — habit-forming.
  • C. diff: a healthcare-associated, spore-forming bacterium causing severe diarrhea, abdominal pain, bloating. Spores survive on surfaces and are not killed by alcohol-based hand sanitizer — must wash hands with soap and water. Risk factors: antibiotic use, elderly, immunocompromised, long-term care residents, prior GI procedures/colonization. Complications: severe dehydration (even kidney failure), toxic megacolon, bowel perforation.
  • C. diff precautions: contact/spore isolation, clean surfaces with bleach, soap-and-water hand hygiene for everyone entering the room, send a stool sample for confirmation (results ~48 hrs). Treatment: fluids, nutrition, antibiotics (PO/IV), possibly surgery; fecal transplant and probiotics may help restore gut flora.

Bowel Incontinence, Flatulence & Hemorrhoids

  • Bowel incontinence = inability to control passage of stool/gas. Causes: muscle/nerve damage, sphincter dysfunction, constipation or diarrhea, large stool volumes, surgery, rectal prolapse. Risk factors: age, female sex, nerve damage (spinal cord injury), dementia, physical disability. Can cause body image disturbance and social isolation. Prevention/treatment centers on managing constipation or diarrhea and avoiding straining (straining itself can cause nerve damage).
  • Flatulence: abdominal distension, cramping, bloating, pain. Causes: constipation, food intolerances, GI disease (gastroenteritis, IBS, Crohn's, celiac), stress. Care: avoid gas-producing foods, small frequent meals, eat/drink slowly, limit carbonated drinks and straws, avoid lying down right after eating.
  • Hemorrhoids: dilated/engorged rectal veins from increased venous pressure (straining, pregnancy, heart failure, liver disease, prolonged toilet sitting). External hemorrhoids are visible/purplish and may need excision; internal ones aren't visible but can still bleed. Treatment: manage constipation (diet, fluids, activity), topical agents, warm sitz baths; surgery is higher-risk and generally avoided when possible.

Colon Cancer Screening & GI Assessment

  • Colon cancer is the #2 leading cause of cancer death in the U.S. (men and women combined). Risk factors: African American race, diets high in red/processed meat and low in fiber, obesity, age 50+, inactivity, alcohol/tobacco use, family history, history of inflammatory bowel disease.
  • Screening typically starts at age 45; frequency depends on risk/findings. Minimums: flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years, colonoscopy every 10 years. Stool DNA/occult blood tests are used but are less accurate than direct colonoscopy visualization.
  • GI assessment: usual elimination pattern, stool description, diet/fluid intake, GI history/surgeries, medications, emotional state, activity level. The Bristol stool chart is used to describe stool form/consistency (know that it exists and is used — not required to memorize the types).
  • No single blood test diagnoses most GI disorders; if stool blood is detected, an H&H is often ordered to gauge blood loss. Fecal occult blood test (FOBT): checks for hidden blood (screens for cancer/unexplained anemia) — needs samples from 2 different areas of a formed stool, across 3 different stools; red meat can cause a false positive.

NG Tubes & Bowel Training

  • An NG tube can be used for feeding/medication (see Nutrition) or, more distinctly in bowel care, for gastric decompression — keeping the stomach empty via low wall suction.
  • NG tube assessment: nasal skin integrity, tube position/migration (chart the tube's cm marking), and suction drainage (amount, color, clarity). Confirm placement with pH strips or abdominal X-ray. Too much suction can erode the gastric mucosa and cause an ulcer — verify ordered suction level (low wall).
  • Bowel training (for chronic constipation or fecal incontinence): a consistent, patient-centered daily routine — choose a realistic time (around the patient's normal schedule/work hours), offer fluids to stimulate defecation, assist to the commode, provide privacy, and pair with regular exercise and a fiber-rich, well-balanced diet (with adequate fluid intake if increasing fiber). Requires patience and consistency — results take time.
  • Older adult considerations: encourage cancer screening, adequate fiber/fluids, regular activity, and remember older adults compensate less well for fluid loss from diarrhea — may need IV rather than PO fluids.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What is post-void residual (PVR), and how is it measured?
The amount of urine left in the bladder after voiding — measured by bladder scanner (ultrasonography, less invasive) or straight catheterization.
What's the difference between stress, urge, and overflow urinary incontinence?
Stress: leakage with effort/exertion (coughing, laughing) from weakened pelvic floor muscles. Urge: an overactive bladder contracts involuntarily with little warning. Overflow: poor bladder emptying or obstruction (e.g. BPH), causing dribbling/leakage.
What defines a concerning urine output, and how long before it's a concern?
Less than 30 mL/hour for more than 2 hours. Also a concern if an awake patient hasn't voided in 6 hours.
Why are hospitals not reimbursed for treating a CAUTI, and what's the core prevention strategy?
CAUTIs are considered reasonably preventable — hand hygiene, sterile insertion technique, proper securement, and removing the catheter as soon as it's no longer needed.
What's the nursing care rule for keeping a catheter drainage bag positioned correctly?
Always keep the bag below the level of the bladder and off the floor — never toss it up onto the bed with the patient.
Which type of laxative/cathartic is safe for daily use, and which types are short-term/PRN only?
Bulk-forming agents (fiber/Metamucil) are safe daily. Emollients (stool softeners), osmotics, and stimulant cathartics should only be used short-term/PRN — overuse can cause dependence.
What position should a patient be in for an enema, and what are the key risks to monitor for?
Left lateral Sims' position. Risks: fluid/electrolyte imbalance, tissue trauma, vagal nerve stimulation (bradycardia/syncope), abdominal pain, and perforation.
What symptom of fecal impaction is easily mistaken for diarrhea, and why is that dangerous?
Oozing of liquid stool around a hardened, impacted mass — dangerous because nurses may mistakenly hold stool softeners/laxatives when the patient actually needs treatment for the impaction.
Why doesn't alcohol-based hand sanitizer work against C. diff, and what must be used instead?
C. diff is spore-forming and survives on surfaces and hand sanitizer — hands must be washed with soap and water.
At what age does colon cancer screening typically start, and what are the minimum screening intervals?
Age 45. Minimum intervals: flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years, colonoscopy every 10 years.
How should a fecal occult blood test (FOBT) sample be collected, and what can cause a false positive?
Samples from 2 different areas of a formed stool, across 3 different stools. Eating red meat can cause a false positive.
What can happen if NG tube suction is set too high, and how should placement be confirmed?
Too much suction can erode the gastric mucosa and cause an ulcer. Confirm placement with pH strips or an abdominal X-ray.
