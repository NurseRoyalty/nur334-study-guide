GERD, Hiatal Hernia & Peptic Ulcer Disease

Upper GI medications, GERD and its complications (including Barrett's esophagus), hiatal hernia, peptic ulcer disease with the HOP complications, and dumping syndrome after gastric surgery.

Upper GI Drug Therapy

Upper GI = mouth, pharynx, esophagus, lower esophageal sphincter, and stomach. Know the generic names — that's what NCLEX uses.

ClassExamplesWhat it does / nursing points
Antacids—Quick but short-lived. Taken 1–3 hours after meals and at bedtime. Treat symptoms only; don't prevent reflux long term.
H2 receptor blockersCimetidine, famotidine (-tidine)Block histamine-2 → decrease gastric acid. Some OTC, some prescription.
Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)Omeprazole, lansoprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole (-prazole)Very effective acid reducers. Pantoprazole is often given IV in the hospital.
Cytoprotective agentSucralfateCoats the stomach to protect it.
Prokinetic agentMetoclopramideSpeeds gastric emptying: gastroparesis, GERD (less food to reflux after meals), poor appetite (e.g., during chemo — an empty stomach makes the patient hungrier).

GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease)

Backward flow of stomach contents into the esophagus. The stomach is acidic on purpose; the esophagus is not. The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) normally keeps acid where it belongs — in GERD the LES is loose, so acid refluxes up.

Manifestations

  • Heartburn — the most common symptom
  • Regurgitation
  • Respiratory symptoms, especially at night (coughing spells from reflux reaching the lungs)

Complications

  • Esophagitis: acid inflames the esophageal lining
  • Barrett's esophagus: long-term esophagitis that changes the cells
  • Cough and bronchospasm

Diagnosis

  • Barium swallow or EGD
  • Often now: a 2-week trial of high-dose PPI. If symptoms respond, the patient stays on the PPI and invasive tests aren't needed (less cost and discomfort)

Lifestyle Teaching for GERD

  • Identify and avoid personal triggers
  • Quit smoking — improves LES strength immediately
  • Avoid fatty foods; eat small meals; no fluids with meals (drink between meals)
  • Avoid late-night snacking
  • Stay upright 2–3 hours after eating; raise the head of the bed on 4–6 inch blocks (gravity is not reflux's friend)
  • Weight loss if indicated — reduces intra-abdominal pressure
  • Medications: antacids, H2 blockers, PPIs, prokinetics

Refractory GERD Procedures

LINX device

  • Endoscopic procedure
  • Ring of rare-earth magnets placed around the LES
  • Strengthens LES closure but still lets food pass when swallowing

Fundoplication

  • Surgical: laparoscopic (small band-aid incisions) or open (heals by primary intention)
  • Nissen fundoplication: the fundus is wrapped and sutured around the lower esophagus like a collar — a "bear hug" that strengthens LES contraction

Hiatal Hernia

  • A portion of the stomach herniates up through an opening in the diaphragm (also called diaphragmatic or esophageal hernia)
  • Cause is often structural: increased abdominal pressure on the LES — pregnancy, obesity
  • It isn't reflux itself — it's a herniated area above the LES
  • May be asymptomatic; if symptomatic, looks like GERD
  • Treatment is similar to GERD

Barrett's Esophagus

Barrett's esophagus: stomach secretions in the esophagus over a long time cause metaplasia (cell changes). Can occur with GERD or hiatal hernia. Confirmed by biopsy. Rare, but it raises the risk of esophageal cancer 30–40 fold — a key reason GERD shouldn't be left untreated.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Erosion of the GI mucosa from the digestive action of hydrochloric acid and pepsin.

Causes / risk factors

  • H. pylori (triggers inflammation)
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) — NSAIDs = GI upset + kidney problems
  • NSAIDs plus H. pylori = double trouble
  • Alcohol, smoking, family history
  • Stress aggravates an existing ulcer (not proven to cause one)

Types

  • Gastric ulcer: usually in the antrum of the stomach
  • Duodenal ulcer: just past the pyloric sphincter — 80% of ulcers
  • About 90% of ulcers involve H. pylori
  • Acute ulcers: shallower, shorter, resolve
  • Chronic ulcers: 4× more common, deeper, more severe, long-term
Remember: NSAIDs → GI upset and kidney problems. Acetaminophen (not an NSAID) → liver (hepatotoxicity).

Diagnosis

  • EGD to look at the ulcer
  • Urea breath test and stool test — non-invasive tests for H. pylori
  • Biopsy — invasive, but the gold standard for H. pylori
  • CBC (H&H) — is it bleeding or has it bled?
  • Stool for occult blood (guaiac) — may be dark/tarry or invisible

Complications: HOP

ComplicationWhat you'll seeNursing care
Hemorrhage (most common)Vital sign trends (HR 70 → 80 → 86 → 95 = investigate); redder NG aspirate; pain may decrease at first (blood neutralizes the acid)Close VS monitoring; NG to low wall suction to decompress; care like upper GI bleed
ObstructionMost likely when the ulcer is near the pyloric sphincter; gradual onsetNG to low wall suction until resolved; irrigate per protocol
PerforationSudden, severe pain radiating to the back, unrelieved; rigid, board-like abdomen; shallow rapid respirations; weak rapid pulse; absent bowel sounds (listen 5 min); rebound tendernessEmergency — bacterial peritonitis can develop within 6–12 hours
Perforation cue: a rigid, board-like abdomen plus sudden severe pain means gastric contents are in the peritoneum. Rebound tenderness (pain when the hand is released, not when pressing) points to peritonitis.

Collaborative Care for PUD

  • Adequate rest to limit the stress response; quit smoking
  • No special ulcer diet — individualized
  • Stop aspirin and NSAIDs for 4–6 weeks; if aspirin must continue, give it with a PPI
  • H. pylori: antibiotics + a PPI; may add sucralfate
  • Refractory PUD — Billroth I and II: reconstruction after a vagotomy (cut the vagus nerve to decrease HCl secretion) and antrectomy (remove the antrum). Billroth I = end-to-end gastroduodenal anastomosis; Billroth II = end-to-side. (Know they exist; details not tested.)

Dumping Syndrome

A post-op complication whenever part of the stomach is removed or bypassed: gastrectomy/PUD surgery, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, and other gastric procedures.

What happens

  • A bolus of undigested, hyperosmolar (high simple-sugar) food dumps from the small stomach into the small intestine
  • Fluid shifts out of the vascular space into the gut (toward the higher concentration)
  • Result: profuse diarrhea + dizziness, sweating, palpitations (temporarily hypovolemic)
  • Later: postprandial hypoglycemia about 2 hours after eating — the sugar load triggers a big insulin release

Teaching

  • 6 small meals a day — never a large meal
  • No fluids with meals (before or after)
  • Avoid concentrated simple sugars
  • Protein and fat are encouraged (rebuild tissue)
  • Rest after eating
  • Usually self-limiting: a few months to about a year

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

A client asks why metoclopramide was ordered for GERD. How should the nurse explain it?
It's a prokinetic that speeds gastric emptying, so there's less food in the stomach to reflux after meals.
Which medication coats the stomach to protect it?
Sucralfate — a cytoprotective agent.
How can you tell an H2 blocker from a PPI by name?
H2 blockers end in -tidine (famotidine, cimetidine); PPIs end in -prazole (omeprazole, pantoprazole).
When should antacids be taken, and what's their limitation?
1–3 hours after meals and at bedtime. They work fast but are short-lived and only treat symptoms.
What is the pathophysiology of GERD?
A loose lower esophageal sphincter lets acidic stomach contents flow back into the esophagus.
A client with GERD wakes at night coughing. Which teaching helps?
Raise the head of the bed on 4–6 inch blocks, avoid late-night snacks, and stay upright 2–3 hours after eating.
Which lifestyle change improves LES strength immediately?
Quitting smoking.
A client with GERD drinks a large glass of water with every meal. What should the nurse teach?
Drink fluids between meals, not with them, and eat small meals — to decrease the amount in the stomach.
How is GERD often diagnosed today without an EGD?
A 2-week trial of high-dose PPI. If symptoms respond, the client continues the PPI.
Why does Barrett's esophagus matter?
Long-term acid exposure causes metaplasia; it raises esophageal cancer risk 30–40 fold. Confirmed by biopsy.
How does a hiatal hernia differ from GERD, and how is it treated?
Part of the stomach herniates up through the diaphragm (above the LES) — often from increased abdominal pressure. Symptoms and treatment are similar to GERD.
What do a LINX device and a Nissen fundoplication have in common?
Both are for refractory GERD and strengthen LES closure — LINX with a ring of magnets, Nissen by wrapping the fundus around the esophagus.
A client taking daily ibuprofen tests positive for H. pylori. Why is this concerning?
NSAIDs plus H. pylori is 'double trouble' — much higher ulcer risk.
Which test is the gold standard for H. pylori, and what are the non-invasive options?
Biopsy (via EGD) is the gold standard; urea breath test and stool test are non-invasive.
What does HOP stand for in PUD?
Hemorrhage (most common), Obstruction, Perforation.
A client with a bleeding ulcer suddenly reports that the pain is gone. How should the nurse interpret this?
Pain can decrease early in hemorrhage because blood neutralizes the acid — check vital sign trends and the NG aspirate; don't assume improvement.
A client with PUD develops sudden severe pain radiating to the back and a rigid, board-like abdomen. What is happening?
Perforation — gastric contents in the peritoneum. Emergency; peritonitis can develop within 6–12 hours.
What is rebound tenderness?
Pain when the examiner releases pressure on the abdomen (not while pressing). It suggests peritonitis.
A client with PUD must stay on aspirin. What should the nurse expect?
Aspirin given with a PPI. Otherwise aspirin and NSAIDs are stopped for 4–6 weeks.
Why is a vagotomy done with a Billroth procedure?
Cutting the vagus nerve decreases hydrochloric acid secretion.
Twenty minutes after eating cake, a client who had a gastrectomy has diarrhea, dizziness, sweating, and palpitations. What is this, and why?
Dumping syndrome: hyperosmolar food dumps into the small intestine and pulls fluid out of the vascular space.
Why can a client with dumping syndrome become hypoglycemic about 2 hours after a meal?
The sugar load causes a large insulin release, leading to postprandial hypoglycemia.
Which diet teaching prevents dumping syndrome?
6 small meals, no fluids with meals, avoid simple sugars, encourage protein and fat, and rest after eating.
