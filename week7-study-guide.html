Week 7 has two parts: caring for the patient with cancer (treatments and their side effects) and the hepatobiliary system — gallbladder, pancreas, and liver — plus the tests used to evaluate them. Each section links to its full topic page.

1. Cancer

CAUTION: Change in bowel/bladder habits · A sore that does not heal · Unusual bleeding or discharge · Thickening or lump · Indigestion or difficulty swallowing · Obvious change in wart or mole · Nagging cough or hoarseness.
  • Goals: cure → control → palliation. Surgery can be prophylactic, diagnostic, curative, or for control (debulking)
  • Chemotherapy (systemic): certified nurse, two-RN double check, central line preferred, premedicate, check counts first and hold if WBC/platelets are too low. Nadir = lowest counts
  • Extravasation of a vesicant: stop the infusion → aspirate → remove the cannula → elevate → antidote per protocol
  • Radiation (local): external (teletherapy) — patient not radioactive. Sealed implant — patient emits radiation, excretions don't. Unsealed (I-131) — excretions are radioactive. Time, distance, shielding
  • Sealed implant care: private room, dosimeter, no pregnant visitors or children under 16, 30 minutes a day at 6 feet, forceps + lead container
  • Bone marrow suppression: ANC <1,000 = neutropenic, <500 = protective precautions; platelets <50,000 bleeding risk, <20,000 transfuse; hemoglobin <11 → epoetin
  • Other side effects: anorexia, nausea/vomiting, stomatitis (no alcohol mouthwash), skin desquamation, fatigue, hair loss, infertility

2. Cholelithiasis & Cholecystitis

  • Cholelithiasis = gallstones; cholecystitis = inflamed gallbladder
  • Risk: middle-aged female, fair skin, overweight, high-fat diet, oral contraceptives
  • Signs: upper abdominal pain radiating to the right shoulder after fatty or large meals; nausea, vomiting, indigestion, belching, gas
  • Obstructive jaundice (stone in the common bile duct): yellow skin, dark urine, clay-colored stools
  • Care: avoid fat; NPO in a flare; opioids, antiemetics, antispasmodics; laparoscopic cholecystectomy is most common
  • Post-op: bandages off next day, low-fat diet for a few weeks, report bile-colored drainage, fever, or increasing pain. T-tube keeps the common bile duct open

3. Pancreatitis

  • Acute: enzymes auto-digest the pancreas. Causes: gallstones and alcohol. Best imaging: CT
  • Findings: severe pain, nausea/vomiting, low-grade fever, ↑ amylase and lipase, ↑ triglycerides, hypovolemia, hypocalcemia, paralytic ileus, possible hyperglycemia
  • Cullen's (umbilicus) and Turner's (flank) signs = severe disease, poor prognosis
  • Complications: pseudocyst (can perforate → peritonitis), abscess (surgical drainage)
  • Care: pain relief first (IV opioids, knees bent), NPO with NG to low wall suction, aggressive IV fluids; later a high-carbohydrate, low-fat diet; no alcohol
  • Chronic: steatorrhea, weight loss. Pancrelipase with the first bite of every meal or snack; don't chew or crush; goal is fewer, less fatty stools. May need 4,000–6,000 calories/day or TPN

  • Hepatitis care: rest at home, balanced diet, diphenhydramine for itching, no alcohol, acetaminophen, or isoniazid
  • Cirrhosis, late: jaundice, low albumin (ascites), low prothrombin (bleeding), portal hypertension (ascites, splenomegaly, spider angiomas, caput medusae, varices), encephalopathy, asterixis
  • Ascites: sodium and fluid restriction, spironolactone + furosemide, albumin, daily weights and abdominal girth, paracentesis (void first), TIPS
  • Bleeding precautions: no aspirin, no injections, no rectal temps or enemas, soft toothbrush, prevent falls and straining
  • Bleeding varices: sclerotherapy, banding, Blakemore tube (ICU)
  • Hepatic encephalopathy: high ammonia crosses the blood–brain barrier. Lactulose to 2–4 stools/day, avoid constipation, control GI bleeding; restrict protein only if severe

5. Hepatobiliary Labs & Diagnostics

  • Ultrasound: 95% accurate for gallstones; NPO 8 hours. HIDA scan if the ultrasound is negative
  • CT with contrast: check BUN/creatinine, warn about warm flush, push fluids. MRI: no metal implants or pregnancy
  • ERCP: NPO, consent, sedation; afterward NPO until the gag reflex returns; pancreatitis is the most common complication
  • Liver biopsy: check PT/INR and type and crossmatch; hold breath after expiration; afterward right side, flat 12–24 hours; watch for bleeding and pneumothorax
  • Liver labs: ↑ AST/ALT (ALT most specific), ↓ albumin, prolonged PT, ↑ ammonia, ↑ bilirubin
  • Bilirubin: conjugated (direct) = water-soluble, in urine, obstruction; unconjugated (indirect) = not in urine, problem in or before the liver
  • Pancreas: amylase rises fast and normalizes in 48–72 hours; lipase rises more slowly and stays up longer

Week 7 red flags: lost blood return during a vesicant infusion · ANC below 500 · vomiting blood in a patient with cirrhosis · new confusion or asterixis · rising heart rate after a liver biopsy · bile-colored drainage after a cholecystectomy · Cullen's or Turner's sign.

Self-Test Flashcards

Click a card to flip it and check your answer.

What are the goals of cancer treatment, in order?
Cure, then control, then palliation.
What is the first action when extravasation is suspected?
Stop the infusion immediately; then aspirate, remove the cannula, elevate the arm, and give the antidote per protocol.
At what ANC is a client neutropenic, and when are protective precautions needed?
Neutropenic below 1,000; protective precautions below 500.
Which type of radiation makes a client's body fluids radioactive?
Unsealed internal radiation, such as swallowed I-131.
What three principles limit a nurse's radiation exposure?
Time, distance, and shielding (a dosimeter only measures exposure).
What causes clay-colored stools and dark urine in gallbladder disease?
Obstructive jaundice — a stone blocking the common bile duct.
What is the priority intervention in acute pancreatitis?
Pain relief, along with NPO status and aggressive IV fluids.
When is pancrelipase taken?
With the first bite of every meal or snack.
Which hepatitis types are spread by blood, and which by the fecal–oral route?
B and C by blood; A by the fecal–oral route.
Which drug lowers ammonia in hepatic encephalopathy, and what is the goal?
Lactulose, titrated to 2–4 stools per day.
How is a client positioned after a liver biopsy?
On the right side, flat for 12–24 hours.
What is the most common complication of ERCP?
Pancreatitis.
