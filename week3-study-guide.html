Electrolyte & Fluid Imbalances + Perioperative Nursing

Unit 1: Electrolyte Imbalances

Overview: The Big Picture

Electrolyte imbalances are among the most commonly tested topics in nursing exams. They're clinically critical because even small deviations can cause severe symptoms—seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory failure—or be completely silent while causing damage.

Key Principle: Electrolytes regulate neuromuscular function, cardiac conduction, fluid shifts, and cellular metabolism. Always assess the TOTAL BODY deficit, not just serum concentration.

Normal Lab Values (Memorize These)

Sodium (Na+) 135-145 mEq/L <120 >160
Potassium (K+) 3.5-5.0 mEq/L <2.5 >6.5
Calcium (Ca2+) 8.5-10.2 mg/dL (total)
4.5-5.3 mg/dL (ionized)		 <6.5 >13

Phosphorus (PO4) 2.5-4.5 mg/dL <1.0 >6.0

Sodium Imbalances

Hyponatremia (Na < 135) and Hypernatremia (Na > 145) are related to total body water, not just sodium content. Assessment requires BUN/Cr ratio, osmolality, and volume status.

Potassium Imbalances

Hyperkalemia (K > 5.5) is immediately life-threatening due to cardiac effects. EKG changes: peaked T waves → widened QRS → absent P wave → eventual cardiac standstill.

Emergency Intervention for Hyperkalemia: Calcium gluconate FIRST (stabilizes cardiac membrane), then shift K+ intracellularly with insulin+glucose or beta-agonists, then remove K+ with diuretics or dialysis.

Critical Concept: Potassium and magnesium work together. Hypokalemia cannot be corrected if magnesium is low—always check Mg2+ and correct it first.

Calcium Imbalances

Hypocalcemia (Ca < 8.5) causes neuromuscular hyperexcitability. Classic signs:

  • Chvostek's sign: Tap facial nerve → facial muscles twitch
  • Trousseau's sign: Inflate BP cuff → carpopedal spasm (hand cramping)
  • Seizures, tetany, laryngospasm
ALWAYS correct calcium for albumin: Corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.8(4 - albumin). A patient with low albumin may have LOW total Ca but NORMAL ionized (active) Ca.

Magnesium Imbalances

Hypomagnesemia (Mg < 1.7) is often overlooked but very common. Problems: torsades de pointes, potassium/calcium depletion that won't respond to treatment, neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Hypermagnesemia (Mg > 2.5): Loss of DTRs is a clinical sign of toxicity. In pre-eclampsia, diminished DTRs mean reduce or hold magnesium sulfate.

Phosphorus Imbalances

Phosphorus and calcium have an inverse relationship. In chronic kidney disease, elevated phosphorus stimulates PTH → secondary hyperparathyroidism → bone loss and vascular calcification.

Refeeding Syndrome: When malnourished patients eat or receive TPN, insulin secretion shifts phosphorus, potassium, and magnesium intracellularly. Result: severe hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia → cardiac dysrhythmias, respiratory failure. Prevent by feeding slowly and monitoring electrolytes.

Unit 2: Fluid Volume Imbalances

The Gold Standard: Daily Weights

1 pound = approximately 500 mL of fluid. Acute weight changes always indicate fluid shifts, not fat or muscle. This is the MOST RELIABLE indicator of fluid status across all patient populations.

Types of FVD and FVE

Type Osmolality Sodium Clinical Example
Isotonic FVD Normal (280-300) 130-150 Diarrhea, vomiting, NG tube
Hypertonic FVD High (>300) >150 Diabetes insipidus, inadequate fluid intake
Hypotonic FVD Low (<280) <130 SIADH, diuretic overuse, tap water drinking

Third-Spacing: The Paradox

In sepsis, burns, malnutrition, or cirrhosis, fluid shifts from intravascular → interstitial space. The patient may look "overloaded" (edema, ascites) but is actually intravascularly depleted. Classic example: cirrhotic with ascites who becomes hypotensive.

Unit 3: Perioperative Nursing

Preoperative Phase

Anticoagulation Management

  • Warfarin: Discontinue 3-5 days pre-op. Bridge with LMWH (stopped 12-24 hours pre-op) or IV heparin (stopped hours pre-op, reversible with protamine) for high-risk patients.
  • Aspirin & NSAIDs: Often continued; depends on surgery type.
  • Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs): Usually stopped 24-48 hours pre-op.

NPO Guidelines (ASA 2011)

  • Solids: 6-8 hours
  • Clear liquids: up to 2 hours pre-op
  • Reduces aspiration risk while preventing dehydration

Malignant Hyperthermia Screening

Malignant Hyperthermia (MH) is triggered by succinylcholine and volatile anesthetics. It's genetic and potentially fatal.

Family History = ALERT: If patient or family has MH history, communicate to anesthesiologist immediately. Use non-triggering anesthetics and have dantrolene available in OR.

Latex Allergy

Can range from contact dermatitis to anaphylaxis. Communicate to entire OR team. Maintain latex-free environment with latex-free gloves, catheters, and equipment.

Intraoperative Phase

Surgical Timeout (WHO Surgical Safety Checklist)

Before incision, entire team verifies: patient identity, correct procedure, correct site, position, allergies, implants, equipment function. Prevents "never events" (wrong site/patient/procedure).

Malignant Hyperthermia Crisis

Signs: muscle rigidity, skin mottling, elevated temp (late sign), tachycardia, elevated K+, myoglobinuria.

Action Plan: Stop triggering agents immediately, hyperventilate with 100% O2, administer dantrolene IV 2.5 mg/kg, use iced saline, monitor core temperature, manage complications (hyperkalemia, acidosis, rhabdomyolysis).

Positioning & Pressure Injury Prevention

  • Prone positioning: Risk of facial edema, corneal abrasion, airway compromise, chest wall compression
  • Use padding, rolls, pillows to protect vulnerable areas
  • Monitor peripheral nerves (brachial plexus in arm abduction, peroneal nerve in leg position)

Postoperative Phase 1 (PACU)

Immediate Concerns

  • Respiratory depression: RR < 8, pinpoint pupils → opioid overdose. Give naloxone IV.
  • Hypothermia: Mild (35-36°C) is expected; active rewarming if shivering increases O2 demand.
  • Pain management: Inadequate pain control → splinting → atelectasis → VTE risk. Assess and treat promptly.

Vital Signs Monitoring

Every 5-15 minutes initially, then every 30 minutes as patient stabilizes. Frequent monitoring detects complications early.

Incision Assessment

  • Clear serous drainage: Normal
  • Purulent drainage: Infection
  • Bloody drainage: Hematoma or bleeding
  • Erythema, warmth, swelling: Infection

Study Tips & Test-Taking Strategies

  • Memorize the normal ranges: You cannot reason your way through electrolyte questions without knowing what "normal" is.
  • Relate symptoms to mechanisms: Don't just memorize signs—understand WHY hypocalcemia causes Chvostek's (nerve hyperexcitability) and hypercalcemia causes sedation.
  • Think in priorities: In hyperkalemia, calcium is FIRST (protects heart), not potassium-lowering. Know your emergency algorithms.
  • Consider total body deficit: Serum electrolytes don't always reflect total depletion. A normal K+ level in a depleted patient will drop as soon as you give IV fluids.
  • Medications affect electrolytes: Loop diuretics → K+ loss. ACE inhibitors + K-sparing diuretics → K+ retention. Know common drug effects.
  • Daily weights > single labs: A patient can look normal on one lab but be dangerously depleted. Trends matter.
Final Reminder: Electrolyte imbalances can kill. Treat them seriously on exams. When in doubt, think: What's the immediate threat to the heart? What's the priority intervention? That's your answer.
