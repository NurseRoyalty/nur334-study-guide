Skin Assessment and Lesion Terminology

Primary & Secondary Lesions

Malignant Skin Lesions

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Characteristics: Red, pearly lesion with well-defined borders; waxy appearance; may have central depression or ulceration; most common type (80% of skin cancers).

Risk factors: UV exposure, fair skin, age >50, male predominance.

Nursing considerations: Most common but least aggressive; 95%+ survival; rarely metastasizes; treat with surgical excision, cryotherapy, or topical agents.

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Characteristics: Scaly, crusty, erythematous nodule or plaque; often with central ulceration; 15-20% of skin cancers. Can metastasize, especially to regional lymph nodes.

Risk factors: UV exposure, immunosuppression, chronic wounds, actinic keratosis.

Nursing considerations: Higher metastatic potential than BCC; treat with surgical excision; may require Mohs micrographic surgery for high-risk lesions; check regional lymph nodes.

Melanoma

ABCDE Rule for detection:

  • A - Asymmetry: Not mirror-image; one half doesn't match the other
  • B - Border: Irregular, ragged, or notched borders
  • C - Color: Variation in color (brown, black, red, white, blue)
  • D - Diameter: >6mm (larger than pencil eraser)
  • E - Evolving: Changing in size, shape, color, or appearance

Breslow Thickness Classification (prognosis):

  • Thin (<0.76mm): 5-yr survival ~95%; may not require lymph node dissection
  • Intermediate (0.76-4mm): 5-yr survival 60-90%; sentinel lymph node biopsy considered
  • Thick (>4mm): 5-yr survival <50%; high risk of metastasis; requires comprehensive staging

Nursing considerations: Most aggressive; 20% of skin cancer deaths despite lower incidence; educate on sun protection, self-monitoring, family screening (genetic risk).

Skin Cancer Risk Factors

  • UV exposure (cumulative and acute/sunburn)
  • Fair skin, blue/green eyes, red/blonde hair
  • Personal or family history of skin cancer
  • Age >50 (cumulative exposure)
  • Immunosuppression (HIV, transplant, chemotherapy)
  • Chemical exposures (arsenic, tar, petroleum)
  • Chronic wounds or scars

Bacterial Skin Infections

Impetigo (Pyoderma)

Causative organisms: Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep) or Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA).

Characteristics: Honey-crusted lesions on face or extremities; highly contagious; common in children and immunocompromised; begins as vesicles → pustules → crusts.

Treatment: Topical antibiotics (mupirocin) for localized; oral/IV antibiotics for extensive disease; good hygiene to prevent spread.

Nursing considerations: Isolate from others; cover lesions; educate on hand hygiene; monitor for post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

Cellulitis

Causative organisms: Group A Streptococcus or Staphylococcus aureus; may involve deeper dermis and subcutaneous tissue.

Characteristics: Red, warm, edematous, poorly demarcated borders; spreading erythema; may have systemic symptoms (fever, chills); lymphangitis and regional lymphadenopathy common.

Treatment: IV antibiotics (cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones); elevation; warm compresses; may require hospitalization if severe or immunocompromised.

Nursing considerations: Assess for rapidly spreading borders (possible streptococcal cellulitis); monitor for sepsis; educate on prevention of entry sites (breaks in skin).

Viral Skin Infections

Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

Characteristics: Recurrent vesicles on erythematous base; grouped "on an erythematous base"; extremely painful; prodrome (tingling, burning).

Phases: Vesicles → pustules → erosions → crusts → healing (5-10 days).

HSV-1: Oral herpes ("cold sores"); reactivation triggered by stress, fever, immunosuppression, UV exposure.

HSV-2: Genital herpes; sexually transmitted; recurrent, painful outbreaks.

Treatment: Antiviral therapy (acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir) most effective within 48 hours of symptom onset; prophylaxis for recurrent cases.

Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) / Shingles (Herpes Zoster)

Characteristics: Reactivation of varicella virus in dorsal root ganglia; painful vesicular rash in dermatomal distribution (follows one nerve root); burning precedes lesions by 2-3 days.

Dermatomal distribution: Thoracic (50%), lumbar (20%), cervical/trigeminal (20%); typically unilateral on torso or face.

Complications: Postherpetic neuralgia (pain lasting >3 months); ophthalmic zoster (eye involvement, vision loss); facial nerve involvement (Ramsay Hunt syndrome).

Treatment: Antiviral therapy CRITICAL within 72 hours of onset (reduces duration and post-herpetic neuralgia risk); supportive care; gabapentin or pregabalin for neuropathic pain.

Nursing considerations: Pain management is priority; maintain lesion hygiene; prevent secondary bacterial infection; monitor vision if ophthalmic involvement; reassure patient rash is not contagious to non-immune individuals.

Molluscum Contagiosum

Characteristics: Viral infection causing small (2-5mm), firm, skin-colored papules with central umbilication; highly contagious by contact; common in children and immunocompromised.

Treatment: Usually self-limited; may use curettage, topical medications, or cryotherapy to prevent spread; avoid squeezing (spreads virus).

Fungal Skin Infections

Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot)

Causative agent: Dermatophytes (Trichophyton).

Characteristics: Itching and burning between toes; maceration, erythema; may spread to sole of foot.

Treatment: Topical antifungals (terbinafine, miconazole); keep feet dry; use antifungal powder; may require oral therapy for resistant cases.

Tinea Corporis (Ringworm)

Characteristics: Circular, scaly patches with clearing center ("annular" lesions); pruritic; may have vesicles at edges.

Treatment: Topical antifungals; may require oral griseofulvin or terbinafine if extensive.

Candidiasis

Causative agent: Candida albicans (normal flora overgrowth).

Common sites: Inframammary folds, skin creases, mucous membranes (oral thrush), vaginal, nail beds.

Risk factors: Immunosuppression, diabetes, prolonged antibiotic use, moisture/maceration, corticosteroid use.

Characteristics: Erythematous with satellite pustules; maceration; white patches (oral); severe itching.

Treatment: Topical or oral antifungals (fluconazole, clotrimazole); reduce moisture; address underlying cause.

Inflammatory Skin Conditions

Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)

Characteristics: Intense pruritus (itching), erythematous patches, lichenification from scratching; worse with stress, irritants; often personal/family history of atopy.

Treatment: Emollients, topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors; avoid irritants; antihistamines for itching.

Psoriasis

Characteristics: Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale; common on elbows, knees, scalp, nails; chronic, relapsing; genetic predisposition; triggered by stress, infection, medications.

Types: Plaque (most common), guttate, inverse, pustular, erythrodermic (severe).

Treatment: Topical therapies (corticosteroids, vitamin D analogues), phototherapy, systemic biologics for moderate/severe disease.

Urticaria (Hives)

Characteristics: Transient, raised, erythematous wheals with pruritus; appear and disappear within hours; may be accompanied by angioedema.

Causes: Allergens (food, drugs, insect bites), infection, stress, physical triggers (heat, cold, pressure).

Acute vs. Chronic: Acute resolves within 6 weeks; chronic persists >6 weeks despite treatment.

Treatment: Identify and avoid triggers; antihistamines (H1 blockers); corticosteroids for severe; consider allergy testing if chronic.
