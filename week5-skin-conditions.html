Skin Cancer Types

Non-Melanoma Skin Cancers

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

  • Most Common: 80% of skin cancers
  • Appearance: Red pearly lesions with defined borders, waxy, may have central depression or ulceration
  • Location: Sun-exposed areas (face, neck, hands)
  • Behavior: Locally invasive, rarely metastasizes but can be disfiguring if untreated
  • Management: Surgical excision, Mohs surgery for large lesions, cryotherapy, topical 5-FU

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

  • Second Most Common: 15-20% of skin cancers
  • Appearance: Scaly, crusty, erythematous nodule or plaque; may have central ulceration
  • Behavior: More aggressive than BCC; can metastasize to regional lymph nodes if neglected
  • Risk Factors: Sun exposure, immunosuppression, chronic wounds, chemical exposure
  • Management: Surgical excision, Mohs surgery, radiation for advanced disease

Melanoma (Malignant Melanoma)

Most Dangerous Skin Cancer

  • Origin: Melanocytes; can develop from existing moles or new lesions
  • Incidence: 5% of skin cancers but 80% of skin cancer deaths
  • ABCDE Rule for Detection: Asymmetry (not mirror-image), Border irregularity (ragged/notched edges), Color variation (multiple colors within lesion), Diameter (>6mm), Evolving (changing in size, shape, or color)
  • Depth Classification (Breslow Thickness): Thickness determines prognosis; thin (<1mm) = 95% survival; thick (>4mm) = 50% survival
  • Management: Wide surgical excision with margins (0.5-2cm depending on depth), sentinel lymph node biopsy for deep lesions, immunotherapy (checkpoint inhibitors), chemotherapy for advanced disease
  • Prognosis: Poor if diagnosed late; early detection critical

Risk Factors for All Skin Cancers

  • UV Exposure: Cumulative sun exposure, tanning beds (100x more UV than sun)
  • Skin Type: Fair skin, blue eyes, red/blonde hair (higher risk)
  • Personal/Family History: Previous skin cancer, family history of melanoma
  • Age: Risk increases with age
  • Immunosuppression: HIV/AIDS, organ transplant recipients
  • Chemical Exposure: Arsenic, occupational exposures

Common Skin Infections

Bacterial Infections

Impetigo & Cellulitis

  • Impetigo: Superficial, honey-crusted lesions; highly contagious; common in children; topical antibiotics usually sufficient
  • Cellulitis: Non-purulent inflammation of dermis/subcutaneous tissue; red, warm, swollen, painful; may have systemic signs (fever); requires IV antibiotics
  • Erysipelas: Superficial cellulitis with sharp demarcation; often on face/lower extremities; Group A Streptococcus

Viral Infections

Herpes Simplex (HSV) and Varicella-Zoster (VZV)

  • Herpes Simplex: Recurrent vesicles on lips/genitals; painful; preceded by prodrome (tingling); antiviral therapy (acyclovir) shortens duration
  • Varicella-Zoster (Shingles): Unilateral dermatomal distribution; very painful even after rash resolves (post-herpetic neuralgia); antiviral therapy critical within 72 hours of onset
  • Molluscum Contagiosum: Umbilicated lesions; viral; self-limited but may last months to years; treatment options: cryotherapy, curettage

Fungal Infections

Common Dermatophyte Infections

  • Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot): Between toes; scaly, macerated; may extend to sole/sides; antifungal cream (tolnaftate, terbinafine)
  • Tinea Corporis (Ringworm): Ring-shaped lesions with central clearing; body surfaces; KOH prep confirms fungal infection
  • Candidiasis (Yeast): Moist areas (skin folds, inframammary); red, macerated; satellite pustules; topical antifungal (nystatin) or azoles

Other Common Skin Conditions

Inflammatory/Allergic Conditions

Eczema, Psoriasis, Urticaria

  • Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema): Chronic inflammation; pruritus (itching); lichenification from scratching; triggered by irritants/allergens; management: emollients, topical steroids, systemic medications
  • Psoriasis: Chronic inflammatory; silvery scales on plaques; symmetric distribution; family history common; triggered by stress, infection; treatment: topical steroids, phototherapy, biologics
  • Urticaria (Hives): Transient wheals; pruritic; angioedema possible (lips, tongue—airway risk); antihistamines first-line; epinephrine if anaphylaxis
