Skin Conditions

Comprehensive coverage of skin cancers, infections, parasitic infestations, and benign skin disorders relevant to nursing assessment and care.

Skin Lesion Terminology & Assessment

Primary Skin Lesions

Lesion Types and Characteristics

  • Macule: Flat, non-palpable lesion < 1 cm (freckles, flat moles)
  • Patch: Flat, non-palpable lesion > 1 cm (vitiligo, large areas of color change)
  • Papule: Raised, palpable lesion < 1 cm (warts, pimples, moles)
  • Plaque: Raised, palpable lesion > 1 cm with flat top (psoriasis, lichen planus)
  • Nodule: Firm, deeper lesion 1-2 cm (lipomas, cysts, lymph nodes)
  • Vesicle: Fluid-filled blister < 1 cm (herpes, chickenpox blisters)
  • Bullae: Fluid-filled blister > 1 cm (pemphigus, severe burn blisters)
  • Pustule: Pus-filled lesion (acne, impetigo, folliculitis)
  • Wheal: Transient raised lesion (urticaria, hives from allergic reaction)
  • Crust: Dried serum/pus/blood on skin surface (impetigo, eczema)
  • Scale: Dry, flaking skin (psoriasis, seborrheic keratosis, tinea)
  • Erosion/Ulcer: Loss of epidermis (shallow = erosion, deep = ulcer with dermis involvement)

Skin Assessment in Diverse Populations

  • Melanin Impact: Higher melanin in darker skin protects against UV damage but masks erythema; redness harder to detect
  • Color Changes in Dark Skin: Look for purple/dark brown instead of red; assess texture changes more carefully
  • Pallor in Dark Skin: Appears as ashen or grayish tone; check inner wrist, oral mucosa, conjunctiva for baseline comparison
  • Cyanosis in Dark Skin: Check nail beds, lips, oral mucosa; skin appears darker/duller rather than blue
  • Jaundice in Dark Skin: Most visible in sclera and hard palate rather than skin
  • Petechiae/Purpura: Harder to see; check non-pigmented areas; compare sides

Melanoma ABCDE Rule

Mnemonic for Melanoma Screening

  • A - Asymmetry: One half does not match the other half
  • B - Border Irregularity: Ragged, notched, or scalloped edges rather than smooth
  • C - Color Variation: Multiple colors (brown, black, tan, red, white, blue)
  • D - Diameter: Generally > 6 mm (pencil eraser size); anything growing rapidly warrants evaluation
  • E - Evolution: Changing in size, shape, color, symptoms (itching, bleeding, pain)
  • Action: Any feature present = urgent dermatology referral for biopsy

Bacterial Skin Infections

Impetigo and Cellulitis

  • Impetigo: Honey-golden crusts on erythematous base; highly contagious, common in children; non-bullous (70%) vs bullous form; topical antibiotics usually sufficient for non-bullous; oral for bullous or widespread
  • Cellulitis: Spreading erythema with poorly demarcated borders, edema, warmth, tenderness; deep dermis/subcutaneous infection; risk of bacteremia/sepsis; requires systemic antibiotics IV/oral depending on severity
  • Erysipelas: Superficial cellulitis with sharply demarcated (well-defined) raised borders; "orange peel" appearance; typically face/legs; Group A Strep; good prognosis with antibiotics
  • Folliculitis: Pustules around hair follicles; mild inflammation; may resolve with hygiene; antibiotics if spreading or Staph aureus

HPV and Other Viral Lesions

Human Papillomavirus and Warts

  • Common Warts: Rough, raised, keratotic lesions; hands/feet; painless; often resolve spontaneously
  • Genital HPV: Multiple types (6, 11 = low-risk; 16, 18, 31, 33 = high-risk); can cause genital warts, cervical/anal cancer; vaccine prevents most
  • Plantar Warts: Painful on weight-bearing; appear as callus with central black dot; difficult to treat
  • Management: Salicylic acid, cryotherapy, imiquimod, surgical removal; many resolve without treatment

Fungal Infections

Candidiasis and Tinea

  • Oral Candidiasis (Thrush): White patches (pseudomembrane) on tongue/mucosa, painful, occurs at CD4 < 200 in HIV; antifungal swish-and-swallow (nystatin) or oral fluconazole
  • Esophageal Candidiasis: Difficulty swallowing, substernal chest pain, indicates advanced AIDS; systemic antifungals required; serious OI
  • Cutaneous Candidiasis: Moist intertrigo (skin fold maceration), erythema with satellite pustules; risk factors: moisture, obesity, diabetes, poor hygiene
  • Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot): Scaling between toes, maceration, itching; topical antifungals first-line; keep feet dry
  • Tinea Corporis (Ringworm): Annular (ring-shaped) lesions with central clearing, scaly borders; spreads by contact; topical antifungals; avoid contact with animals
  • Tinea Capitis (Scalp Ringworm): Scaling, alopecia, fungi invasion of hair shaft; systemic antifungals (griseofulvin, terbinafine); contagious to others

Benign and Inflammatory Skin Conditions

Psoriasis and Dermatitis

Common Non-Malignant Conditions

  • Psoriasis: Autoimmune; thick, silvery, scaly plaques on extensor surfaces, elbows, knees; very pruritic; triggered by stress, infections, certain drugs; treat with topical steroids, phototherapy, biologics
  • Contact Dermatitis (Allergic): Reaction to irritant or allergen (poison ivy/oak/sumac, nickel, latex); erythema, vesicles, intense pruritus; treatment: remove trigger, topical steroids, antihistamines
  • Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema): Chronic inflammatory, intensely itchy dry skin; impaired barrier function; infancy through adulthood; emollients, topical steroids, immunosuppressants

Severe Cutaneous Adverse Reactions

EMERGENCY: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome & Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis
SJS = < 10% BSA skin detachment; TEN = > 30% BSA. Prodrome: fever, malaise, followed by rapid maculopapular rash → blistering → epidermal necrosis → massive sloughing. Mucous membrane involvement (mouth, eyes, genitals). IMMEDIATE discontinuation of offending drug (sulfonamides, NSAIDs, anticonvulsants, antibiotics most common). Transfer to burn unit. Supportive care, fluid/electrolyte management. Mortality 5-50%.
