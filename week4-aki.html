Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

← Back to Study Guide

Overview

Acute Kidney Injury = rapid loss of kidney function occurring over HOURS to DAYS. This is characterized by rising creatinine and declining GFR. The critical distinction: AKI is REVERSIBLE, whereas chronic kidney disease is NOT.

  • Represents a sudden decline in kidney function
  • Can range from minimal deterioration to severe impairment
  • May occur with or without oliguria (decreased urine output)
  • Common in hospitalized and critically ill patients
  • Often one of several comorbidities present

Diagnostic Criteria for AKI

Key Lab Findings

  • Increased BUN: Normal 10-20 mg/dL; >20 indicates kidney dysfunction
  • Elevated Creatinine: Normal 0.5-1.2 mg/dL; rapid increase indicates kidney injury
  • Decreased GFR: <90 mL/min (normal is ≥90)
  • Decreased Urine Output (Oliguria):
    • <30 mL/hour (preferred threshold—more immediate)
    • OR <400 mL in 24 hours (less sensitive)
    • Use 30 mL/hour rule—don't wait 24 hours to identify the problem

Causes by Category

PRE-renal Causes (Most Common: ~50%)

  • Decreased Perfusion to Kidneys:
    • Hypotension / shock (cardiogenic, septic, hypovolemic)
    • Dehydration / hypovolemia
    • Heart failure (decreased cardiac output)
    • Severe bleeding
  • Mechanism: Without adequate perfusion, kidneys "shut down" → GFR drops
  • Reversibility: Most reversible IF caught early and treated promptly

RENAL Causes (Intrinsic Damage: ~40%)

  • Most Common: Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN) — damage to kidney tubules
  • Other Intrinsic Causes:
    • Glomerulonephritis
    • Drug Toxicity: aminoglycosides (antibiotics), NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, contrast agents
    • Rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown)
    • Sepsis with direct kidney inflammation
  • Reversibility: Longer recovery time; higher mortality

POST-renal Causes (Obstruction: ~10%)

  • Kidney Stones: Blocking ureteral flow
  • Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH): Enlarged prostate obstructing urethra
  • Tumors: Obstructing urine flow
  • Strictures: Urethral narrowing
  • Mechanism: Urine backs up → pressure on kidneys → kidney dysfunction
  • Reversibility: Usually reversible IF obstruction relieved quickly

Phases of AKI

Oliguric Phase

  • Duration: Days 1-3 typically
  • Urine Output: <400 mL in 24 hours
  • Onset: Usually within 24 hours of insult
  • Lab Findings:
    • Rising BUN and creatinine
    • Hyperkalemia (K+ retention)
    • Metabolic acidosis (waste accumulation)
    • Dilutional hyponatremia (sodium dilution from fluid retention)
  • Clinical Manifestations:
    • Fluid retention / edema
    • Neurologic changes (confusion, disorientation from hyponatremia)
    • Nausea, anorexia
    • Fatigue, weakness

Diuretic Phase

  • Timing: Usually Week 2-3
  • Kidneys start producing large volumes of dilute urine
  • Gradual improvement in kidney function
  • Monitor for dehydration (may need fluid replacement)

Recovery Phase

  • Return to normal or near-normal kidney function
  • May take weeks to months
  • Some patients develop chronic kidney disease if recovery incomplete

Clinical Manifestations (Oliguric Phase)

Results of Fluid/Electrolyte Imbalances

  • Fluid Retention: Edema, hypertension, pulmonary edema
  • Hyperkalemia (K+ >5.5): Dangerous cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, ECG changes (peaked T-waves)
  • Dilutional Hyponatremia: Confusion, disorientation, seizures in severe cases
  • Metabolic Acidosis: From waste product accumulation (BUN, creatinine, urea)
  • Elevated Phosphorus & Magnesium: From retention
  • Hypocalcemia: From hyperphosphatemia

Systemic Manifestations

  • Anorexia, nausea, vomiting
  • Lethargy, fatigue, weakness
  • Uremia (urea in blood)—causes restlessness, altered mental status
  • High mortality in ICU patients (~50-80%)

Management

Identify and Treat the Cause

  • PRE-renal: IV fluids, blood products, inotropes to restore perfusion
  • RENAL: Stop nephrotoxic drugs; supportive care during recovery
  • POST-renal: Relieve obstruction (urinary catheter, nephrostomy tube, surgery)

Manage Complications

  • Hyperkalemia (LIFE-THREATENING):
    • Potassium-binding resins (K-exilate) via GI tract
    • Calcium gluconate IV (protects cardiac membrane)
    • Insulin + dextrose (drives K+ into cells)
    • Low potassium diet
    • Diuretics if heart function permits
  • Fluid Overload:
    • Fluid restriction (typically 400-500 mL/day + urine output)
    • Diuretics (if effective); dialysis if fluid overload severe
    • Daily weights to monitor fluid status
  • Metabolic Acidosis:
    • Sodium bicarbonate IV if severe
    • Dialysis if severe
  • Nutrition: Limited protein (reduces nitrogenous waste); adequate calories
  • Infection Prevention: Immunocompromised; meticulous care of lines

Dialysis Indications

  • Serum potassium >6.5 (life-threatening)
  • Severe metabolic acidosis
  • Severe fluid overload / pulmonary edema
  • Uremia with altered mental status
  • If conservative management fails

Nursing Management

  • Monitor Labs Closely: Daily creatinine, BUN, potassium, sodium
  • Strict I&O: Accurate fluid intake/output records (critical for AKI)
  • Daily Weights: Best indicator of fluid status
  • Monitor Vital Signs: Watch for fluid overload (HTN, JVD, edema) or hypotension
  • Cardiac Monitoring: Watch for dysrhythmias from hyperkalemia
  • Fluid Restriction: Enforce 400-500 mL/day + UOP (can be difficult)
  • Dietary Management: Low potassium, low sodium, controlled protein
  • Prevent Infection: Careful line care; avoid unnecessary catheters
  • Patient Support: Explain AKI is usually reversible (unlike CKD)

Prognosis

  • PRE-renal AKI: Most reversible; good prognosis if treated promptly
  • RENAL AKI (ATN): Longer recovery; higher mortality, especially in ICU
  • POST-renal AKI: Usually reversible once obstruction relieved
  • Mortality: AKI has high mortality in hospitalized patients (~20-50%) because patients usually have multiple comorbidities
  • Long-term Risk: Some patients with severe AKI develop chronic kidney disease

Key Takeaways

  • AKI = rapid kidney function loss over hours-days; REVERSIBLE (unlike CKD)
  • Three causes: PRE-renal (decreased perfusion—most common), RENAL (intrinsic damage), POST-renal (obstruction)
  • Oliguria: <30 mL/hr or <400 mL/24h is the key threshold
  • PRE-renal is most reversible; responds to fluid/perfusion restoration
  • Hyperkalemia is LIFE-THREATENING—monitor closely, use binders, watch for arrhythmias
  • Use 30 mL/hr rule for oliguria—more sensitive than 24-hour calculation
  • Daily weights and strict I&O are critical nursing interventions
  • Dialysis may be needed if conservative management fails
  • High mortality in hospital but potentially good recovery if cause treated early
