Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1: Post-Epistaxis Assessment Priority

A patient who had anterior epistaxis with packing placed 6 hours ago now has the packing removed. Which assessment should the nurse perform FIRST?

A) Pain level on a 0–10 scale
B) Oxygen saturation and airway patency
C) Drainage on the packing that was removed
D) Patient's comfort level with eating/drinking

Correct Answer: B) Oxygen saturation and airway patency
The packing can obstruct the airway or impair breathing, and a patient post-epistaxis is at risk for airway compromise. Using ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation), airway status is always the first priority. Pain and comfort are important but secondary. Drainage is monitored but doesn't take priority over ABC assessment.

Question 2: Sputum Sample Collection Quality

You need to collect a sputum sample for culture and sensitivity from a patient with suspected pneumonia. What is most important to communicate to the patient?

A) Spit the sputum directly into the sterile cup
B) Cough deeply to bring up sputum from the lower lungs, not saliva
C) Rinse your mouth with water first
D) Collect the sample right after meals for best yield

Correct Answer: B) Cough deeply to bring up sputum from the lower lungs, not saliva
A good sputum sample requires a cough that brings up actual sputum (from the lower respiratory tract), not just saliva or spit. The culture needs a genuine specimen from where infection lives. Collecting early morning or 1–2 hours after eating is ideal (when secretions have pooled), not right after meals. Water rinse is okay but the key point is the quality of the specimen.

Question 3: Bronchoscopy NPO Requirements

A patient is scheduled for bronchoscopy this morning to rule out lung cancer. When should NPO (nothing by mouth) start, and why?

A) 2 hours before the procedure (patient just needs an empty stomach)
B) 4 hours before (standard preop fasting)
C) 8 to 12 hours before (the procedure requires sedation and gag reflex protection)
D) 24 hours before (to ensure no aspiration risk)

Correct Answer: C) 8 to 12 hours before
Bronchoscopy requires sedation (conscious sedation or general anesthesia), so a full NPO period of 8–12 hours is standard to prevent aspiration. After the procedure, the patient cannot eat or drink until the gag reflex returns—a protective mechanism that prevents food/fluids from entering the airway. Standard 4-hour fasting is too short for a procedure involving sedation.

Question 4: Suctioning Duration Safety

You are suctioning a patient's endotracheal tube. How long should each pass take?

A) As long as needed to clear all secretions (30–60 seconds)
B) 10 seconds or less per pass
C) 15–20 seconds maximum
D) 5 seconds (suctioning is very traumatic)

Correct Answer: B) 10 seconds or less per pass
Each suctioning pass should be under 10 seconds to minimize airway trauma and hypoxemia. If more suctioning is needed, you can make multiple passes with re-oxygenation between them. Pre-oxygenating the patient before and after suctioning helps maintain saturation.

Question 5: Accidental Decannulation — First Action

A patient with a tracheostomy suddenly coughs forcefully and the trach tube comes completely out. What is your FIRST action?

A) Call for help and get a new trach
B) Insert the obturator into the replacement outer cannula
C) Reinsert the patient's original trach tube immediately
D) Assess the patient's breathing status before doing anything

Correct Answer: B) Insert the obturator into the replacement outer cannula
The obturator is a guide that goes into the OUTER cannula only (not the inner cannula). The sequence is: (1) obturator into outer cannula, (2) extend the neck and open the stoma, (3) insert the outer cannula with obturator, (4) immediately remove the obturator once in place, (5) assess lung sounds and O2 sat, (6) secure the ties. The obturator is never left in—it's only for reinsertion guidance.

Question 6: Trach Cuff Management — Speaking Valve

A patient with a tracheostomy wants to speak using a speaking valve. What must be true first?

A) The cuff must be inflated to seal the trach
B) The cuff must be deflated so air can flow around it
C) The patient must be on the ventilator
D) The inner cannula must be removed

Correct Answer: B) The cuff must be deflated so air can flow around it
A passive mirror (speaking) valve is a one-way valve that requires the cuff to be deflated. If the cuff were inflated, air would have no way around it and the patient would not be able to exhale for phonation (voice production). The deflated cuff lets air flow past the valve for speech.

Question 7: Bedside Safety for Tracheostomy

You're caring for a patient with a new tracheostomy. Which items MUST always be at the bedside for safety?

A) Obturator, two spare trach tubes (one same size, one smaller), Ambu bag with trach adapter
B) Obturator and one spare trach tube
C) Just a suction machine
D) Obturator and an emergency trach kit

Correct Answer: A) Obturator, two spare trach tubes (one same size, one smaller), Ambu bag with trach adapter
If a trach is accidentally dislodged, you need: (1) the obturator to reinsert it, (2) a replacement tube the same size as the current one, (3) a smaller tube in case of swelling/edema, and (4) an Ambu bag with a trach adapter—not a face mask, because air comes out the trach, not the mouth/nose. This is the complete safety setup.

Question 8: Preventing Secretion Thickening in Trach Patients

A patient with a new tracheostomy is at risk for thick secretions that could plug the airway. Which nursing interventions PREVENT this thickening?

A) Change the inner cannula frequently
B) Ensure adequate humidification and keep the patient well hydrated
C) Keep the patient on bed rest
D) Suction the trach every 2 hours

Correct Answer: B) Ensure adequate humidification and keep the patient well hydrated
Changing the inner cannula won't prevent thick secretions from forming—it helps remove them once they're thick. Frequent suctioning addresses thick secretions after they develop but doesn't prevent them. Bed rest actually worsens secretion clearance. Humidification (using humid oxygen) and hydration (drinking fluids, IV fluids) are what PREVENT secretions from thickening in the first place.

Question 9: Temperature and Oxygen Demand

A patient with an infection has a fever of 39°C (102°F). Why is it important to bring this fever down?

A) Fever always causes permanent brain damage
B) High temperature increases cellular metabolic activity, increasing oxygen demand
C) Fever will cause the patient to become dehydrated
D) Masking fever with acetaminophen treats the infection

Correct Answer: B) High temperature increases cellular metabolic activity, increasing oxygen demand
Every degree of temperature rise increases cellular metabolism and O2 consumption—cells need more oxygen to fuel the increased metabolic activity. This is why normalizing temperature is a priority in patients with respiratory compromise or limited oxygen reserves. It's not about preventing brain damage (that's only with extremely high temps) but about reducing the workload on the respiratory system. While dehydration can accompany fever, that's not the primary reason for temperature management in this context.

Question 10: Comprehensive Patient Assessment — Equipment

You're doing morning rounds on a post-op patient with a tracheostomy, Foley catheter, NG tube to suction, and two IV lines. What is part of your ongoing assessment?

A) Only the trach (that's the most critical)
B) The trach and IVs (tubes are always monitored)
C) Everything connected to the patient is part of your assessment—trach, Foley output/appearance, NG tube drainage/function, IVs for patency/infiltration
D) Just ask the patient how they feel

Correct Answer: C) Everything connected to the patient is part of your assessment—trach, Foley output/appearance, NG tube drainage/function, IVs for patency/infiltration
Every piece of equipment, tube, and line connected to a patient is part of the ongoing assessment. For a Foley, you're checking urine color (is it clear, concentrated, bloody?), volume (adequate output?), and whether it's patent. For an NG tube, you're assessing drainage characteristics (bright red indicates bleeding, green/brown indicates GI content), function, and proper placement. This comprehensive equipment assessment catches problems early.

Key Concepts to Remember

  • ABCs always come first: When a trach is out, when a patient is post-epistaxis, when you're assessing priorities—airway and breathing are the first assessment, not comfort or pain.
  • Quality > quantity in specimen collection: A good sputum sample is a cough-produced specimen, not saliva. C&S results take 48–72 hours, so getting the sample right the first time matters.
  • Sedation + airway = long NPO: Bronchoscopy and similar procedures requiring sedation need 8–12 hours NPO to prevent aspiration, and gag reflex return is required before eating/drinking post-procedure.
  • The obturator is a guide, not a keeper: It goes in the OUTER cannula ONLY, and is removed immediately after reinsertion. Never left in place.
  • Temperature matters: Fever increases metabolic O2 demand. Normalizing temperature reduces the work the lungs have to do.
  • Hydration is dual-purpose: It keeps secretions thin (easier to cough/suction) AND supports temperature regulation.
  • Every tube/line is part of assessment: Foleys, NG tubes, IVs, chest tubes, trachs—what's connected is assessed continuously.
