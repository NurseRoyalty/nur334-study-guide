Respiratory Assessment & Clinical Priorities

ABCs of Respiratory Assessment

Always start with the ABCs:

  • Airway: Is it patent? Any stridor, hoarseness, or obstruction?
  • Breathing: What's the rate, depth, effort? Use pulse oximetry (SpO₂) to assess oxygenation status.
  • Circulation: Is there adequate perfusion? Check heart rate, BP, color, skin temperature.

Key concept: ABCs tell you immediately if a patient is in crisis. Never skip this mental checklist.

Pulse Oximetry & Oxygen Saturation Goals

Normal oxygen saturation: 95–100% on room air (sea level)

Acceptable on supplemental oxygen: 94–100% (slightly lower may be acceptable if the patient is chronically low)

COPD patients: Often run 88–92% SpO₂ (CO₂ retainers). Their drive to breathe is based on high CO₂, not low O₂. Giving too much supplemental oxygen can suppress their respiratory drive and worsen their condition.

When weaning oxygen: Gradual reduction while monitoring SpO₂. Do not drop below 88–90% without provider order.

Specimen Collection: Sputum, Blood, Urine

Sputum for culture & sensitivity (C&S):

  • Patient must cough deeply to bring up sputum from the LOWER lungs (where infection lives)—not just saliva or spit.
  • Best collected early morning (when secretions have pooled overnight) or 1–2 hours after meals.
  • Use a sterile cup; do not touch the inside of the container.
  • Label immediately with patient name, date, time, and source.

Blood cultures:

  • Collect from TWO SEPARATE sites (different arms if possible) to differentiate true bacteremia from contamination.
  • Use antiseptic prep (chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine); let dry completely before puncture.
  • Do NOT touch the site after prepping.

Catheterized urine specimen:

  • NEVER collect from the drainage bag—bacteria colonize there.
  • Use the catheter port (a small rubber or self-sealing port on the tubing).
  • Clamp the tubing below the port for 15–20 minutes to allow urine to collect.
  • Use a sterile needle and syringe to aspirate from the port; place in sterile container.

Tracheostomy: Structure & Emergency Care

Tracheostomy anatomy:

  • Outer cannula: The larger outer tube that sits in the stoma.
  • Inner cannula: The smaller tube that sits inside the outer cannula; it can be removed for cleaning.
  • Obturator: A guide rod (looks like a tapered plug) that goes INTO the outer cannula only to make insertion easier. Always remove immediately after reinsertion—never leave in place.

Accidental decannulation emergency sequence:

  1. Stay calm. Keep the stoma open if possible.
  2. Get a replacement trach tube (same size) and obturator.
  3. Insert the obturator INTO the outer cannula ONLY.
  4. Position the patient supine with a rolled towel under the shoulders to extend the neck slightly and open the stoma.
  5. Gently insert the outer cannula (with obturator) into the stoma at a 45° angle, then straighten.
  6. Immediately remove the obturator.
  7. Insert the inner cannula and secure all ties.
  8. Assess breath sounds, SpO₂, and work of breathing immediately.

Bedside emergency equipment: Keep oxygen, suction, replacement trach tube, obturator, and ambu bag at the bedside at all times for a trach patient.

Bronchoscopy: Preparation & Patient Care

What it is: Direct visualization of the airways using a flexible or rigid scope to diagnose (biopsy, brushings, washings) or treat (remove foreign objects, place stents).

Pre-procedure:

  • NPO 8–12 hours before (requires sedation; aspiration risk).
  • Remove dentures, contact lenses, jewelry.
  • Sign informed consent.
  • Pre-procedure medications may include an anticholinergic (atropine) to reduce secretions.

During the procedure: Patient receives conscious sedation or light general anesthesia. The scope is passed through the mouth or nose.

Post-procedure:

  • Nothing by mouth until the gag reflex returns (usually 1–2 hours). The gag reflex protects the airway.
  • Assess for sore throat, hoarseness, chest discomfort, shortness of breath.
  • Monitor SpO₂ and vital signs.
  • Warn patient: throat may feel sore, voice may be hoarse for a day or two.
  • Minor blood-tinged sputum is normal; large amounts require provider notification.

Suctioning: Technique & Safety

Why suctioning matters: It removes secretions but also removes oxygen. Balance is key.

Standard technique:

  • Pre-oxygenate: Give 100% O₂ for 2–3 minutes before suctioning (via bag-valve-mask or on the ventilator).
  • Insert the catheter: Without suction applied, gently advance the suction catheter down the airway.
  • Apply suction on withdrawal: Use a twisting motion as you pull back. Each pass should take 10 seconds or less.
  • Limit passes: Do not exceed 3 passes; re-oxygenate between passes.
  • Post-oxygenate: Provide 100% O₂ for 1–2 minutes after suctioning to restore oxygen levels.

Dangers of prolonged suctioning: Hypoxemia, airway trauma, increased intracranial pressure (in brain-injured patients), dysrhythmias.

Signs to stop immediately: SpO₂ dropping, heart rate changing, patient becoming distressed.

Epistaxis: Anterior vs. Posterior

Anterior epistaxis (80% of cases):

  • Bleeding from Kiesselbach plexus (rich network of vessels in anterior nasal septum).
  • Treatment: Have patient sit upright, lean forward (not back—don't swallow blood). Apply direct pressure to the soft part of the nose for 10–15 minutes.
  • Can often be stopped with ice, pressure, and maybe topical epinephrine (1:1000).
  • If uncontrolled, nasal packing (anterior packing) is applied for 24–48 hours.

Posterior epistaxis (20% of cases, more serious):

  • Bleeding from posterior ethmoid or sphenopalatine arteries (deep in the nasopharynx).
  • Often triggered by hypertension, coagulopathy, or trauma.
  • Presentation: Blood drains into throat (patient may swallow blood, not have anterior nosebleed).
  • Treatment: Requires posterior packing (often with a Foley balloon catheter) and usually admission. May need interventional radiology (embolization) if severe.
  • Emergency risk: Airway compromise, aspiration of blood, hypovolemic shock.

Post-packing care: Monitor for airway obstruction, nosebleed recurrence, and infection. Patient on antibiotics while packed.

Respiratory Labs & Diagnostics Quick Reference

Sputum culture & sensitivity: Identifies organism and best antibiotic. Takes 48–72 hours.

AFB (Acid-Fast Bacillus) smear: Looks for tuberculosis. Requires special staining; takes 24 hours. SMEAR POSITIVE = contagious. Patient needs respiratory isolation.

Chest X-ray: Standard imaging for respiratory complaints. Can show pneumonia, pneumothorax, effusion, masses.

High-resolution CT chest: Better detail than X-ray; used when X-ray inconclusive or to evaluate masses. Contrast may be used but is contraindicated if CKD (contrast-induced acute kidney injury risk).

Bronchoscopy with biopsy: Gold standard for diagnosis of masses, infiltrates, or staging lung cancer. Also used therapeutically (remove secretions, place stents).

PET scan: Helps stage cancer (hypermetabolic tumors light up); used before surgery to rule out metastases.

Thoracentesis: Needle aspiration of pleural fluid for diagnosis (infection, malignancy) or therapeutic (remove large effusions). Risks: pneumothorax, hemothorax, infection.

Clinical Judgment Questions

Question 1: Post-Epistaxis Assessment Priority

A patient who had anterior epistaxis with packing placed 6 hours ago now has the packing removed. Which assessment should the nurse perform FIRST?

A) Pain level on a 0–10 scale
B) Oxygen saturation and airway patency
C) Drainage on the packing that was removed
D) Patient's comfort level with eating/drinking

Correct Answer: B) Oxygen saturation and airway patency
The packing can obstruct the airway or impair breathing, and a patient post-epistaxis is at risk for airway compromise. Using ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation), airway status is always the first priority. Pain and comfort are important but secondary. Drainage is monitored but doesn't take priority over ABC assessment.

Question 2: Sputum Sample Collection Quality

You need to collect a sputum sample for culture and sensitivity from a patient with suspected pneumonia. What is most important to communicate to the patient?

A) Spit the sputum directly into the sterile cup
B) Cough deeply to bring up sputum from the lower lungs, not saliva
C) Rinse your mouth with water first
D) Collect the sample right after meals for best yield

Correct Answer: B) Cough deeply to bring up sputum from the lower lungs, not saliva
A good sputum sample requires a cough that brings up actual sputum (from the lower respiratory tract), not just saliva or spit. The culture needs a genuine specimen from where infection lives. Collecting early morning or 1–2 hours after eating is ideal (when secretions have pooled), not right after meals. Water rinse is okay but the key point is the quality of the specimen.

Question 3: Bronchoscopy NPO Requirements

A patient is scheduled for bronchoscopy this morning to rule out lung cancer. When should NPO (nothing by mouth) start, and why?

A) 2 hours before the procedure (patient just needs an empty stomach)
B) 4 hours before (standard preop fasting)
C) 8 to 12 hours before (the procedure requires sedation and gag reflex protection)
D) 24 hours before (to ensure no aspiration risk)

Correct Answer: C) 8 to 12 hours before
Bronchoscopy requires sedation (conscious sedation or general anesthesia), so a full NPO period of 8–12 hours is standard to prevent aspiration. After the procedure, the patient cannot eat or drink until the gag reflex returns—a protective mechanism that prevents food/fluids from entering the airway. Standard 4-hour fasting is too short for a procedure involving sedation.

Question 4: Suctioning Duration Safety

You are suctioning a patient's endotracheal tube. How long should each pass take?

A) As long as needed to clear all secretions (30–60 seconds)
B) 10 seconds or less per pass
C) 15–20 seconds maximum
D) 5 seconds (suctioning is very traumatic)

Correct Answer: B) 10 seconds or less per pass
Each suctioning pass should be under 10 seconds to minimize airway trauma and hypoxemia. If more suctioning is needed, you can make multiple passes with re-oxygenation between them. Pre-oxygenating the patient before and after suctioning helps maintain saturation.

Question 5: Accidental Decannulation — First Action

A patient with a tracheostomy suddenly coughs forcefully and the trach tube comes completely out. What is your FIRST action?

A) Call for help and get a new trach
B) Insert the obturator into the replacement outer cannula
C) Reinsert the patient's original trach tube immediately
D) Assess the patient's breathing status before doing anything

Correct Answer: B) Insert the obturator into the replacement outer cannula
The obturator is a guide that goes into the OUTER cannula only (not the inner cannula). The sequence is: (1) obturator into outer cannula, (2) extend the neck and open the stoma, (3) insert the outer cannula with obturator, (4) immediately remove the obturator once in place, (5) assess lung sounds and O2 sat, (6) secure the ties. The obturator is never left in—it's only for reinsertion guidance.
