Oxygenation & Tracheostomy Management

Enhanced with live lecture clinical emphasis on assessments, specimen collection, respiratory diagnostics, and comprehensive tracheostomy care with critical safety details.

Pulse Oximetry Assessment

Normal Values & Interpretation

  • Normal O2 saturation: 95-100%
  • Clinical consideration: Don't aim for 100% on supplemental oxygen - goal is to wean down and normalize toward 95-98% as patient improves
  • Readings below 95% in non-compromised patients warrant intervention
  • Pulse oximeter must have good peripheral perfusion to read accurately - assess finger color and capillary refill

Placement & Technique

  • Fingertip placement (index finger preferred, thumb acceptable if sensor can wrap around)
  • Ear lobe for patients with poor peripheral perfusion
  • Avoid placing on same arm as blood pressure cuff (cuff inflation prevents reading)
  • Remove nail polish or artificial nails - interferes with light transmission
  • Ensure good contact - sensor should not be loose or wet
ASSESSMENT PRIORITY: When patient presents with any respiratory concern, ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) come first. Check O2 sat immediately, then assess work of breathing, then other parameters. Do not get distracted by comfort concerns when airway status is questionable.

Specimen Collection: Sputum Studies

Culture & Sensitivity (C&S) Collection

CRITICAL: Good sputum requires patient to COUGH, not just spit saliva. Spit samples are contaminated and worthless.
  • Timing: Early morning sample preferred (secretions accumulated overnight)
  • Technique: Have patient rinse mouth with water first to minimize oral flora contamination
  • Collection container: Sterile sputum cup - label immediately with patient ID, time, date
  • Results timeline: Culture takes 48-72 hours; do not expect results same-day
  • Gram stain: Can be processed faster for preliminary organism identification

Quality Indicators for Sputum Sample

  • Sample should contain actual sputum (respiratory secretion), not saliva
  • Volume: minimum 1mL, preferably 5mL for adequate culture
  • Appearance: purulent or mucopurulent (indicates infection)
  • Reject sample if contaminated with saliva or food particles
NURSING ASSESSMENT: When obtaining sputum samples, assess the patient's ability to cough effectively. Note: thick, tenacious secretions? Color? Amount? Odor? These observations help guide treatment decisions while waiting for culture results.

Respiratory Diagnostics

Imaging Studies

  • Chest X-ray: First-line imaging for most respiratory concerns; assess for infiltrates, pneumothorax, effusion, consolidation
  • CT chest: Higher resolution for detailed parenchymal and mediastinal assessment
  • Nursing role: Position patient for clear imaging (upright if possible), remove jewelry/metallic objects, ensure patient cooperation with breath-holding instructions

Bronchoscopy

Pre-procedure NPO Status: Patient must be NPO 8-12 hours before bronchoscopy (nothing by mouth - no food, fluids, or medications that need food)
  • Procedure: Direct visualization of trachea and bronchi using flexible or rigid scope
  • Indications: Evaluate hemoptysis, suspected malignancy, assess airway, remove foreign body or secretions
  • Post-procedure assessment: Monitor for return of gag reflex before allowing patient to eat/drink - risk of aspiration if reflex not intact
  • Common finding: Temporary hoarseness after procedure is normal

Lung Biopsy

  • Methods: Transbronchial (through bronchoscope), percutaneous (needle), or open (surgical)
  • Post-biopsy monitoring: Watch for pneumothorax, hemoptysis, infection
  • Chest X-ray: Obtained after procedure to rule out pneumothorax

Thoracentesis

  • Procedure: Needle aspiration of pleural fluid from pleural space
  • Positioning: Patient sits upright and leans forward (allows better access to fluid pocket)
  • Post-procedure: Monitor for shortness of breath, chest pain, decreased breath sounds (signs of pneumothorax)
  • Specimen handling: Label tubes for different analyses (cell count, chemistry, culture, cytology)

Tracheostomy Care: Clinical Essentials

Bedside Safety Items (Must Always Be Present)

Every trach patient requires these items at bedside - lack of them is a safety violation:
  • Obturator (same size as patient's trach)
  • Spare tracheostomy tube (same size)
  • Backup tracheostomy tube (one size smaller)
  • Ambu bag (manual resuscitation bag) with trach adapter
  • Suction equipment and supplies

Insertion Technique & Obturator Use

CRITICAL STEP: When inserting a new tracheostomy tube, the obturator goes ONLY in the OUTER cannula as a guide. After placement is confirmed (ability to pass suction catheter through, lung sounds present), the obturator is removed IMMEDIATELY. It never stays in during normal trach management.

Suctioning

Critical Timing: Each suctioning pass should NOT exceed 10 seconds of actual suction applied. Longer passes cause hypoxemia and tissue trauma.

Temperature Regulation & Oxygenation

Clinical Link: Fever INCREASES cellular oxygen demand. For every 1 degree Celsius elevation, metabolic rate increases approximately 10%. Normalizing temperature reduces metabolic demand and improves oxygenation status.

Hydration & Respiratory Secretion Management

Dual Purpose of Hydration: Adequate fluid intake serves TWO critical functions:
  1. Secretion viscosity: Hydration keeps secretions thin and mobile; dehydration = thick, tenacious secretions
  2. Temperature regulation: Adequate hydration supports body's ability to regulate temperature and maintain perfusion
