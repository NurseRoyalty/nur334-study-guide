Fluid & Electrolytes

NUR 325 review, covering: fluid compartments and fluid movement (diffusion, osmosis, colloids, hydrostatic/oncotic pressure), electrolyte basics, and imbalances/replacement for sodium, potassium, magnesium, calcium, and phosphorus.

Fluid Compartments, Movement & Pressures

About 50–60% of adult body weight is water — fluid containing dissolved/suspended substances like glucose, electrolytes, and proteins. It's stored in two spaces: intracellular (inside cells — about 70% of body fluid) and extracellular (outside cells — about 30%). Extracellular fluid splits further into interstitial fluid (between cells — a cushion) and intravascular fluid (plasma, the liquid part of blood). Body fluid transports nutrients/waste, acts as a solvent, and maintains temperature, digestion/elimination, acid-base balance, and joint/tissue lubrication.

ProcessWhat MovesDirection
OsmosisWater, across a semi-permeable membrane.Low solute concentration → high solute concentration, until concentrations equalize (or hydrostatic pressure opposes further movement).
DiffusionMolecules/solutes.High concentration → low concentration, until concentration is equal. Liquid volume stays the same.

The body constantly works toward homeostasis — balancing solvents (fluid) and solutes (electrolytes/particles). Osmotic pressure drives water across the cell membrane toward the more concentrated side: if solutes build up on one side of a membrane, water shifts in to balance it out, which is why severe water loss/gain or electrolyte imbalances also cause fluid imbalances.

Colloids

  • Substances that raise colloid osmotic pressure (also called oncotic pressure) and pull fluid from the interstitial compartment into the plasma/blood compartment.
  • Three primary colloids: albumin (the main one), globulin, and fibrinogen — measured together as total protein level (a measure of oncotic pressure, not an indicator of dietary protein intake).
  • Colloid oncotic pressure decreases with age and with malnutrition; can be replaced with colloid replacement products (more detail in Med-Surg/critical care).
PressureDriven ByWhat It Does
Hydrostatic PressureBlood pressure, at the capillary level.Pushes fluid out of the vascular system into the interstitial space, supplying nutrients to tissues/cells.
Oncotic (Colloid Osmotic) PressurePlasma colloids (mainly albumin).Pulls fluid into the vascular space from tissue — plasma proteins attract water, which helps remove metabolic waste from cells/tissues.

Electrolyte Basics

Electrolytes are substances that carry an electrical charge in solution. They influence fluid balance, acid-base balance, nerve impulses, muscle contraction, heart rhythm, and many other cell functions — and because electrolytes work together, a change in one often affects others (nothing happens in a silo). Electrolyte concentration depends on intake (diet), absorption (e.g., small intestine function), distribution (charge balance between compartments), and excretion (mainly the kidneys).

LocationHighest-Concentration Electrolytes
IntracellularPotassium, magnesium, phosphorus.
ExtracellularSodium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium.

Sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium are positively charged (cations); phosphorus, chloride, and bicarbonate are negatively charged (anions). Positive and negative charges balance each other out — a process called electroneutrality — which is why losing (or replacing) one electrolyte often shifts another. Normal reference ranges vary by lab/equipment; the values below are what this course uses.

Memory hooks for which body system each major electrolyte imbalance tends to hit first: sodium → brain (confusion, altered LOC), potassium → heart (cardiac rhythm disturbances), magnesium → muscle ("M for magnesium, M for muscle movement"), and calcium → neuromuscular (numbness/tingling, tetany, hyperactive reflexes).

Sodium Imbalances — Hyponatremia & Hypernatremia

Normal sodium: 136–145. Below 136 is hyponatremia; above 145 is hypernatremia. Sodium is the main extracellular cation and the primary driver of fluid balance — "where salt goes, water follows." Sodium governs osmolality (concentration in blood vessel/interstitial space, not what's inside the cell), influences acid-base balance, and activates muscle/nerve action potentials.

Hyponatremia (< 136)

  • Causes: GI losses (diarrhea, vomiting, fistulas, NG suction), renal losses (adrenal insufficiency, diuretics), skin losses (burns, wound damage), fasting/not eating or drinking, polydipsia (water intoxication), or excess hypotonic fluid.
  • Signs/symptoms: confusion and altered LOC (cells swell as water shifts in — brain cells are especially sensitive), anorexia, muscle weakness; if severe/untreated, seizures and coma. CNS symptoms appear first.

Hypernatremia (> 145)

  • Causes: essentially never dietary intake (near-impossible to eat enough salt) — instead IV fluids/tube feeds high in sodium, saltwater near-drowning, or inadequate water intake/excess water loss (severe diarrhea, high fever, heat stroke, profound diuresis).
  • Signs/symptoms: altered LOC, confusion, seizure, coma, extreme thirst, dry/sticky mucous membranes, muscle cramps — cells shrink as water shifts out.

Hyponatremia specifically breaks down into two causes with opposite fluid pictures, both showing the same CNS symptoms:

TypeCauseFluid Picture
Dilutional HyponatremiaToo much water taken in — sodium is diluted.Hypervolemia: increased blood pressure, weight gain, rapid/bounding pulse, increased urine specific gravity.
Depletional HyponatremiaAn actual loss of sodium and fluid.Hypovolemia: decreased blood pressure, tachycardia, dry skin, weight loss, decreased urine specific gravity.

Sodium Correction & Sodium Bicarbonate

Sodium imbalances must be corrected slowly — think sodium, think brain, think slow. Correcting hyponatremia too fast (especially one that's lasted > 48 hours) causes brain dehydration and risks osmotic demyelination syndrome, which can lead to irreversible neurologic damage, locked-in syndrome, or brain herniation. Correcting hypernatremia too fast risks cerebral edema. Either direction, sodium is corrected gradually — typically over about 48 hours — with frequent lab monitoring.
SituationTreatment
Depletional HyponatremiaSodium replacement (PO or IV — often normal saline); withhold diuretics until the imbalance is fixed.
Dilutional HyponatremiaFluid restriction instead of sodium replacement — fluid levels come down and sodium concentration corrects.
Hypernatremia (sodium excess)Remove sodium, via fluids.
Hypernatremia (water loss)Add water.

Sodium bicarbonate dissociates to provide bicarbonate ions, neutralizing acid concentration and raising blood/urine pH — its main indication is metabolic acidosis, though it also raises plasma sodium concentration. It's reserved for people with long-term acid-base-related sodium deficiency, not routine hyponatremia treatment; the IV route is not used for hyponatremia. Given PO or IV. It's a vesicant at high concentrations (give via central line when possible). Adverse effects: edema, cerebral hemorrhage, hypernatremia, other electrolyte abnormalities, metabolic alkalosis, tetany, and pulmonary edema/heart failure exacerbation (from the fluid shifts). Monitor cardiac status/telemetry, ABGs, other electrolytes, and IV patency; watch for interactions when mixed with other sodium solutions.

Potassium Imbalances — Hypokalemia & Hyperkalemia

Normal potassium: 3.5–5. Below 3.5 is hypokalemia; above 5 is hyperkalemia. Potassium is an intracellular cation — 98% of the body's potassium lives inside cells, maintained by an ATP-driven active transport pump. It's the main driver of cardiac and muscle cell contraction/excitability, and it helps control intracellular osmolality. Diet is the main source of potassium; the kidneys are the main route of loss.

Hypokalemia (< 3.5)

  • Causes: renal/GI losses (diarrhea, vomiting, ileostomy drainage, diuresis/furosemide), acid-base disorders (alkalosis shifts potassium from extracellular into intracellular space). Rarely from inadequate dietary intake alone.
  • Signs/symptoms: cardiac rhythm disturbances (can be lethal if severe), muscle weakness, leg cramps, decreased bowel motility → constipation, nausea, ileus.

Hyperkalemia (> 5)

  • Causes: decreased potassium output (renal failure, low urine output), massive cell injury releasing intracellular potassium (burns, crush injuries, sepsis), or certain drugs (potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs).
  • Signs/symptoms: peaked T waves on ECG, muscle weakness, cramps, abdominal cramping, diarrhea, vomiting.

Potassium Replacement — Oral & IV

Potassium chloride (KCl) is given to treat/prevent potassium depletion when dietary measures aren't enough — there's no real "mechanism of action" beyond replacing what's missing. Oral forms (powder mixed into liquid, or liquid) taste awful and can cause GI distress/ulcers/bleeding — dilute with water or juice, and assess for nausea/vomiting. Pill form can be a large, hard-to-swallow tablet.

IV potassium must always be diluted and can never be pushed IV — undiluted IV potassium can cause ventricular fibrillation, a lethal rhythm incompatible with life. It must be given slowly (usually over an hour or more): max 10 mEq/hr peripherally, up to 20 mEq/hr via a central line. Even in an emergency, potassium correction speed cannot be increased. The patient needs documented urine output/functioning kidneys before giving it (contraindicated in renal failure — question any order to give potassium to a dialysis patient), telemetry monitoring during administration, and frequent potassium level checks. Central line preferred (peripheral IV potassium can cause phlebitis/pain).

Hyperkalemia Treatment

TreatmentDetail
Kayexalate (Sodium Polystyrene Sulfonate)A cation exchange resin — binds potassium in the digestive tract and swaps it for sodium, lowering serum potassium by about 0.5–1 mEq/L over 4–6 hours. Given PO, or rectally as a powder/suspension/enema. Only use with normal bowel function — not for patients who are constipated or have an ileus. Adverse effects: constipation, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, hypokalemia; serious: intestinal obstruction and intestinal necrosis.
D50 + Regular InsulinA combination (10 units regular insulin + 1 ampule of 50% dextrose) that shifts potassium temporarily from outside cells to inside — used in urgent/emergent situations (e.g., cardiac dysrhythmia from hyperkalemia) for a fast, short-term fix. Insulin drives the potassium shift; dextrose prevents the hypo/hyperglycemia that giving insulin alone would cause.

Insulin's most important safety rule — always check a current blood sugar before the dose — applies here too. With the D50/insulin cocktail, recheck blood sugar about 5 minutes after giving the D50 (or sooner if hypoglycemia symptoms appear). Full insulin safety detail in Diabetes & Metabolic Syndrome

Magnesium Imbalances & Replacement

Normal magnesium: 1.3–2.1. Below 1.3 is hypomagnesemia; above 2.1 is hypermagnesemia. Magnesium and potassium are closely linked — magnesium blocks/controls potassium's movement out of cardiac cells, stabilizing cardiac and smooth muscle.

Hypomagnesemia (< 1.3)

  • Causes: diuresis, GI/renal losses, limited intake (starvation, fasting), alcohol abuse, pancreatitis, hyperglycemia.
  • Signs/symptoms: overactive-muscle picture — hyperactive reflexes, confusion, cramps, tremors, seizures, and nystagmus (rapid, uncontrolled eye movement — side to side, up/down, or circular; usually bilateral).

Hypermagnesemia (> 2.1)

  • Causes: increased intake accompanied by renal failure (e.g., milk of magnesia in a patient with chronic renal failure — think elderly patients).
  • Signs/symptoms: opposite of low magnesium — lethargy, floppiness, muscle weakness, decreased reflexes, flushed/warm skin; very high levels can drop pulse/blood pressure.

Magnesium sulfate (IV) or magnesium oxide (PO) replace magnesium — one of the few electrolytes that can be given IV push if absolutely necessary, though IV administration can burn. Beyond replacing a deficiency, magnesium sulfate is also used for constipation, and in OB to prevent/treat seizures from preeclampsia/eclampsia, as well as for cardiac rhythm disturbances. Treating hypermagnesemia: stop replacement (if intake-driven), or dialysis to remove excess magnesium.

Calcium Imbalances

Normal calcium: 9–10.5. Below 9 is hypocalcemia; above 10.5 is hypercalcemia. About 99% of body calcium is stored in bone (thyroid/parathyroid hormones control release from and absorption into bone); of the small remainder, about 40% is protein-bound, 10% is bound to other substances, and 50% is ionized and available for use. Calcium is needed for enzyme reactions, membrane potential/nerve excitability, skeletal/cardiac/smooth muscle contraction, hormone/neurotransmitter release, cardiac contractility/automaticity, and blood clotting.

TypeCausesSigns/Symptoms
Hypocalcemia (< 9)Inability to mobilize calcium from bone (hypoparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia), increased renal loss (renal failure), increased binding, decreased intake/absorption or vitamin D, acute pancreatitis, thyroid/parathyroid surgery (monitor closely — calcium can drop suddenly post-op).Increased neuromuscular excitability: numbness/tingling, muscle cramps, bone pain, tetany, laryngeal spasms, hyperactive reflexes; prolonged QT interval (arrhythmia risk).
Hypercalcemia (> 10.5)Hyperparathyroidism, certain cancers (lung, breast, hematologic), immobility, antacid overdose (e.g., Tums).Acts as a sedative: fatigue, lethargy, confusion, weakness; very high/prolonged levels can cause seizures and coma. Chronic hypercalcemia raises kidney stone risk.

Two Classic Signs of Low Calcium

  • Trousseau's sign — inflate a blood pressure cuff to ~20 mmHg above the patient's systolic pressure for about 3 minutes; a resulting carpal (wrist/hand) spasm is a positive sign.
  • Chvostek's sign — gently tap the facial nerve just in front of the ear; ipsilateral twitching of facial muscles is a positive sign.

Calcium & Phosphorus Treatment

SituationTreatment
HypocalcemiaIV calcium chloride (ionized, preferred form) or calcium gluconate — both are vesicants, given via central line. Oral calcium (elemental calcium, calcium carbonate/Tums) is also used. May also need vitamin D — an active form if the patient has impaired liver/kidney function.
HypercalcemiaAdequate hydration (3–4 L/day) to increase urine output, promote renal calcium excretion, and reduce kidney stone risk; diuretics (sodium excretion is accompanied by calcium excretion); dialysis if the patient has renal failure.

Phosphorus has an inverse relationship with calcium — low calcium accompanies high phosphate, and vice versa. Normal phosphorus: 3.0–4.5 (below 3.0 is hypophosphatemia, above 4.5 is hyperphosphatemia). About 85% of body phosphorus is in bone, 14% is intracellular, and only about 1% is extracellular (what's measured on a blood draw is the circulating inorganic form). Phosphorus plays roles in bone formation, ATP formation, glucose/protein/fat metabolism enzymes, DNA/RNA structure, acid-base buffering, and normal WBC/platelet function.

Phosphorus Imbalances

TypeCausesSigns/Symptoms
Hypophosphatemia (< 3.0)Decreased absorption (antacid overdose), severe diarrhea, increased kidney elimination, malnutrition (alcoholism, TPN use, recovery from malnutrition — re-feeding syndrome, where reintroducing nutrition too fast in a severely malnourished patient causes a critically low phosphate; introduce nutrition slowly to prevent it).Mild/moderate: few manifestations. Severe: tremors, paresthesia, confusion up to coma, seizures, muscle weakness, joint stiffness, bone pain, hemolytic anemia, platelet dysfunction, impaired WBC function.
Hyperphosphatemia (> 4.5)Kidney failure, phosphate-containing laxatives/enemas, massive trauma or heat stroke (intracellular phosphate shifts out), hypoparathyroidism.Usually asymptomatic on its own — symptoms seen are actually from the accompanying low calcium (inverse relationship): muscle spasms, paresthesia, tetany.

Treatment: for low phosphate, IV (given slowly over a long period) or oral replacement — use oral intake cautiously in chronic kidney disease/hypocalcemia (risk of phosphate calcifications in joints). For high phosphate, treat the cause — calcium-based phosphate binders, or hemodialysis in renal failure.

Self-Test Flashcards

What's the difference between osmosis and diffusion?
Osmosis is the movement of WATER across a semi-permeable membrane, from an area of low solute concentration to high solute concentration. Diffusion is the movement of MOLECULES/SOLUTES from an area of high concentration to low concentration. Both stop once concentrations equalize.
What drives hydrostatic pressure vs. oncotic pressure, and which direction does each push fluid?
Hydrostatic pressure is driven by blood pressure at the capillary level and pushes fluid OUT of the vascular system into the interstitial space. Oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure is driven by plasma colloids (mainly albumin) and pulls fluid INTO the vascular space from the tissue.
What's electroneutrality, and which electrolytes are cations vs. anions?
Electroneutrality is the balancing of positive and negative electrolyte charges in the body — losing or replacing one electrolyte often shifts another. Cations (positive): sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium. Anions (negative): phosphorus, chloride, bicarbonate.
A patient is hyponatremic. How do you tell dilutional from depletional hyponatremia at the bedside?
Dilutional (too much water, sodium diluted) looks hypervolemic: increased BP, weight gain, bounding pulse, increased urine specific gravity — treated with fluid restriction. Depletional (actual sodium/fluid loss) looks hypovolemic: decreased BP, tachycardia, dry skin, weight loss, decreased urine specific gravity — treated with sodium replacement (often normal saline).
Why must sodium imbalances always be corrected slowly, regardless of direction?
Correcting hyponatremia too fast (especially if it's lasted > 48 hr) causes brain dehydration and risks osmotic demyelination syndrome — irreversible neurologic damage, even locked-in syndrome. Correcting hypernatremia too fast risks cerebral edema. Both directions are corrected gradually, typically over about 48 hours.
Why can undiluted potassium never be given as an IV push?
Undiluted IV potassium can cause ventricular fibrillation — a lethal rhythm incompatible with life. IV potassium must always be diluted and infused slowly (max 10 mEq/hr peripherally, 20 mEq/hr via central line), with telemetry monitoring, even in an emergency.
What's the mechanism of Kayexalate, and what's the key contraindication?
Kayexalate (sodium polystyrene sulfonate) is a cation exchange resin that binds potassium in the digestive tract and swaps it for sodium, lowering serum potassium. It's only appropriate for patients with NORMAL bowel function — not for constipated patients or those with an ileus, due to the risk of intestinal obstruction/necrosis.
How does the D50/insulin combination treat hyperkalemia, and what must be monitored?
Insulin shifts potassium from outside cells to inside (temporarily, not a true reduction) — dextrose (D50) is given alongside to prevent the hypo/hyperglycemia insulin would otherwise cause. It's for urgent/emergent hyperkalemia (e.g., cardiac dysrhythmia). Check blood sugar before giving it, and again about 5 minutes after the D50.
How do the signs/symptoms of low vs. high magnesium differ, and what's the classic eye sign of hypomagnesemia?
Low magnesium = overactive muscles: hyperactive reflexes, confusion, cramps, tremors, seizures, and nystagmus (rapid uncontrolled eye movement). High magnesium = the opposite: lethargy, floppiness, muscle weakness, decreased reflexes, flushed/warm skin.
How do you elicit Chvostek's sign vs. Trousseau's sign, and what do both indicate?
Chvostek's sign: tap the facial nerve just in front of the ear — ipsilateral facial muscle twitching is positive. Trousseau's sign: inflate a BP cuff ~20 mmHg above systolic for ~3 minutes — a resulting carpal spasm is positive. Both are classic signs of LOW CALCIUM (hypocalcemia).
What's re-feeding syndrome, and which electrolyte is the earliest warning sign?
Re-feeding syndrome happens when nutrition is reintroduced too quickly to a severely malnourished patient, causing electrolyte disturbances. A critically low PHOSPHATE level is one of the first signs — prevent it by introducing nutrition slowly.
A patient has high phosphorus but is largely asymptomatic except for muscle spasms and tetany. Why?
Phosphorus has an inverse relationship with calcium — high phosphate is accompanied by low calcium. Hyperphosphatemia itself is usually asymptomatic; the muscle spasms, paresthesia, and tetany seen are actually signs of the accompanying hypocalcemia.
