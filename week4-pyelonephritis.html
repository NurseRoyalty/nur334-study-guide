Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI)

← Back to Study Guide

Overview

Pyelonephritis is a serious infection involving the kidney parenchyma and collecting system—this is more than just a bladder infection. It's an UPPER UTI characterized by systemic manifestations, not just localized bladder symptoms.

  • Requires prompt treatment to prevent sepsis and permanent kidney damage
  • Often results from untreated lower UTI that ascends the ureters
  • Can be life-threatening without appropriate intervention

Clinical Manifestations

The FLANK Mnemonic (Key Symptoms)

  • F = Fever — High fever, typically >38.5°C (101.3°F). This is SYSTEMIC, not localized.
  • L = Lumbar (Flank) Pain — Severe pain in the flank region on affected side
  • A = Abdominal Distension/Tenderness — Often present; patient very uncomfortable
  • N = Nausea/Vomiting — Common systemic response to infection
  • K = Kidney Tenderness (CVA) — Tenderness elicited on Costovertebral Angle percussion

Additional Features

  • Chills and malaise (systemic signs)
  • May have dysuria and urinary frequency (from lower UTI component)
  • Range of severity: From mildly fatigued to dramatic onset with chills and high fever
Red Flag: Systemic symptoms (fever, chills, malaise) are what distinguish pyelonephritis from uncomplicated cystitis. This requires urgent treatment.

CVA Tenderness Assessment

What is CVA Tenderness?

CVA = Costovertebral Angle (NOT Cerebrovascular Accident, which is stroke). The CVA is the anatomical angle formed by the last rib and the spine on each side of the body. The kidneys lie deep in this region.

How to Assess

  • Patient sits upright or lies prone
  • Place your NON-dominant hand over the CVA (that angle region)
  • Strike your hand gently with your dominant fist (percuss)
  • Patient with pyelonephritis will experience excruciating tenderness and may jump

Why This Happens

When inflammation and infection affect the kidney parenchyma, the entire kidney area becomes hypersensitive. Even light percussion causes significant discomfort.

Important: CVA tenderness is an UNEXPECTED finding and indicates upper UTI involvement. This is not normal and requires immediate provider notification.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic Tests

  • Urinalysis: Pyuria (WBCs) with WBC casts (indicates renal involvement)
  • Urine Culture: Essential—confirms organism and guides antibiotic selection
  • CBC with Differential: Elevated WBC count; look for shift to left (increased neutrophils and bands—immature WBCs)
    • "Shift to the left" = increased immature WBCs = severe infection
  • Blood Cultures: If sepsis suspected—look for bacteremia
  • Imaging: Ultrasound or CT to rule out obstruction (kidney stones, abscess)

Treatment

Mild Pyelonephritis (Outpatient)

  • Adequate oral fluid intake
  • NSAIDs or acetaminophen for symptom management
  • Oral antibiotics (broad-spectrum initially, then narrowed per culture)
  • Follow-up with urine culture and imaging
  • Note: Antibiotics typically continued for 14-21 days (longer than uncomplicated UTI)

Severe Pyelonephritis (Hospitalization)

  • IV Hydration: Critical to restore intravascular volume and renal perfusion
  • IV Antibiotics: Fluoroquinolones or cephalosporins (more aggressive than oral therapy)
  • Antipyretics: NSAIDs or acetaminophen to reduce fever and inflammation
  • Switch to Oral: Once nausea/vomiting improves and patient tolerating PO intake
  • Follow-up Cultures: Repeat urine culture 14-21 days after therapy starts
  • Monitor Closely: Watch for signs of sepsis (hypotension, tachycardia, altered mental status)

Nursing Management

Priorities

  • Monitor Vital Signs Closely: Watch for fever spikes and signs of sepsis
    • Tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea are concerning
    • Altered mental status suggests sepsis progression
  • Fluid Management:
    • Encourage fluid intake (dilutes urine, promotes flushing)
    • IV hydration if hospitalized
    • Monitor I&O carefully
  • Pain Management: Analgesics and comfort measures for flank pain
  • Infection Control: Standard precautions; teach patient about completing full antibiotic course

Patient Teaching

  • COMPLETE the full course of antibiotics (typically 14-21 days)—don't stop when feeling better
  • Drink adequate fluids daily
  • Return for follow-up urine culture as scheduled
  • Report fever, increased flank pain, or inability to tolerate medications
  • Proper perineal hygiene to prevent recurrence

Potential Complications

  • Sepsis: Progression to bacteremia and systemic inflammatory response
  • Renal Abscess: Collection of pus within kidney tissue
  • Permanent Renal Scarring: Repeated episodes → chronic kidney damage
  • Chronic Kidney Disease: If severe or recurrent pyelonephritis

Key Takeaways

  • Pyelonephritis is serious—it's an upper UTI with SYSTEMIC manifestations
  • FLANK mnemonic captures the key symptoms: Fever, Lumbar pain, Abdominal tenderness, Nausea, Kidney tenderness
  • CVA tenderness is pathognomonic (characteristic) of pyelonephritis
  • Requires longer antibiotic course (14-21 days) than simple cystitis
  • WBC casts in urine indicate renal involvement
  • Watch closely for progression to sepsis—this can be life-threatening
  • Recurrent pyelonephritis can lead to chronic kidney scarring and disease

Self-Test Flashcards

A client with 2 days of dysuria now has a temperature of 39°C, chills, and flank pain. What has most likely happened?
The infection has ascended to the kidney (pyelonephritis, an upper UTI). Systemic signs distinguish it from cystitis.
What does the FLANK mnemonic stand for?
Fever (usually >38.5°C), Lumbar (flank) pain, Abdominal distension/tenderness, Nausea/vomiting, Kidney (CVA) tenderness.
How is CVA tenderness assessed?
Client sits up or lies prone. Place your non-dominant hand over the costovertebral angle and strike it gently with your dominant fist.
A client jumps in pain when the nurse percusses the CVA. What should the nurse do?
Notify the provider. CVA tenderness is an unexpected finding indicating upper UTI (kidney) involvement.
Which urinalysis finding points to the kidney rather than the bladder?
WBC casts (with pyuria).
What does a 'shift to the left' on the CBC mean?
More neutrophils and bands (immature WBCs), indicating a severe infection.
Why is an ultrasound or CT ordered for pyelonephritis?
To rule out an obstruction (such as a kidney stone) or an abscess.
How long are antibiotics given for pyelonephritis compared with cystitis?
Usually 14–21 days, compared with 3–7 days for uncomplicated cystitis.
When can a hospitalized client switch from IV to oral antibiotics?
Once nausea and vomiting improve and the client tolerates oral intake.
A client with pyelonephritis becomes confused, with HR 122, RR 26, BP 88/52. What is happening?
Progression to sepsis. Notify the provider immediately.
What should the client be taught about antibiotics at discharge?
Finish the full 14–21 day course even after feeling better, and return for the follow-up urine culture.
How can repeated pyelonephritis lead to CKD?
Recurrent or severe episodes cause permanent renal scarring, which can progress to chronic kidney disease.
